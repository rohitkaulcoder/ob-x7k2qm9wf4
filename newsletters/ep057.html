Confirmation day

Friday sealed the worst week for tech in over a year. The Nasdaq had its worst day since June and its worst month in more than a year. Intel crashed 29%, the worst session in the company’s history, vaporizing more than $15B of paper holdings belonging to, of all parties, the U.S. government and Nvidia. The Journal’s sequence is the one we lived: Anthropic’s S-1 momentarily rejuvenated investors, then public earnings delivered the reality check. Notice who took the hit: not the story stocks, the infrastructure. When the selloff moves downstream from the buyers of AI to the sellers of AI, the market is saying the buildout itself is the risk. The one exception is instructive: Microsoft rose, the mega-cap whose AI story is still treated as a revenue story rather than a cost story. Everyone else got marked as a spender. The debate has moved from “how big is the AI boom” to “who pays for it” - and heading into next week’s midterms, that is a political question as much as a market one.

The biggest heist in crypto history

Binance is racing to cover losses after hackers plundered $6.3B - an order of magnitude beyond anything crypto has seen. Mt. Gox, the theft that defined the industry’s first decade, was under half a billion at the time and still bankrupted the biggest exchange on earth. This one works out to roughly $6.3B across an engineer’s compromised laptop and other breaches; Tether froze $1.57B of USDT and Circle more than $800M of USDC, leaving ~$3.3B under the hackers’ control. The freezes are the tell: stablecoins are issued by companies, and companies can freeze at the address level - the most centralized feature of supposedly decentralized money turned out to be the most useful. Attribution points to TraderTraitor, a North Korean state group, which makes this a sanctions story, not just a security story. CZ, now a shareholder, says he expects all user funds to be fully covered. Ethena disclosed $8.8M of exposure; Polymarket halted some trading after user account hacks. The Information’s morning briefing had the first numbers; the Journal’s free summary carried the overnight detail.

Black October

Security professionals have a name for this month: Black October. AI-detected vulnerabilities are emerging faster than humans can patch, and the community is, in Big Technology’s quote, “tired, driven to discouragement.” The week’s evidence: OpenAI dealt with a Hugging Face hack; eMudhra leaked eight private 384-bit keys usable for man-in-the-middle attacks on server-to-server traffic; a PHP remote-code-execution flaw lands on a stack that runs roughly 70% of websites; Railway exposed customer secrets. The deeper point is tempo: defenders patch on human schedules, attackers now find on machine schedules, and the gap between those two clocks is where the damage lives. Big Technology’s scenarios for what comes next are not rogue AI but humans with AI - a catastrophic hack, a novel pathogen, a war started on a manipulated signal - and its critique is that the industry is waiting for the catastrophe before acting. The market is already pricing the catch-up: The Information reports cybersecurity startup Cyera is discussing a $20B valuation.

DeepSeek’s state-backed warchest

The Information mapped DeepSeek’s ~$16B raise (105.5B yuan) at a ~$87B valuation - China’s largest AI funding round, and only the company’s second ever. State-owned investors now hold 27%, up from 2% in round one; Tencent and CATL remain the top two backers, joined by ChangXin Memory, SMIC, the telecom carriers, CICC, Citic and China Life, plus a who’s-who of consumer tech (Vivo, Meituan, Midea, Luxshare, Geely, Ctrip, Xiaohongshu) at 1-3B yuan each. The first round’s structure is the tell about control: every investor except the national AI fund invested through an entity Liang owns, forfeiting voting rights and locking up for five years - and did it anyway. The round doubled mid-raise for two reasons: black-market Nvidia B300 servers hit $2.85M apiece, five times normal, and the first public Chinese model stocks (MiniMax, Z.ai) have plunged since their lockups expired, so DeepSeek raised a bubble warchest while it could. The student distilling OpenAI’s model is now state-funded.

What the S-1 actually says

Jamin Ball’s Clouded Judgement does the arithmetic the hot takes skipped. Anthropic holds $134B of cash and investments; it has raised $141B in its life - so it has burned roughly $7B total to become the company that just filed for the biggest IPO ever, on a run-rate heading toward $16.8B of quarterly revenue. The $108B “convertible notes payable” is not debt in any economic sense: it is the Amazon and Google converts, marked to market at the current share price. And the $81B first-half “net loss” is the same mark-to-market - $72B of Amazon convert revaluation plus $9B of forward commitments, an accounting artifact of your stock going up between rounds. Actual Q2 GAAP operating margin: +8%. Ball’s honest bear point is the inversion of the SaaS promise: Anthropic was accidentally profitable, monetizing its compute fleet better than expected, and will now burn on purpose to stay at the frontier. The questions that matter: can it stay at the frontier, can anyone build a durable business on frontier models, and what does staying cost. The bears are worried about the wrong line items.

Clouded Judgement, Dispelling the Myths of the Anthropic S-1, Oct 30 (free, full text).

Who gets paid

The Information traced Anthropic’s expected November IPO, at around $1.5T, to the pension funds that own it through Spark Capital. Spark went in at the Series C in early 2023 (partner Yasmin Razavi) and put ~$777M across six rounds; that position could return $9B or more. The LPs reading this weekend: Orange County and LA city employees ($33M each into Growth Fund IV, more into V), Massachusetts teachers (up to $42M), South Carolina (in the round priced at $965B). The context is venture’s liquidity drought: DPI for 2021-vintage funds was five cents on the dollar at the end of last year, the lowest five-year figure this century per PitchBook. That is why the mega-IPOs have to happen, and why LPs keep re-upping with the winners - Massachusetts and South Carolina just committed to Spark’s next $3B fund. SpaceX’s June listing at $1.75T started the thaw (Harvard disclosed a $2.2B stake); Anthropic is the next tap, with lockups delaying cash for up to a year.

Paywalled for readers: The Information, An Anthropic Windfall Could Await State Pension Funds, Oct 30 (full text read).

New founders, and an India note

Andrew Chen’s a16z piece asks whether AI leverage kills the classic cofounder pairing: if one founder can hundred-x their output, who needs a team? His honest answer is maybe - and the counter is that a hundred-x’d team still beats a hundred-x’d individual, because someone has to do a million customer meetings and software does not take customers to dinner. a16z is running the experiment itself: its Speedrun program writes first checks up to $1M. Two India data points from Newcomer’s free weekly: Jio’s phone business celebrated India’s biggest-ever IPO, and Thursday’s Mettle Capital $350M raise says local Series A money is still there for the right teams. Elsewhere in the froth: Kalshi raised $1B, and Revolut’s Nik Storonsky has public choice words for the VC industry. The venture model is being stress-tested from every side at once, and it keeps holding.
