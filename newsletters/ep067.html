A one-company story

Day two of the Jensen Huang fallout, and Monday’s tape told you how the market is processing it. Nvidia fell as much as 8.8% at the lows, erasing almost $400 billion of market value intraday, and closed down 4.2% at $178.76. On the same day the S&P 500 rose 2.4% and the Nasdaq 100 climbed 3.4%, their best day since April, as investors bought last week’s dip and oil slid on Iran optimism. Nvidia fell anyway. When the index rips and the biggest company in it drops, that is not macro; that is a single name being re-priced. And look where the money rotated: AMD up 13%, Broadcom up nearly 9%. Google chose this Monday to unveil a new AI chip lineup built with AMD, including a variant for governments and regulated industries, and Anthropic shipped Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 ahead of its IPO. Everyone in AI circled the same date on the calendar.

Alice Huang and Megaspeed

The woman at the center is Alice Huang, a former executive at Megaspeed International, the Singapore-based neocloud itself under investigation for allegedly passing Nvidia chips to Chinese companies. Per the New York Times’ reporting, U.S. intelligence determined she was a Chinese spy; Big Technology’s account says the U.S. government believes she passed information to Beijing while building data centers in Asia with Nvidia’s help. Overnight, Bloomberg added that Nvidia’s general counsel intervened in the U.S. probe of suspected Megaspeed chip smuggling, and that a short seller had flagged Huang’s ties to a Megaspeed executive as “uncomfortably close” months ago. Nvidia’s board says it has no reason to believe secrets were compromised; Sinocism’s Bill Bishop asks the obvious counterfactual about who was ever alone with his phone. China denies the allegations. Trump voiced support for Huang on Monday while confirming the FBI is looking into the affair. Board, Congress and FBI are now investigating in parallel, and every quote above is an allegation or an assessment, not a finding.

Is the affair securities fraud?

Matt Levine asks the week’s best legal question, and the answer teaches you how the machine works. Rule 10b-5 makes it illegal to misstate or omit material facts in connection with securities trading; material means a reasonable investor would care; and the doctrine Levine has documented for years is that every bad thing a public company does can be characterized as securities fraud, because if hiding it was misleading and the stock drops when it surfaces, the drop is the damage. Sure enough, at 9:04 Monday morning, Schall, Brown & Schwartz announced an investigation into whether Nvidia issued false or misleading statements or failed to disclose pertinent information. The misstatement theory writes itself: two weeks ago Huang called the interactions “completely normal.” The plaintiffs’ firm does not need a crime; it needs a class, a drop, and a statement that looks bad in hindsight, and then Nvidia chooses between discovery and a settlement check. But here is why this one is genuinely material, not just mechanically actionable. The market runs a shared model of AI cash flows, call it $4 trillion a year in five years, and Nvidia is the toll booth. Bernstein’s Stacy Rasgon: “Jensen is not an ordinary CEO; he IS Nvidia,” with supposedly 60 direct reports and no number two. Any fact that shifts the probability of Nvidia keeping its China sales, its export-control lobbying credibility, or its CEO is a fact that moves the model. A one-percent probability shift is worth tens of billions. That is why Levine calls this plausibly the most expensive extramarital affair in human history, and adds: it kind of should be.

The succession question

Headhunters are already working the phones. The Information’s Briefing floats two out-of-left-field names: AMD’s Lisa Su, who runs the only credible rival in AI chips but competes with Nvidia directly and is Huang’s cousin, and Arm’s Rene Haas, who spent seven years at Nvidia. CFO Colette Kress could hold the seat as interim. The nearer-term problem is distraction: with board, congressional and FBI investigations running at once, Huang is less able to run a company that, per The Information, he touches in nearly every aspect of daily operations, at exactly the moment Google and AMD are coming for the franchise. Governance footnote of the day: Coursera adopted a poison pill after activist Pale Fire disclosed a 14.7% stake; the trigger is 15%.

Export controls, back on the table

The Information’s exclusive is the story that matters most to Nvidia’s actual revenue. Three bipartisan bills sit in the Senate version of the National Defense Authorization Act, which is expected to pass before the new Congress sits in January: the Match Act (cut China off from semiconductor manufacturing equipment, including lithography, and pressure the Dutch and Japanese to follow), the Overwatch Act (more Congressional oversight of Commerce chip licenses), and the Chip Security Act (tracking capabilities on AI chips). In late October the White House gave Congressional staff feedback on Match and Overwatch, read by people familiar as a signal it may not block them this time, and asked for a provision requiring Commerce to write a rule against remote access, the loophole where Chinese labs rent restricted chips through data centers in Singapore and Malaysia. Commerce has dragged its feet for two years; Howard Lutnick has told people he will not move without Trump’s instruction; Nvidia has lobbied especially hard against remote-access rules, arguing controls have backfired by pushing China to build its own chips. The clock is the story: the defense bill passes before the Democratic-led Congress takes power in January, so Republicans are motivated to move while they control the text. One lobbyist: “it’s just an easier argument to make today than it was last week.” The policy winner is Dario Amodei, who has argued for controls for years and now faces almost no resistance.

Fireworks’ second breach

Fireworks, the open-model inference startup, disclosed a second and more serious hack: customer billing information, model weights and datasets were exposed. Large customers including Harvey were told in the past week; Harvey says no Harvey customer data was impacted; Notion, Vercel and Cursor are also customers, and some seven-figure accounts are considering switching. Fireworks is working with Palo Alto Networks’ Unit 42 and ties the attack to a cybercriminal group doing “LLM-jacking” - stealing AI access tokens to resell. The sequence is the nightmare version for a trust business: an October incident, in which an unauthorized party used internal Fireworks credentials to reach model-evaluation data, was disclosed at the time with no evidence of customer-model access; now the bigger breach lands days before a fundraise that was expected to value the company at $30 billion or more, up from $17.5 billion in July, with $1 billion of annualized revenue and brand-new CFO Dave Wehner. Zoom out and it is the same asymmetry everywhere: models find flaws faster than companies patch them, CyberCartel is using Anthropic, SpaceX and Moonshot models against 400-plus Latin American financial targets, and even October’s PHP vulnerability was machine-found. Offense is winning.

Meta’s enterprise wedge is WhatsApp

Meta is building a sales organization for small and midsize businesses and partnering with Accenture, Deloitte and IBM to sell and deploy AI products to big companies, per The Information. The backdrop is $145 billion of planned capex this year and the September launch of the Meta Enterprise Platform under former MongoDB CEO Chirantan “CJ” Desai. The graveyard is relevant: Workplace peaked at 7 million paid subscribers in 2021 and was discontinued in 2024, and last year’s attempt to put Meta business agents on retailers’ own websites died in December after Amazon and Booking.com refused to hand over customer access and brands demanded control over tone, testing and data. The twist is where the same agents work: Meta’s own rails. Movida, a Brazilian car-rental company, runs one on WhatsApp, and Flipkart has signed up. Meta’s enterprise future may not look like selling software to CIOs at all; it looks like renting out the messaging layer it already owns.

Three Indian Series As in one week

Postround’s weekly tracker (Series A deals above $8 million, worldwide ex-China) counts nineteen deals for the week of November 1, and three are Indian: Two Point O Capital, a Mumbai clean-energy and climate-finance platform, raised $10 million from Transition VC; PowerUp Money, a Bengaluru consumer and SMB fintech, raised $10 million from Flourish Ventures; and altM, a Bengaluru biomaterials startup, raised $10.3 million from Mela Ventures and Rainmatter. Two of the three leads are domestic funds. The global pattern is AI infrastructure for specific bottlenecks: Humble Robotics raised $51 million from Obvious Ventures for cabless autonomous electric freight on dock-to-dock routes; Firmly raised $15 million led by Squarepoint, with J.P. Morgan Payments, Mastercard and Samsung NEXT, building merchant-side rails for agentic commerce that keep the retailer as merchant of record; and Larridin raised $30 million from Pelion for measuring AI spend against output, reporting median year-one savings of $2.4 million. When the AI bill becomes a board-level expense line, the meter becomes a business.
