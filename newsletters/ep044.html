The risk is not the crash, it is the twenty years

The AI trade closed its worst week since June. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell for eight straight sessions, the longest streak since 2008. CoreWeave lost a quarter of its value in five days and its bonds trade at distressed levels. Jensen Huang's answer, from Berlin: all of this is going to pass. The desk worry is narrower: not that the technology fails, but that the spending is unsustainable at these borrowing costs.

Into that week Michael Burry published a history lesson, not a crash call. Deflate the S&P 500 by CPI and three long bears appear: 1929-49, real prices down 58%; 1966-82, down about 53%; 2000-11, down about 40%. With dividends reinvested the losses soften to about 10%, 25%, and a real gain by 1949 respectively. His summary: it has always been a very long wait, not a wipeout.

The quiet caveat is that the textbook recovery assumes twenty years of disciplined reinvestment by holders who never needed the money. Real holders retire into the wrong decade. And the venture corollary is underappreciated: modern venture was born inside the second of those bears, when institutions abandoned public equities and entry prices collapsed. If another long winter comes, the worst place to be is the public index and the historically best place is early entry. The eras that punish everyone are the ones that stop paying for stories and start paying for yield.

Beijing builds a permission layer

China proposed export controls on 40 strategic technologies, 23 tied to AI: chips, large language models, solar, biotech. Registration is effective immediately, even for shipments already moving, while public comment runs to November 15. This is not an export ban. It is a licensing lever, a catalogue with a state veto over categories rather than a list of banned firms. A lever never pulled still shapes every negotiation.

The timing is the message: one month after the Xi-White House truce cut tariffs on $30 billion of goods and opened an AI dialogue. Beijing is regulating from strength; Chinese exports rose 36% year on year in September, driven by AI products. The sleeper clause reaches cross-border IP transfers and ownership changes, putting two years of biopharma dealmaking in scope. For India, the read-through is uncomfortable: its AI stack leans on cheap, good Chinese open models and hardware, and every new permission layer is a tax it did not budget for.

The spy in the lost and found

The FBI arrested a former OpenAI engineer, Abdulrahman Alfozan, for allegedly planting a toilet-paper-wrapped recording device in a conference room. The DOJ pointedly notes he is a Saudi national; motive is unproven. The cinematic detail: he allegedly mislaid a second bug in a bag and retrieved it from the company's lost and found.

The schadenfreude is misplaced. Security consultants told The Information the same thing: frantic hiring means minimal diligence, a usable bug costs under $100 disguised as a phone charger or smoke alarm, and if you find one there are two. Meanwhile Cursor staff were investigated over whether confidential data reached SpaceX for AI training. The market's answer is not fewer secrets but more surveillance: Strider Technologies has raised $110 million to flag insider espionage, mostly, it says, coerced or approached engineers rather than trained spooks. The talent war created security debt. Espionage risk now has its own ARR.

The Information, The Weekend, Oct 17 (full newsletter text read).

The foundation that says nothing

The Economist scoured the Musk Foundation's tax returns. America's fifth-largest private foundation discloses almost nothing, gives relatively little to outside work, and much of what it gives lands near its founder's own interests. Verdict: opaque, miserly, self-serving. A foundation that size is a governance artifact, not a rounding error. Earlier industrial fortunes at least produced libraries and eradication campaigns.

The Economist, Oct 17 (newsletter summary; article itself not read in full).
