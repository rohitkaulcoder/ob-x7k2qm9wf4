The price war comes for the IPO

Weeks before the largest IPO ever, Anthropic’s best asset - its margin - has become its biggest liability, because it gives OpenAI something to aim at. The Information’s reporting is a tour of the battlefield. At Rippling, Anthropic’s share of coding work flipped from 57% to 18% in five months; OpenAI went from 16% to 52%. Replit moved most of its spend for a discount above 30% on a $100 million-plus annual contract - and Anthropic lost the account partly because its own policy ends discounts once a customer hits contracted usage limits, the confidence move of a company that thinks its models are irreplaceable. DataCamp was offered 40% off, conditioned on adopting Codex over Claude Code. A bank doubled its ChatGPT seats at a flat fee. Khosla and a16z are arming their own portfolios with OpenAI credits and engineer hotlines. The economics are pointed: Anthropic spent $5.6 billion on compute in Q2 against $11.5 billion in revenue - a real gross margin - while OpenAI projects spending more on compute than it earns. OpenAI is buying share with money it does not have, out of the $30 billion round that priced at $1.25 trillion the same week. Anthropic’s growth slowed to a still-absurd 45% between Q2 and Q3, and the S-1 will have to defend the direction of that deceleration. The counterweight is that switching is nearly free - Replit swapped providers in hours - and the model still wins: after Claude Opus 5.5 shipped, Anthropic retook the OpenRouter lead at twice OpenAI’s usage. The moat is the model. The question is whether you defend a moat with margin or with velocity.

Bloomberg Economics adds the frame the roadshow will hate: at $2 trillion, Anthropic’s own warnings about mass unemployment become the pitch - if AI is coming for your job, own the thing that is coming. Doom as the hedge. (Covered from Bloomberg’s weekend newsletter summary; the full article was unreachable behind a bot wall this morning.)

The carry trade in a hard hat

CoreWeave has halved in twelve days, and the reason is not any contract being canceled - nothing about the company changed. What changed is the price of the machine it is built on. A neocloud is a carry trade: sign a compute contract (Meta’s is $35 billion), borrow against it, spend the debt on GPUs that depreciate from delivery day, and live on the spread between what the fleet earns and what the debt costs. Equity holders sit on the sliver between two big numbers, so small moves in either one erase them. September’s inflation jump raised the odds of a hike - and every refinancing, always soon for CoreWeave, gets more expensive. At the same time the asset side got its first deep-pocketed competitor that needs no debt at all: SpaceX is selling excess compute from cash. And the demand side’s best customer, Meta, is building its own data centers and chips. Jensen Huang says neocloud demand is “off the charts” - he would know, and he would also say so; he sells the chips. The honest signal is in the sympathy trade: Nebius and IREN fell 7% and 8% alongside on Thursday. If the cohort keeps falling together, the market is repricing the model, not the company. This is the third costume for the same lesson in four days - private credit marks, venture funds borrowing against illiquid marks, a neocloud borrowing against depreciating GPUs. Every business model in this boom is a bet that capital stays cheap and the asset stays scarce. Blackstone owns the scarcity. CoreWeave rents it.

Populism insurance at venture scale

The ultrawealthy have discovered giving, at a price point that tells you what they think the problem costs. The Journal reports the rally around Trump Accounts - investment accounts seeded for children - with Musk pledging SpaceX shares to millions of kids, Michael Dell aboard, and Brad Gerstner, who sold the concept to the White House, calling it a new social contract. The timing is not subtle: communities are fighting data centers and polls show majorities wanting billionaires weaker. Bloomberg Opinion’s Gongloff is blunt about Musk’s version - one share for each of 72 million children, “the first of many”: a promise to give shares whose value rests on promises, from a man projecting SpaceX revenue from $19 billion to $1 trillion by 2030. There is a real policy buried in Gerstner’s idea - broad equity ownership reconciled Americans to capitalism once before. But as an answer to a national argument about who the AI boom is for, two hundred dollars a child is not a social contract. It is a finder fee.

Burry’s rotten breadth

Michael Burry’s new framework, from the free portion of his Substack (we are not subscribers; the payoff table is paywalled): at the S&P 500’s October 6 record high, sort the index into Leaders within 10% of their highs, the Broken down 20-40%, and the Busted down more than 40%. The second-order fact needs no table: at the market’s all-time high, a meaningful slice of the index had already halved-ish. Breadth was rotten at the top - exactly the signature of spring 2000, when the median stock peaked long before the index did. Burry’s question is whether the Busted become the haven through the bear, as they did then. The man who shorted the housing bubble hiding from the AI bubble in the stocks it already destroyed is, at minimum, a trade with a sense of humor. The India read-across: a market held up by a handful of names while the median small cap bleeds is the same shape in a different timezone.
