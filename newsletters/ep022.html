The price of running AI

AI valuations have moved down the stack. It is no longer enough to train an impressive model. Somebody must serve each request, supply the memory, build the room and finance the power. Each layer claims to be the scarce one. They cannot all collect the same premium indefinitely.

Fal has discussed a round above $15 billion, possibly $17 billion to $20 billion. Its annualized revenue pace has reportedly doubled since March to $800 million. Fireworks completed an employee share sale at a $17.5 billion valuation and is considering a new round that could target $30 billion. Its annualized revenue was $1 billion in July. The fundraising discussions are not completed rounds, and an annualized pace is not trailing audited sales.

These companies sell the operational work of inference: loading models, batching requests and keeping expensive chips busy without making customers wait. Open models give developers choice, but running them well takes engineering. The Information says gross margins have historically been around 50%, against more than 70% for the best software companies. At $1 billion in sales, that is $500 million rather than $700 million left after direct serving costs. A software multiple may be the wrong instrument.

The squeeze comes from both ends. Frontier labs can lower API prices; cloud suppliers can raise compute costs. The durable business is not merely an API with a catalogue of models. It must show better cost per completed task, reliably, across hardware and changing models. For an Indian founder buying inference, that metric matters more than a cheap token quote.

Move the warehouse onto the roof

DensityAI, founded by former Tesla Dojo leaders, is in advanced talks over a financing at a reported $10 billion valuation. Its leaders say AWS would buy eventual chips if they meet performance requirements. This is a conditional claim about a chip that may still be years from mass production.

The engineering pitch is specific. AI processors do arithmetic quickly but wait for model weights to arrive from memory. High-bandwidth memory usually sits beside the compute die. DensityAI has told investors it wants to stack DRAM directly above the compute area, shortening the electrical path. Think of moving a warehouse from beside a factory onto its roof.

The roof is hot. DRAM is heat-sensitive. Stacking fragile layers can warp them, reduce yield or force the processor to slow down. If a fabrication run produces 90 working chips out of 100, each good unit bears roughly 1.11 units of fabrication cost. At 50 working chips, each bears two. A power saving in operation can vanish in manufacturing. The diligence questions are measured bandwidth, sustained power per useful token, heat, yield and the actual AWS purchasing condition. A ten-billion-dollar valuation is not a benchmark.

Twenty-four gigawatts, but of what?

SemiAnalysis mapped more than 1,000 Chinese data centres across over 60 players and estimates more than 24GW of delivered capacity. Its year-end 2026 comparison puts the US at 56GW, Asia-Pacific excluding China at roughly 15GW and Europe, Middle East and Africa at roughly 14GW. Those are model estimates, not a public census.

A gigawatt measures electrical capacity, not productive AI output. A vacant legacy hall and a fully fitted AI facility may both count. China has had high vacancy in its retail-first data-centre market even while demand for suitable AI capacity rises. Empty floor space does not supply a missing accelerator, network or tenant.

SemiAnalysis estimates ByteDance occupies roughly one fifth of delivered Chinese capacity and rents nearly all of it. Alibaba, Tencent and Baidu together spent $20 billion on capital expenditure in the second quarter, more than twice the year-earlier figure, with all three posting negative free cash flow. The two US-listed landlords show only part of the market; state-owned carriers still own around a third. Fast construction lowers a bottleneck and can create another one: competing halls with poor pricing power. Count installed chips, contracted tenants and utilization, not just megawatts.

The hand is the bottleneck

Tesla reportedly lifted Optimus production from a few dozen robots a week in the second quarter to several hundred a week last month. Managers want a line capable of more than a thousand a week by year-end. That is a target, not customer delivery. The eventual plan is around 20,000 a week.

The Information reports more than 100 tiny parts in the hand and forearm that still require manual assembly. Fixtures struggle to align parts at line speed, and touch sensors have had reliability problems. Tesla is developing a replaceable sensor glove. Current robots are mainly used internally in controlled tasks; the commercial version needs greater durability. Leasing initial units would let Tesla upgrade them and gather operating data, but keeps repair and residual-value risk on Tesla's books.

A humanoid demo proves movement. A business needs paid hours between interventions. In an Indian factory, where labour costs differ sharply from California's, that distinction is decisive.

Investors want to choose the asset

Bloomberg reports rising demand for deal-by-deal special-purpose vehicles rather than blind-pool funds. A survey of 410 private-market participants found 87% reporting greater demand for bespoke SPVs. Reported demand is not capital committed.

An SPV lets investors see the company, price and terms before wiring. Twenty investors putting in $100,000 each can assemble a $2 million deal without waiting for a whole fund to close. The price of choice is concentration, due diligence and a separate vehicle's legal and reporting costs. Slower exits make LPs reluctant to hand out blank cheques. They do not make single-asset risk disappear. A good manager still needs access and judgment; a wrapper around a hot deal is not alpha.

Source: Bloomberg Going Private, Sep 25, 2026

The front door changes hands

Clouded Judgement argues that personal agents could become the customer's interaction layer, turning travel sites and merchants into suppliers behind a new front door. It cites an Expedia partnership and Amazon's resistance. That is a plausible theory, not a law. Agents still need access, payments, support and enough trust to complete a transaction. Incumbents still own inventory, fulfilment and customer relationships.

Across today's stories the same question repeats: which constraint is measurable and who owns it? An inference API, a memory stack, a data centre, a robot line and an SPV can each solve a genuine problem. Their premium lasts only while that problem remains scarce.
