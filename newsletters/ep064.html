The week that was

Friday happened twice. First Asia opened and face-planted into the hole Thursday’s American session had dug: the Nikkei fell 6%, South Korea’s Kospi dropped as much as 8.8% intraday and tripped a circuit breaker, Samsung and SK Hynix each lost more than 6%, and Taiwan’s index closed in a bear market. Korea’s benchmark has now surrendered $1.7T in this reversal; Bloomberg counted 22 regional billionaires down a combined $21B in a day, and funds woke to margin calls from Taipei to Hong Kong. The asymmetry is structural: Asian tech indices are, in effect, leveraged positions on the AI supply chain, and when that trade gaps against you overnight there is nothing else in the index to catch you. Then America opened and calmly went the other way - the Nasdaq bounced 1.1% on Friday, with CoreWeave, Intel, Nvidia, Micron and Arm all climbing, and the October jobs report landed hot: 165,000 added against 59,000 expected, unemployment down to 4.1%. The real economy is not trading like the Nasdaq. The more interesting counter-evidence came from the cash registers rather than the tickers: Stripe, which processes payments for 72,000 software businesses, published a SaaS index from actual transactions showing revenue growth above 30% year over year and accelerating, fastest at the youngest companies. While the stock market was pricing the SaaSpocalypse, the till was pricing acceleration. Private credit agrees: eight months after UBS sketched a worst case of 15% default rates, Bloomberg finds borrowers holding up, with Blackstone’s European credit head arguing you can believe AI is profoundly impactful without believing it kills every software company at once. The one marker still pointing down is the Anthropic IPO whisper number, which Bloomberg Opinion has sliding from $2T to the $1.2T range ahead of a likely late-November listing; Newcomer hears skepticism even at $1.5T. Both things are true at once - the trade is crowded and fragile, and the demand underneath it is real - and holding both at once is what a repricing feels like.

TSMC weighs memory

The Information’s Friday exclusive is the story that could matter most in five years: TSMC is drawing up plans to manufacture 3D DRAM, and it is doing so at the request of Nvidia, its largest customer. The first-principles version: processors think, memory holds what they are thinking about, and modern AI models are so large that feeding the processor has become the binding constraint - memory prices have gone vertical, badly enough that Apple raised device prices this year citing memory costs. Conventional DRAM lays its cells in a single flat layer; 3D DRAM stacks them vertically, a parking garage instead of a parking lot, trading brutal manufacturing complexity for density. That market belongs to three specialists - Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron - all racing on 3D DRAM themselves. A TSMC entry breaks the cartel’s shape. The geometry is loaded in both directions. Nvidia’s motive is obvious: its roadmap is hostage to HBM supply it does not control, and a second source built by its most trusted manufacturer is attractive. TSMC’s hesitation is equally rational: memory is the most cyclical business in semiconductors - shortage, spike, overbuild, crash - and TI’s reporting says that history could still kill the venture. TSMC made standard memory from the 1990s until 2008, so this is a return, not a debut; the effort is led by advanced-packaging VP Hsu Kuo-chin, whose biography includes running Micron’s Taiwan operations; and there is a genuine conflict to manage, since TSMC has partnered with SK Hynix since 2024 to build the base chip under SK Hynix’s next-generation HBM. The precedent for the fast path is Micron’s March purchase of a Powerchip fab for $1.8B to convert for advanced memory. TSMC’s official line was a refusal to comment on market rumors and a restatement of its dedicated-foundry mission; Nvidia said nothing. If TSMC commits, the AI supply-chain map gets redrawn around one company that would then touch logic, packaging and memory.

Washington reorders

The post-election reshuffle took days, not weeks. Stephen Miller becomes White House chief of staff on Monday, replacing Susie Wiles, who moves to the president’s political operation; the Miami US attorney who ran investigations into Trump’s foes has resigned, with more Justice Department departures expected. Read it as a White House hunkering down for two years of hostile oversight. The first concrete exhibit arrived the same day: the chairs of the House China Committee (Moolenaar), Foreign Affairs (Mast) and Intelligence (Crawford) asked Jensen Huang to attend a closed-door briefing on November 18 over the New York Times’ October reporting that Huang had a “close relationship” with Alice Huang, an executive at Singapore-based data-center company Megaspeed, whom US officials reportedly determined was funneling information to Chinese intelligence. “It is vital that Congress determine the extent to which U.S. national security has been placed at risk,” the chairs wrote. Stratechery’s Sharp China had the clean one-liner: whatever else the election means, life is about to get a lot more complicated for Jensen Huang in Washington. Meanwhile, Noah Smith’s cold-shower essay on the new majority is the week’s best corrective: this was the biggest midterm wave in half a century - 1974 and 1976 are the only comps, and both preceded Republican decades - and it was won by moderates, with the Blue Dog Coalition adding at least 14 members and California’s billionaire tax failing. His borrowed line for the winners: you know how to win a victory, Hannibal; you do not know how to use it. Guardrail status, applied as promised: Alaska’s governor race remains uncalled by the AP (campaigns projecting their own wins is not a call), and Proposition 40 - No leading roughly 56-44, sponsor conceded - still has no formal call either.

Texas and the four hundred million

The data-center backlash now has a balance sheet. Newcomer’s tally: Elon Musk spent more than $400M on the midterms, roughly half of it in Texas, backing Republicans who lost. Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa made data-center opposition - and Musk personally - a centerpiece of her campaign, and incoming comptroller Sarah Eckhardt now reviews the subsidy program that approved Musk’s Terafab, having campaigned as a watchdog on exactly those subsidies. Of 41 data-center measures on state and local ballots, voters sided against data centers in 40. Effingham County, Georgia postponed a data-center vote as results rolled in; satellite images show a Microsoft/IREN project missing generators and cooling; a Microsoft/Nebius project in New Jersey is stuck in permitting; GlobalData puts the average project delay at 33% and counting. Tesla and SpaceX fell 11% and 9% on Thursday before Friday’s bounce. Friday’s coda: Musk announced Tesla is scrapping the Cybertruck - once promised as Tesla’s “best product ever” - winding production down next year to make room for the Cybercab in Texas. Four hundred million dollars of political spending bought the most hostile regulatory environment of Musk’s career in his own home state. The market spent the back half of the week pricing that sentence.

Private markets did not get the memo

While the tape panicked, the check-writers kept writing. Fal raised $1.65B across two rounds (Sequoia led the E, Bessemer the F, with Nvidia participating); Zipline raised $1.5B led by Paradigm; Northwood Space $375M co-led by a16z and DFJ Growth; Applied Compute $350M led by Elad Gil; and Valor Equity Partners has raised nearly $3.3B for a new fund. Either all of them are wrong, or the selloff is about positioning rather than demand. Two corporate stories sharpen the point. Unity Software, one of the names left for dead in the SaaSpocalypse, has drawn a takeover approach from a consortium including Silver Lake and Saudi Arabia’s PIF - on the order of $26B against a $20B market cap - days after reporting 32% revenue growth with its AI ad business set to double to as much as $520M next quarter; the stock is up nearly 200% since March. And Anthropic, ahead of the IPO, launched personal finance for Claude: bank accounts connect through Plaid with read-only access, no training on the conversations, explicitly not investment advice - matching OpenAI’s May move in ChatGPT.

The agent cloud, and the Apple contrast

Two frameworks worth keeping. Jamin Ball’s “agent cloud” map: the old cloud let one application serve millions of humans - virtual servers, file storage, managed database, load balancer. The agent cloud must let millions of agents each run their own little application, each on an isolated machine, because the code was written by a model seconds ago and cannot be trusted on a shared box. The new stack: sandboxes instead of servers, persistent state instead of file storage, inference clouds instead of raw compute, model routers instead of load balancers, and reinforcement-learning loops on top. His punchline: same primitives, far more units - so agent infrastructure ends up dwarfing classic cloud. The land grab has started: Dropbox bought state-storage startup Tigris this week; OpenAI bought Pierre Computer Company. Second, a16z’s charts quantify the Apple contrast: hyperscaler capex is up 6-9x since 2023 while Apple’s is below its pre-spree level; Apple is the only Magnificent Seven stock beating the Nasdaq this year, with iPhone growth rebounded and services compounding at 13%. The market is, for now, paying a premium for sitting the arms race out. And one demand-side data point for the week’s ledger: Roblox says over 80% of its top 100 games were built using AI tools, with daily asset uploads tripling month over month. Somebody is using all this compute.
