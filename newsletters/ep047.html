The supplier is now the co-signer

Nvidia is discussing guaranteeing some of the loans OpenAI plans to raise for its own data centers. Not selling chips, not buying equity: co-signing the debt. The structure matters more than the headline. A guarantee is a contingent liability, free on today’s income statement, that hands an unprofitable customer investment-grade borrowing costs in exchange for a locked-in, multi-year order book. Add the lease-not-buy chip model, where Nvidia keeps the GPUs on its own balance sheet, and OpenAI becomes simultaneously Nvidia’s customer, investment, and guaranteed borrower. That is not diversification; it is concentration wearing a trench coat.

The rhyme is vendor financing. Lucent and Nortel lent carriers the money to buy their own equipment, and when the carriers broke, the guarantees came home. The escalation ladder this quarter has been steady: equity stakes, then neocloud backstops and rent-backs, now the debt of the largest compute buyer on earth. Each step moves demand risk from the customer’s balance sheet to the supplier’s. The defense is coherent, hardware-backed, supply-constrained, cheap insurance on a $100 billion program. It was also the carriers’ defense in 1999. The origin detail deserves a footnote of its own: the whole rapprochement began when Jensen Huang called Sam Altman after The Information’s June TPU scoop. One phone call, and three months later the chipmaker is discussing the lab’s mortgage.

Lenskart prices the monopoly case

Lenskart is listing near $9 billion, up from a $6 billion private mark: roughly 10-12x revenue and 70-80x EBITDA. The nospeedlimit synthesis of the broker notes shows why the ask is directionally serious. FY25 revenue of ₹6,700 crore, 40% of it international; ₹1,000 crore EBITDA; 2,700 stores; and still only about 5% of an Indian market growing 13% a year while Lenskart compounds at 33%. The mechanics are unusually clean: vertical integration in the Luxottica mold, a franchise shift that cut receivable days from 48 to 9, and a 28-day working-capital cycle that turns the same capital twelve times a year. International stores are a quarter of the count and half the EBITDA. The bear case is the multiple; the bull case is that monopoly retailers at 5% share do not stay at 5%. Coming two days after Affirma’s finished 12x TBO exit, the Indian liquidity window is having its best week of the year.

Apple bought the whole sport

Apple TV takes US Formula One rights for five years from 2026, at a reported $150-170 million a year, nearly double ESPN’s $90 million, and Thompson’s read is the right one: this is not about production, F1 owns the world feed, it is about distribution as differentiation. F1 lands in the base Apple TV tier, not an add-on, and F1 TV Premium survives inside it. What Apple sells is surface area: Maps, Music, Sports, Fitness, a $630 million movie, and the home screen. Basic cable is now the niche product; F1’s young, increasingly female audience is streaming-native; and the ESPN comparison, a simulcast of the Sky feed with someone else’s promos, is what minimum-effort rights ownership looks like. Owning a sport end to end is a bet that the iPhone’s notification tray is worth more than dial position ever was.

The skeptic with no round to raise

Andrej Karpathy told Dwarkesh Patel that reinforcement learning is “terrible,” supervision sucked through a straw; that coding agents drag you back to conventional patterns; that LLMs do not generalize; and that Amodei’s 90%-of-code claim has plainly not happened. The zebra point is the deep one: animals are born with working competence and no training data; nothing in the current paradigm learns like that. The incentive analysis is why the interview traveled: Karpathy is not raising money, and the people with aggressive timelines are. The same week, OpenAI had to walk back claims that GPT-5 solved novel math problems it had merely memorized. None of this says the buildout is fake. It says the ceiling is real, the buildout is priced for no ceiling, and the gap between those two facts is what the next year of earnings calls is about.
