The bill arrives

Wednesday was the victory lap - the Dow’s best day in eighteen months. Thursday was the invoice. The Nasdaq 100 fell 6.4%, erasing $2.3T; the S&P 500 dropped 4.4%, its worst session since April 2025; the VIX hit its highest level since that same shock, and Bloomberg reports Goldman’s trading desk phones were, in their word, exploding. Two repricings landed at once. The first is political: the trade of the last two years - short California, long Texas, Y’all Street, reincorporations and all - blew up in a night, and no one is longer Texas than Musk. Tesla fell 11% on Thursday after Wednesday’s drop; SpaceX’s marks fell over 15% across the two sessions; the Bloomberg Billionaires Index had Musk down roughly $74B by midday. The second repricing is more interesting because it is not about politics at all: the compute narrative flipped. Three months ago the story was desperate undersupply. This week, serious investors are openly discussing a glut - Microsoft is reconsidering its neocloud commitments over delays and cost, and The Information notes Microsoft spent about $5B less on capex last quarter than projected, partly because third-party capacity simply wasn’t ready. Joe Weisenthal’s kicker: if America turns hostile to data centers, that is nominally bullish for SpaceX (Musk’s answer on X: data centers in space are looking appealing) - but the Malaysian ringgit was the world’s second-best major currency on Thursday. The data centers may just go to Malaysia.

Called, conceded, and still counting

The promised discipline, applied. Alaska’s governor race remains uncalled: AP says counting continues and the race could go to a ranked-choice tabulation; Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins leads. California’s Proposition 40, the one-time 5% billionaire wealth tax, remains formally uncalled too - but the No side now leads about 56-44 and SEIU-UHW, the union that sponsored it, all but conceded on election night, promising a 2028 rematch. Two companion poison-pill measures, Props 41 and 42, are passing comfortably. Treat “conceded” and “called” as different verbs; the count continues. The one genuinely new fact with consequences: Scott Wiener is headed to Congress from Nancy Pelosi’s old seat, his opponent having conceded Wednesday, per The Information. Wiener authored SB 53, the only meaningful frontier-AI safety law in America, and campaigned on a federal version - plus a federal AI regulator and unemployment insurance for AI displacement. He arrives battle-tested: SB 1047 drew fire from a16z, OpenAI, Google and Meta before Newsom vetoed it; the compromise SB 53 won Anthropic’s endorsement. The industry wants one federal law to preempt the state patchwork SB 53 inspired in New York and Illinois. The man who wrote the template just got a seat at the table where preemption gets negotiated.

The industry hears footsteps

Washington’s new alignment showed up in corporate behavior within 48 hours. Meta’s president said Wednesday that every hyperscaler is asking whether it needs to build outside the United States. On Thursday, OpenAI published data-center commitments that read like pre-emption: no NDAs in community deals, and a promise that residents won’t pay for the electricity its projects consume - with Chris Lehane saying the ballot box sent a message that must be answered. Elizabeth Warren is expected to take the Senate Banking gavel, and her months of unanswered hearing requests to Jensen Huang can now be enforced with subpoenas. Sam Liccardo, whose district covers the heart of Silicon Valley, says everyone must face the music of the new alignment - while urging his party to pass laws rather than stage outrage. Note what did not happen: no one in the industry is arguing against regulation in principle anymore. They are negotiating terms. That is what losing looks like when you can afford it.

Tokenomics

The most important document of the week is Anthropic’s amended S-1, and the Asimov Addendum’s analysis of what it omits. What it shows: revenue of $33.2B in the first nine months of 2026, $17B in Q3 alone; a first-ever operating profit in Q2 ($974M) swinging to a $567M operating loss in Q3, driven by roughly $5B of spot compute bought far above contract prices. What it omits: the unit economics. Anthropic sells intelligence by the token - 79% of revenue is usage-based - yet reports no token volumes, no revenue per token, no cost per token. The proposed fix is borrowed from oil and gas, where the SEC requires every producer to disclose volumes, average price and average cost per barrel in standardized units: treat the token like the barrel, and add a load-factor equivalent - contracted compute capacity, delivered capacity, utilization. The headline commitment figure is roughly $895B of compute leases and purchase commitments; the filing itself says about $214B of that is cancellable or conditional, excludes another $150-175B of no-fixed-price estimates, and the balance sheet shows just $4.5B of lease liabilities, because a lease only exists once the equipment is ready. Like an airline that has ordered three hundred planes: payments start on delivery, full seats or not. Meanwhile 44% of revenue flows through the Amazon, Google and Microsoft cloud marketplaces, which are simultaneously Anthropic’s largest investors and compute suppliers ($121B, $110B, $45B committed respectively). Post-IPO, the founders keep 50.1% of votes while the Long-Term Benefit Trust picks four of seven directors - so shareholders will never vote their way to better numbers. The S-1 sets the template every later AI IPO will copy. What is missing now will be missing for a decade.

The Ironwood benchmark

SemiAnalysis has published the first serious third-party results for Google’s TPU v7 - Ironwood - on AgentX, the benchmark built to mimic how AI is actually used now: multi-turn agent sessions, accumulating context, heavy prefix reuse, sub-agent bursts. That pattern is memory-bound, not math-bound, so the scoreboard is tokens per dollar at a given speed. The results are a genuine event. On Qwen 3.5 (397B), TPU v7 at FP8 precision delivered 1.67x better performance per dollar than Nvidia’s GB300 at FP4 - and lower precision moves half the bytes per weight, so it usually wins on cost when quality holds; beating FP4 with FP8 is doing it the hard way. On GLM-5.2, Ironwood was the cheapest FP8 option anywhere - 1.2 to 2.7x the tokens per dollar of the B200, B300 and MI355X. On Meta’s Muse Spark, a model whose attention shape literally wastes a quarter of the TPU’s systolic array, it was still cost-competitive. The engineering: megakernels (fuse the whole decode step into one long-running program, prefetching the next layer’s weights while the current one computes - AMD’s analogous MonoKernel cut 2,459 launches to 210 on its own chips) and disaggregated serving via the llm-d router, which splits compute-hungry prefill from bandwidth-hungry decode and routes each turn back to the host holding its cache, lifting hit rates from 56% to 76%. Nvidia’s response is the tell: per SemiAnalysis, a strike force of engineers and agent swarms worked 997 - nine to nine, seven days a week - and its first FP4 counter-submission still trailed TPU v7’s FP8 by 1.64x on cost. Caveats, honestly stated: Nvidia’s FP4 still wins some matchups, Rubin beats v7 outright but belongs against TPU v8 due early next year, and none of this touches training. But the moat was always software and ecosystem, and Google is giving open-source maintainers chips while Nvidia’s contributions sit in legal review for months. For a decade the question was when anyone would make Nvidia compete on price for inference. The answer appears to be: now.

The retrofit network

Nvidia’s answer to the moratorium wave is to stop asking for permission. The Information reports Nvidia has approached Apollo about building an “inference network” of small data centers across as many as 20 states, retrofitting the buildings of Brightspeed - the struggling, Apollo-owned telecom formed from $7.5B of Lumen’s old local phone business. Brightspeed’s 130-plus switching buildings are already wired with roughly a gigawatt of usable power, enough for tens of billions of dollars of Vera Rubin systems, possibly alongside Groq chips, with first retrofits targeted for early 2027. The genius is jurisdictional: retrofits of existing powered buildings need fewer local approvals than new construction, which is exactly the scarce resource after Tuesday. Nvidia submitted a written proposal to Brightspeed on Wednesday. The demand side is desperate enough to be creative: Datum’s CEO says Anthropic has been calling around for five-to-ten-megawatt pockets in old buildings. The assets everyone mocked as stranded copper-era real estate turn out to be pre-permitted electricity with a roof on it. Watch the eighties’ telco buildings trade like oil leases.

Cyber keeps printing

The market sold everything Thursday except a reason to slow down on security. Cyera is raising about $800M led by Cyberstarts at a $20B valuation - up from $12B in June, and from the $15M at which Cyberstarts wrote the $4.2M seed check in 2021, a detail worth pausing on given the same fund seeded Wiz before Google’s $34B acquisition. ARR is in the hundreds of millions. The round lands in the middle of an agent-identity M&A wave: Palo Alto Networks is in talks to pay around $500M for Clutch Security, which tracks the credentials AI agents use, following similar buys by Workday, Okta and CrowdStrike. Since Anthropic’s Mythos model, the security budget line has stopped being discretionary - more than half a dozen security executives told The Information they plan record spend. The mechanism is simple: the same models that double a lab’s own development pace (METR’s finding inside Anthropic) are available to the other side. Offense and defense both compound; only one of them bills you.

Quick hits

Brian Chesky’s side quest is now disclosed: Airbnb sold IP to his secretive AI lab for $7.1M in cash, the CTO holds equity in it too, and the company insists nothing changes - Martin Peers’ response, that a part-time CEO is an admission about the day job, is the correct one. Apple raised $7.5B in its first bond sale in over a year - debt-funded AI, like Microsoft, Oracle and Meta before it - while cutting some internal AI budgets from $300 to $200 per engineer, heading to $100. Sequoia is closing its London office to refocus on Silicon Valley. China blocked SoftBank’s $5.4B purchase of ABB’s robotics unit. AMD bought inference-chip designer Taalas for about $2.9B. Isomorphic Labs is in talks to raise $5B at roughly $50B. Uber plans to more than double its self-driving fleet with outside capital. Amazon dropped Grubhub for Uber Eats on the same day Grubhub’s parent Wonder filed to go public. Ramp’s founders told the Upstarts podcast that customer savings improved from 2% of spend in 2020 to 7.5% now, and that they were the first alpha customer of both Claude Code and Devin. And a claims investor persuaded Kalshi to open a market on an NCAA settlement payout - prediction markets as balance-sheet hedges, which is a new sentence in finance.
