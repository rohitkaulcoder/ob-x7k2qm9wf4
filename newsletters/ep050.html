The flood splits into two rivers

Three funding stories landed inside one day, and together they are the week. OpenAI is closing a $30 billion round within days at up to $1.25 trillion before the new money - Coatue and UAE investors leading, with a separate UAE compute negotiation running alongside, the money and the metal arriving from the same direction. Anthropic’s S-1 is expected any day, for the largest IPO ever. And DeepSeek closed its second round ever: ¥106 billion, nearly $16 billion, double the original target, at a valuation north of ¥600 billion, with a Shanghai listing in preparation. The least-covered of the three is the most structural: the Chinese state-adjacent capital system is now matching the American private one bid for bid, and DeepSeek is reportedly still trying to buy Nvidia chips anyway - sovereign stack for survival, American silicon for performance. The same morning, the US Treasury proposed banning American investment in high-bandwidth memory and all AI systems in China. Not specific companies. All AI systems. That is a proposal to decouple venture capital itself, announced in the week both ecosystems raised record money. For an India-based fund, this is the map for the next five years: two capital systems, two compute stacks, a compliance border between them.

When the fund can’t fund

The most useful VC story of the week is about plumbing. B Capital wanted to double down on Reflection AI in a $2.5 billion round, but the valuation had tripled in six months and a meaningful check no longer fit inside its $500 million fund. So the firm reached around the fund - twice over. First, the LP co-investment pool: capital raised directly from limited partners, outside the fund, concentrated into a few hot names at reduced fees - B Capital sometimes charges zero management fee and 10% carry instead of two-and-twenty. Through the pool it put $100 million into Reflection, double its previous check. Lightspeed has done $5 billion of these in five years, much of it into Anthropic; CalPERS put $744 million into a Thrive SPV for Stripe in 2023. Second, and newer: warehouse loans - revolving credit lines, a private equity instrument, now made by JPMorgan to venture firms so they can sign a hot round this week and raise the fund to repay the loan later. Speed is allocation, allocation is returns, returns are the next fundraise.

The fee shift is the tell. The fee-bearing capital - the main fund - is now too small to hold the firm’s best opportunities, and the fee-light capital is where the real money goes. Rounds over $100 million took 87% of all VC dollars in the first half; megafunds are re-raising in under two years, breaking the old cadence, because the last fund is already deployed into the same five names. The risk nobody is pricing: warehouse loans are leverage against illiquid marks, and the pools concentrate LPs into the same companies their funds already hold. If the Anthropic IPO stumbles, the exposure is triple - fund, pool and SPV - and some of it is borrowed. Correlation is the thing you cannot diversify when everything you own is the same thing three ways.

The other way out of the Nvidia tax

SemiAnalysis spent two weeks inside Amazon’s Trainium3 environment and came out impressed, which is not their default register. The knock on Trainium was always: fine for inference, unusable for research training - inference runs one fixed architecture millions of times, while researchers change the model daily and need the stack to handle anything PyTorch can express. The old Trainium stack forced a rigid compile-everything model with bespoke annotations; researchers hated it, and researcher hate is why CUDA won the decade. The news is a rebuilt, first-class native PyTorch experience - eager mode, torch.compile, standard APIs - which SemiAnalysis tested hands-on and calls genuinely usable, if not yet CUDA-mature. Two details matter more than the benchmarks: Amazon throws up to 80,000 physical CPU cores at automated quality control for the Neuron stack (software maturity is a capital expenditure problem, and Amazon is spending it), and the Neuron Explorer profiler produces visualizations SemiAnalysis calls “GPU impossible” - better than anything Nvidia or AMD ships. The proof point: Anthropic runs production inference and RL rollouts on Trainium2 and 3 today. Note the symmetry with Wednesday’s story: Huawei’s stack works for DeepSeek, Amazon’s works for Anthropic. Every Nvidia challenger has a customer of one - but Amazon’s customer of one may be a $2 trillion public company by next month, and its S-1 disclosures will move both stocks.

Politics as a competitive weapon

The Information reconstructed the Ramp-Airwallex feud, and it is a case study. Ramp tried to partner with Airwallex; the deal collapsed in April 2025 when Airwallex’s Jack Zhang pulled the plug, partly under investor pressure to compete instead. Ramp’s Eric Glyman warned the relationship might not stay friendly. It didn’t: Ramp’s politically connected investors - Joe Lonsdale, Keith Rabois - have since run a public and private campaign to get Washington to investigate Airwallex’s China ties: a think tank funded by the Leonard Leo network, a letter from Senator Cotton, a letter from the House China committee chair to the Treasury Secretary, a “Chinese backdoor” post from Rabois, whose husband is the Under Secretary of State for economic affairs. Airwallex hired Bill Barr. Two things are true at once: the China-data questions are real - Ramp’s own diligence shows they predated the feud - and this is also opposition research dressed as national security, funded by a competitor’s investors, timed to Airwallex’s IPO ambitions. The reason it matters beyond two fintechs: when China-taint becomes a cheap, undisprovable competitive weapon, every cross-border company - including every Indian startup with Chinese LPs, suppliers, or a Singapore holdco - should game out being on the receiving end. Watch whether Airwallex’s IPO proceeds on schedule. That is the tell.

Instacart’s exit math

Instacart is exploring a sale: working with a bank, preliminary conversations with DoorDash and Uber (the Uber talks are not ongoing), valued around $12 billion, with a typical 30-40% premium implying $16-17 billion. The buyer math is easy - Uber is $135 billion of market cap, DoorDash roughly $80 billion, and either could pay in stock. The obstacle is antitrust: both suitors run grocery delivery, so any combination invites scrutiny, though this administration’s enforcers have preferred conditions to blocks. Shares rose 10% on the report. A fourteen-year run as a standalone company, ended not with a bang but with a consolidation premium - and a lesson about building on a demand spike.
