A chip is a plumbing problem

SemiAnalysis has taken apart Intel's Panther Lake. Its 18A process is no longer a roadmap: a commercial chip carries both RibbonFET gate-all-around transistors and PowerVia backside power. That matters. But a shipped internal product is not yet proof of a profitable external foundry.

Transistors are switches. Their gates control current between source and drain. As switches shrink, a gate on one side struggles to stop current leaking through. The old fin design wrapped the channel on three sides; a gate-all-around design surrounds it. Intel stacks four thin channel sheets vertically in its RibbonFET. Better control can reduce leakage, but making and aligning the layers adds process steps. Every step can affect yield.

The second change is to the wiring. Ordinary chips route power and signals through frontside metal above the switches. The power rails occupy space signals could use, and narrow routes lose voltage. PowerVia places the main power distribution behind the transistor layer, connecting back to local contacts through tiny vertical vias. Intel builds the frontside signal stack, bonds the wafer to a carrier, thins the substrate until buried via ends emerge, then builds the backside metal. It is less a new lane on the road than a second road on the other side of the city.

Shorter, wider power routes can reduce resistance and free signal-routing space. Extra metal and carriers can add capacitance, thermal resistance and manufacturing complexity. The teardown records fourteen levels of frontside signal metal and six backside power levels. The measurement to watch is performance and power in a working package, not a node name.

One package, several owners

Panther Lake's compute tiles use Intel 18A. Its larger graphics tile uses TSMC N3E, and the input-output tiles use TSMC N6. Intel assembles these smaller specialist chips on a passive base through Foveros-S packaging. Reuse and smaller dies can save money, but bonding, interfaces, the base and test steps have costs of their own.

SemiAnalysis measures Intel 18A compute logic at density similar to TSMC N3E GPU logic, not ahead of newer TSMC N3P or N2 or Samsung SF2 peak densities. Density is only one dimension. Intel's CPU cores are incremental and the premium graphics tile is not made in Intel's fab. Its less expensive Wildcat Lake product uses the same 18A process but removes the passive base. The process can be competitive while the package economics differ.

A simple illustration: if three untested tiles each work nine times in ten, the odds of all three working together are about 73%, before assembly defects. Testing good tiles before bonding changes that calculation, at a cost. A foundry customer wants good chips per wafer, cost per good package and repeatable delivery. Intel now has a physical exhibit. The second outside order is still the score.

India's capital goes to different bottlenecks

The Runway's India deal roundup puts Brahma AI's $150 million round from Multiples at a $2 billion post-money valuation. Prime Focus retains 66% through DNEG, and the company says further commitments could expand the financing. Brahma sells content intelligence, management and creation tools. At this price, recurring software economics matter more than a cinematic demo. The question is how much revenue comes from repeatable workflow versus one-off production work.

Samara Capital agreed to combine Associated Road Carriers and Chennai-area container operator Calyx. Combined FY2026 revenue was reportedly Rs 2,140 crore. Samara did not disclose price; Mint cited about Rs 1,200 crore from unnamed sources. Logistics consolidation is a route-density and empty-mile problem, not a model benchmark.

Ampin Energy Transition raised $100 million from Norfund for new solar and wind capacity. Norfund expects the investment to draw another $700 million of private capital; that is an expectation, not funds already committed. Ultraviolette raised $85 million for electric motorcycles. Ema raised $77 million, Firecrawl $75 million and Dextr AI $6.7 million for different agent workflows. Wipro reportedly uses Ema across more than 100 workflows and roughly 2.9 million employee queries annually. Queries are activity, not proof of savings. Each deal should be judged by cash returned for its own scarce input: capital, energy, routes or human hours.

Being right too early is expensive

The Information finds AI short sellers wary despite concerns about debt, circular financing and valuation. Steve Eisman says he is not ready for a big short and is watching whether OpenAI goes public. Some see weak points in private credit, but mapping exposure is not the same as taking a trade.

Shorting has an asymmetric clock. Borrow a share, sell it for $100 and buy it back at $60, and the gross gain is $40. If it rises to $200 first, the position loses $100 before fees and may be closed by a margin call. An eventual thesis cannot repay a loss incurred on the path. The article cites S3 Partners estimates of $460 million in CoreWeave short-seller losses this year and $8 billion on Nvidia, while Oracle shorts reportedly gained $2.3 billion. Those are aggregate estimates, not the profit ledger of any one manager. Nvidia's revenue growth gives a bear little comfort while the stock keeps rising.

It is possible to think AI spending will overshoot and still decline to short a supplier with more than doubling quarterly revenue. A capital cycle is not a calendar.

The agent gets a short leash

The Information's weekend editor let Meta's Muse monitor music reviews, listings and SEC filings. It handled those low-risk jobs well. He hesitated when the app asked for access to email and a credit-card account, citing reported double-booked hotels and a reported security vulnerability. He did use it to set a thermostat schedule through a poor web interface. The task worked but took more than fifteen minutes.

Access is a product feature, not a free input. The business that becomes the customer's new front door must earn narrow, reversible permission before it can do valuable work in sensitive accounts. A successful alert does not confer the right to buy, book or read everything. There is also a cost question: a fifteen-minute agent run must save more than it costs in compute and oversight. Trust and unit economics may bind harder than model intelligence.
