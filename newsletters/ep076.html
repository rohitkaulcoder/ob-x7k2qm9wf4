The biggest IPO in history is on

Anthropic priced last night at $83 a share - several dollars above the $70-78 range, on an upsized share count, because investors asked for several times more shares than the company was selling. Fully diluted, that values the company around $1.5 trillion. Total raised, including the private placement: more than $94 billion - SpaceX’s record $75 billion lasted exactly five months. Shares list midday today on the Nasdaq, ticker ANT. Martin Peers’s call: he’d bet the stock opens at $150 as everyone and their dog vies for a piece - and that the smart money waits, because it probably settles lower in a few days. The offshore perps market is implying a $1.7 trillion valuation, but remember the SpaceX lesson from June: perps said $176, the stock opened at $150 against a $135 offer. The signal runs hot. Three things to watch when trading starts: the pop itself (banks target roughly twenty percent), the allocation (who got filled at $83, who got scaled back), and whether the stock holds its first hour - SpaceX opened strong and faded. The Information is running a two-hour live summit on the debut, which tells you how the trade press sees demand. And one continuity note from yesterday: Microsoft wrote a one-billion-dollar check into this IPO - the same Microsoft whose internal data says Claude is stalling in Office. Frenemies to the end.

The pretend demand is bigger than the real market

Matt Levine did the arithmetic and it is genuinely absurd. The book was close to seven times oversubscribed - call it $500 billion of orders. For scale: every IPO on US exchanges this year, all of them combined, raised $165 billion. The demand for this one stock roughly equals six years of American IPOs. The best illustration of how fake IPO orders are: Brad Gerstner’s Altimeter put in an order for $5 billion of Anthropic stock. Altimeter has $18 billion under management, of which $10.3 billion is in public stocks. He ordered half his portfolio. He will not get filled - everybody knows he will not get filled - and that is exactly why he could ask. Which is the entry point to the machine every founder eventually meets: how an IPO price actually gets made, from first principles. Step one, the bookbuild - the company and its bankers market a range and collect orders, and an order is not a contract, it is a vibe with a number on it. Step two, allocation - the company sells 825 million shares including the founders’ stock, 948 million with the greenshoe, the fifteen percent overallotment the banks can exercise if demand holds; seven times covered means most institutions get a fraction of what they asked for, and the bankers decide who: long-only funds who will hold, yes; fast-money flippers, zeroed. Step three, the pop - banks target roughly a twenty percent first-day gain, enough to reward the institutions who play ball, not so much that the company feels robbed. The range is conservative on purpose, the oversubscription is theater with real consequences, and the pop is a tip the company leaves on the table for the market. Today we find out if the machine still works when the deal is bigger than the last six years of IPO demand.

The revenue line versus the commitment line

The bear case is not the price - it is the plumbing. Anthropic generated $5.6 billion in free cash flow in the first nine months of this year - and it is about to give that up, shifting from renting compute from the clouds to leasing chips and running its own data centers. The prospectus says, coyly, this will change “the cash flow profile of our business.” Count the nut: $452 billion of purchase commitments and lease agreements due before the end of 2030. Against that: $33 billion of revenue in the first nine months, and an annualized run rate that hit $81 billion at the end of October. Peers’s contrast is the right one: Google went public in 2004 as a cash machine and never looked back; Anthropic is going public as a company choosing to become capital-intensive at the exact moment open-source models are pressuring prices. The man who has to make the math work is Krishna Rao, the low-profile CFO. Bloomberg’s profile: during the Pentagon guardrails standoff, when investors pushed Anthropic to cave on military restrictions, Rao was “a great firewall,” fielding direct calls from backers urging him to give in. XN’s Gaurav Kapadia, who holds a large Anthropic stake, credits Rao with keeping the company culturally intact. Rao’s own phrase for forecasting AI demand is the cone of uncertainty - and starting today, he forecasts in public, every quarter, with a bellwether ticker attached. The question public markets will ask every quarter is simpler: does the revenue line outrun the commitment line. $81 billion annualized against $452 billion of promises. Watch that ratio.

The matchmaker

The Nvidia saga keeps mutating. Thread one: the first all-hands without Jensen happened Wednesday morning. Colette Kress told employees, quote, we’ve got this - and one notable executive was on stage: Jay Puri, the former sales chief we covered yesterday, the one investigative roads may lead to. On stage. The search firm Heidrick & Struggles has started approaching outside candidates - one name already out: Arm CEO Rene Haas. Thread two is the bombshell, an Information exclusive we read in full: Nvidia’s own sales team introduced Alibaba to Alice Huang - twice, across several months in 2022, months before the export controls even took effect. The introductions helped her land Alibaba as a customer, and the Alibaba relationship helped her get the bank loans that financed the chip purchases that became Megaspeed. This is the first reporting that Nvidia, as a company, actively matchmade the arrangement - not a rogue founder, the sales organization. Thread three, the paper trail: in 2024, Megaspeed’s Malaysian subsidiary Speedmatrix pledged a range of its assets to a Singapore company ultimately owned by Alibaba - it is in corporate filings. Nvidia’s response: the company, quote, works across the industry to comply with the evolving rules. The stock slipped another 2.9% on a record quarter; it has lost more than a third of its value since early October. The business prints; the discount is governance.

And the China access story has a second front, also an Information exclusive read in full. ByteDance is in advanced talks to spend an additional $500 million on Microsoft cloud services - primarily to run OpenAI models through Azure. OpenAI is furious: it has complained to Microsoft for months that Chinese firms are, quote, distilling its models, and OpenAI itself blocks most Chinese customers - but Microsoft holds the resale rights from its $13 billion investment and doesn’t impose the same restrictions. The part that should make Anthropic wince on its IPO morning: at least part of the ByteDance business used to be Anthropic’s. Anthropic cut off Chinese customers last September, tightened enforcement in July - and Microsoft is now pursuing the other Chinese firms Anthropic cut off, including Alibaba. ByteDance was already spending a billion dollars a year with Microsoft on AI and cloud as of June. Microsoft says it found no evidence of distillation by ByteDance - software development, mostly - and Zhang Yiming has told employees ByteDance won’t do it. Same theme as the chips, different layer: every control creates a middleman, and the middleman always has a reseller agreement. Add Tencent president Martin Lau’s verdict on the new remote-access rule - it, quote, sets everyone back in China - and the detail that US banks are now demanding export-ban protection clauses in that $2.8 billion Bridge Data Centres loan covered yesterday. The compliance era is repricing everything.

The other side of the AI trade

Palo Alto Networks is buying Applied Compute - a young startup that customizes AI models on a company’s own data - for $4.5 billion in stock, and the stock jumped nine percent after hours. This is Palo Alto’s third swing this year: Chronosphere for $3.35 billion, CyberArk for $25 billion, and now a model-customization shop. The logic, per The Information’s Aaron Holmes: AI security will increasingly be handled by customized small models, and enterprises don’t want their sensitive data anywhere near the frontier models. And look at the week that proves the point. A string of nationwide outages - JPMorgan, United, Apple - got a House hearing, and the director of national intelligence told Congress these were not a coordinated attack: what is happening is AI models finding vulnerabilities faster than ever, forcing companies to rush out patches - presumably AI-written patches - and the patching itself is breaking things. The same week, the startup Calif found a critical Java vulnerability, and OpenAI gave NATO access to its advanced cybersecurity models. The offense-defense loop is now machine-speed on both sides.

The scoreboard

The venture lens, because the application layer is where our world lives. Bret Taylor’s Sierra is in talks to raise $1.5 billion at a $30 billion valuation, nearly double its May price; Lightspeed, Greenoaks and Benchmark are circling. The fundamentals behind the markup: Sierra crossed $400 million in annualized revenue, doubling in five months. Rival Decagon is around $100 million annualized and projecting up to $200 million by year-end, also talking to investors. But hold both truths: revenue is exploding, and these businesses do not have software margins - frontier model costs plus forward-deployed engineers eat the gross margin that used to make SaaS a seventy-percent-gross game. The market is paying software multiples for consulting-flavored margins. Also on the sheet: Isomorphic Labs raised $5 billion in a Series C led by Greenoaks and Sequoia; Genesis AI raised $500 million for general-purpose robots at a $3.5 billion valuation, led by Premji Invest - yes, that Premji - with Khosla and Eclipse; Model ML raised $250 million at $1.5 billion. The IPO window being open is pulling every private markup forward. And the turnaround story of the window is Netlify: twelve years old, growth flat a few years ago, now closing the year at $130 million in ARR, up 130% from $57 million. The reason: a hundred thousand new users signed up in a single day last week, two-thirds of them not engineers - marketers, small business owners, HR reps - arriving through ChatGPT, Claude Code and Lovable, shipping a million new projects a week. The platform goes from ten million developers to twenty-five million in one year. CEO Matt Biilmann’s framing is the thesis of the whole year: the SaaSpocalypse flattening of software is real, and the majority of software that runs companies is going to be built by domain experts. A twelve-year-old company found its second life by being the landing pad for everyone else’s agents.
