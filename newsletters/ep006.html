iPhone Duo day

It happened: John Ternus walked onto the Apple Park stage for his first iPhone event as CEO, said the words "one more thing," and unfolded the iPhone Duo - Apple's first foldable, nineteen years into the iPhone, priced from $1,999 to $3,200 depending on storage. The event - "Surprise and shine," under ninety minutes - also brought the iPhone 18 Pro and Pro Max, Apple Watch 12, Watch Ultra 4, and AirPods 5. But the Duo is the whole show.

Ternus took a direct swing at Samsung and Google on stage, mocking their foldables as "two phones glued together" - Apple's pitch is one continuous device that happens to bend. No Face ID, a genuinely strange omission on a $2,000 flagship. And for the history books: Jobs's exact "one more thing" cadence, on his first iPhone keynote, for the riskiest form factor since the original iPhone. That's a man announcing this is his company now.

The business case: Apple doesn't need the Duo to be a volume product - at $2,000-3,200 it's a margin and halo product, and Apple entering the category is itself the answer to whether foldables are real. The sleeper announcement was Siri Recap on the new Watch: always-on ambient listening that summarizes your day - Apple walking straight into the AI wearable fight with Meta, with the most privacy-sensitive feature imaginable, on the one brand people still trust with that. The stock shrugged (down 0.3%); the verdict that matters comes in December quarter numbers.

Anthropic's extinction number

An Anthropic safety researcher publicly quit and did not go quietly: he accused both Anthropic and OpenAI of acting irresponsibly, warned the technology will soon "revolutionize any field overnight," and posted that there is a greater than 10% chance AI could kill all humans within the next decade. Ten percent, from someone who works on the models. The Wall Street Journal covered the exit as a departure over "out-of-control AI fears."

The detail that made us sit up: Anthropic's own published risk report last month raised its assessment of models going off the rails from "very low" to "low" - reassuring until you notice the direction of travel.

The dark joke on tech X - a Wall Street analyst asking Dario Amodei about "the extinction number" on an earnings call - is about to be a real question, because Anthropic is weeks from an IPO roadshow. How many companies in history have had staffers openly saying the product could wipe out the population while the company raises money? Anthropic is about to fight a price war, market an IPO, and manage a safety revolt - three fires on one stage. (The follow-up, with a name and a face, led Ep 7.)

OpenAI's agents solve a decades-old math problem

A team of OpenAI agents - plural, coordinating and checking each other - solved an open theoretical mathematics problem that had stumped human mathematicians for decades. Not a competition problem, not a benchmark: the kind working mathematicians chip at for years. The Economist asks the right follow-up: if agents can crack decades-old theory problems, the bottleneck in math stops being insight and starts being which problems are worth pointing the machines at.

The controversy: The Information reports the result has stoked data-sharing concerns - a fight over who contributed what and who gets credit (paywalled, headline treatment). Pair it with the rest of OpenAI's week: working with Samsung on next-gen chips, telling Adobe and other partners they can no longer advertise competing image and voice AI inside ChatGPT, and the US government formally accusing six Chinese labs (DeepSeek, Moonshot, Alibaba, MiniMax, StepFun, Z.ai) of large-scale distillation. OpenAI is pulling up the drawbridge on every side in the same week it posts a math result that makes the frontier feel very real.

  • Peter Yang's review of GPT-6 Astra - "incredible and frustrating" - is the perfect counterweight: Star Fox clones in minutes, face-plants on everyday reliability. Both things are true at once, and that gap is the actual state of the frontier.

Where does a robot think?

SemiAnalysis published the best framing we've read on physical AI. The core insight: in language models, hardware bends to the model - train the biggest thing you can, then figure out serving. Robotics inverts that completely. A robot runs real-time control loops and can't miss a deadline; if the model is slow, the action is obsolete. And the manufacturer pays for the compute on every unit, upfront. So hardware is fixed first, and the model is designed to fit inside it.

One more inversion: LLMs had infinite text; robots have almost no training data, because every hour of robot experience costs real money in the physical world - hence the chase for world models and synthetic embodied data. That's why frontier robot models are tiny: Physical Intelligence's pi-0.7 runs at 5B parameters; NVIDIA's DreamZero is 14B and still needs two GB200 GPUs off the robot; Figure runs Helix entirely onboard. SemiAnalysis thinks the end state is a cascade: heavy thinking in the cloud, a small safety model on the machine.

The investor read: if the cascade view is right, every robot sold carries a recurring inference bill, and value capture splits between edge silicon and cloud GPU time - NVIDIA winning both ends. Meanwhile China curbed humanoid robot IPOs after Unitree's volatile debut: when a government rations IPO slots, the sector got frothy before it got real. For India: its robotics startups are almost all in the narrow-deployment bucket - warehouses and factories - precisely where on-device economics work today.

Lightfield's $47M second act

Lightfield raised $47M led by a16z, with Maverick, Coatue, Greylock, and Lightspeed in the pile. This is the team that built Tome - the AI presentation tool that hit 25M users and raised $80M in the hype wave - then looked at it, decided it wasn't the generational business, slashed headcount from seventy to seven, and rebuilt as an AI-native CRM. Now: 5,000 companies on it, net dollar retention of 400%, and CEO Keith Peiris describes the ambition as a "business world model" - ask any question about your company, from Asia expansion to this prospect's discount.

Salesforce has survived twenty years of declared killers, so the graveyard is real - but 400% NDR is the kind of number that makes you check the graveyard's math.

  • a16z's second announcement this week was very different flavor: Covenant, a defense company unveiling a heavy-payload long-range strike missile called Anthem, pitched as affordable mass. Defense tech is no longer the edgy corner of venture - it's the mainstream.

Levine: are AI data-center bonds the new catastrophe bonds?

Matt Levine's frame: two theories of risk. The academic one says you fear assets that blow up exactly when everything else blows up; the career-risk one says professionals actually fear blowing up alone on a sunny day. Revisiting the famous economic catastrophe bonds paper - AAA mortgage securities were safe on average but guaranteed to default at the worst possible time - he asks: are AI data-center bonds the new catastrophe bonds? Rated safe, correlated to everything, priced by people whose careers don't survive standing apart from the boom.

If the AI buildout wobbles, the question isn't whether the bonds lose money - it's whether they lose money at the exact moment your whole portfolio does. Levine's answer: obviously yes, and the ratings can't see it.

Source: Bloomberg - Money Stuff, Sep 9, 2026

Macro: Brent over $101, and a quietly bullish India signal

Brent crossed $101, up nearly 4%, after Iran said it's ready for a more intense war and won't back down against the tanker strikes and blockade. Trump says the war ends immediately after the midterms - weight that however you like. Ten-year yield at 4.84%.

And the Economist's Buttonwood tested Scott Bessent's theory that surging bond yields are just growth optimism by looking at India, where growth is blistering but government bond spreads are near historic lows. In plain terms: the bond market looks at India's growth and charges it almost nothing extra. A quietly bullish signal for everyone deploying capital there.
