Two red lights

One physical, one digital, both inside twenty-four hours. At 3:54 PM Eastern on Friday, the FAA ordered a nationwide ground stop, all departing flights held at every airport in the country, “due to extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety.” When the order went out, 4,042 flights headed for US destinations were already airborne, per Flightradar24; those continued to their destinations, while inbound international flights were handled case by case. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy says a communication issue hit multiple air route traffic control centers, and FAA technicians are investigating. It is only the second time in recent memory the FAA has stopped all departures nationwide; the first was the NOTAM system outage in January 2023. Meanwhile Microsoft spent Thursday and Friday fighting a global outage of Office, Outlook, Teams and Copilot, with hundreds of thousands of users in more than 70 countries reporting problems on Downdetector; by Friday afternoon access was improving but the company was still working through an email backlog, and says there is no indication of malicious activity. Add the Fireworks breach and the Grok Build data leak earlier in the week and the theme writes itself: the systems everything else runs on are the soft underbelly.

The last days

The Information’s reconstruction of Jensen Huang’s final week reads like a screenplay. After the first affair report surfaced last Friday, general counsel Tim Teter drove to Huang’s home and asked him directly whether it was true; Huang said it was. By Sunday he had apologized publicly and the board announced its investigation, but only after Teter urged reluctant directors, people who had known Huang for decades, to act. The detail that explains the speed of everything after: in conversations with senior Nvidia executives, Huang maintained the affair with Alice Huang was his only infidelity. Had that held, The Information writes, perhaps he survives. On Wednesday afternoon, news organizations reported the second affair, with Kimberly Powell, a vice president who reported directly to him, and one person familiar says the Powell news is what prompted the board to call the Wednesday meeting. That evening he resigned. Bloomberg’s inside account of his final hours carries a quote we will not fully repeat here. The family angle is striking: daughter Madison advised him to get his own publicist, having seen the writing on the wall, and the family had already retained crisis specialist Risa Heller, whose past clients include Harvey Weinstein. Huang has hired Paul Weiss and agreed to testify under oath, behind closed doors, before Congressional committees, before Thanksgiving. Nvidia says the investigation continues even though he is gone. One more thread to hold: The Information also reports companies connected to Alice Huang invested in DeepSeek after promising supplies of Nvidia Blackwell chips. As recently as October the stock was at an all-time high and his fortune topped $200 billion. That is how fast a legend unwinds.

The interpretive war

In one corner, Jessica Lessin at The Information: Jensen didn’t create the AI boom, the AI boom created Jensen. Four years ago the industry needed a poster child for a technology it couldn’t explain, and it built one, the leather jacket, the podcast circuit, the myth that cloaked, as she puts it, myriad affairs. Her prediction: what replaces the myth isn’t collapse, it’s competition, because Google, AMD and Amazon no longer have to compete with a legend. In the other corner, half of Silicon Valley. a16z’s Martin Casado called the firing a horrible decision, Deel’s Alex Bouaziz called it a mistake, Sarah Guo and Runway’s Cristóbal Valenzuela called it a big loss, and at the Cerebral Valley conference investors were openly debating whether the industry survives his exit, in their words, the way cockroaches survive nuclear fallout. The founder-control reading is the one actually relevant to a VC: Bloomberg notes Huang owned just 3.5% of Nvidia with no supervoting shares, versus Zuckerberg’s 13% with control at Meta and Musk’s roughly 48% of SpaceX. The Valley’s lesson is “never let go.” Washington’s input, for the record: Trump told Stephen A. Smith he always thought Huang was a fine man, David Sacks posted that the spy narrative is “collapsing already,” and Trump suggested, apparently seriously, Tim Cook for the Nvidia job. The actual succession race, per The Information: Arm CEO Rene Haas has been floated, and CUDA architect Ian Buck is the leading internal candidate. Our ruling: Lessin wins on the economics. Nvidia is largely sold out of AI chips for 2027 and 2028. The myth was a demand-side phenomenon. The supply side doesn’t need a rockstar to print sold-out silicon.

The third channel

The deep dive is money plumbing, because Bloomberg’s Odd Lots just surfaced the least understood way the AI buildout is raising everyone’s borrowing costs. The two headline channels are known: data centers push up inflation, and tech companies issuing hundreds of billions in bonds crowd out other borrowers. New research from John Briggs and Eric Benoist at Natixis quantifies a third channel, hiding in the swaps market. The mechanics: a data center developer borrows, say, $5 billion at a floating rate to build a facility; the hyperscaler tenant signs a fifteen-year lease with fixed payments. The developer’s income is fixed but its interest cost floats, so it goes to a swaps dealer and pays fixed, receives floating, locking its cost. Every trade has another side: the dealer is now receiving fixed and holding rate risk it must hedge, and the fixed rate on that swap is set by supply and demand. When thousands of megawatts of data centers all show up at once wanting to pay fixed, the price of fixed goes up. Natixis estimates this hedging flow has added up to $317 million of DV01 this year, dollar value of one basis point, the money that changes hands for each one-hundredth-of-a-percent move in rates. That is more than the $210 million they estimate came from the direct bond sales themselves. The plumbing flow is bigger than the headline flow.

Bloomberg, Odd Lots newsletter, Nov 13 (free, full text in email); Natixis research (John Briggs, Eric Benoist) as cited therein, framed as their estimate.

The broken rule

Why it matters: swap rates are quoted against Treasuries, and the gap, the swap spread, has followed one reliable rule for decades. When primary dealers, the big banks that have to absorb Treasury auctions, get stuffed with government debt, their balance sheet space gets expensive and Treasuries cheapen relative to swaps. Supply and demand for balance sheet: clean, mechanical, dependable. The Natixis work says that relationship is now out the window, because the flood of data-center borrowers paying fixed is keeping swap rates elevated relative to Treasuries regardless of what dealer balance sheets are doing. The AI buildout has become a rate-setting force that doesn’t show up in any issuance headline. The analogy: one homebuyer in town got so big that mortgage rates rose for everyone, but his loan isn’t in the mortgage statistics; you have to look at the plumbing of the bank that funded him. Connect it back to the week. Yesterday we quoted AllianceBernstein’s Samantha Lau on Anthropic’s IPO: don’t worry about profitability, worry about financing. This is the mechanism behind that sentence. Every basis point of rate pressure makes the 460-megawatt Nscale build, the West Virginia site, every project in yesterday’s $140 billion inventory more expensive to carry. And Nvidia reports earnings Tuesday with a chunk of its own balance sheet guaranteeing those projects. The AI boom’s cost of capital is quietly being set in the swaps market by people who have never trained a model. Underwriting an infrastructure-heavy AI deal from here, the question isn’t just power and chips; it’s what the swap desk is doing to your cost of debt.

The frontier blinks

Credit to Clouded Judgement’s Jamin Ball for spotting it. For two months, frontier model pricing held: $10 per million input tokens, $50 per million output, the same at OpenAI for GPT-6 Astra and at Anthropic for Fable 5.1. On Monday, Anthropic released Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 at $8 and $40, a 20% cut to frontier pricing. Ball’s math: the new models are about 30% faster, a chip costs the same per hour no matter how many tokens it produces, so 30% more tokens per hour means each token carries about 77% of its old cost, a 23% drop. Price fell 20%: they passed the efficiency gain straight through, keeping the three-point spread as margin. They didn’t have to. They could have held frontier pricing and banked the margin; that they chose not to, days before their IPO, says it’s still a land grab and they’d rather be the cheap default than the premium option. It is the Snowflake playbook: itemize the efficiency gains you hand back, storage compression, newer chips, faster queries, and train customers to expect prices to fall. Remember what Artificial Analysis told subscribers this week: the cost of a given task is falling five to ten times a year. A startup’s biggest cost line just got a standing annual deflation rate.

The Musk consolidation

The Information scoops that SpaceX’s AI group just axed most of the team behind Grok Imagine, its video and image generation tool: about 60 people laid off in the past week from the SpaceX Super Intelligence group, with a handful left to keep the product alive. The stated priority is AI coding tools, hence the $60 billion Cursor acquisition in August, and the Grok Bot agent product. Note the echo: OpenAI killed Sora earlier this year for the same reason; video generation burns enormous compute for weak revenue. Musk posted in March he was doubling down, that the next release would be epic. Instead, retreat. Before the cuts, Grok Imagine was generating more than two million videos a month in the March quarter, and its splashy Odyssey movie project was panned. The strategic cost is real: the American presence in frontier video models basically collapses to Google and Meta, while the leaderboard is now Chinese property. ByteDance’s Seedance 2.5 tops image-to-video on the Artificial Analysis rankings, Alibaba’s Wan 3.0 leads text-to-video, and MiniMax’s open-source H3 model is quietly becoming the foundation underneath US startups like HeyGen, Utopai and Pika; Grok Imagine’s latest model ranks 17th and 16th. Meanwhile the merger watch: Bloomberg says SpaceX, at $1.9 trillion, and Tesla, at $1.5 trillion, could announce a merger as early as December, which would be the largest deal ever done, and The Information adds the two live obstacles: how to wall off Tesla’s Chinese factories from SpaceX’s defense work, and a Democratic Congress that has promised to scrutinize everything Musk touches. Watch whether the Grok retreat reads as focus, or as freeing up compute and capital ahead of a deal.

IPO watch, day four

Bloomberg now reports Anthropic’s order book is about six times covered for the shares on offer, with the company and its co-founders seeking to raise as much as $64.4 billion in the IPO plus $26 billion in a private placement, on target to raise the most money of any IPO in history. Pricing lands next week against everything above: the Jensen fallout, the swaps-market cost of capital, and their own price cut signaling land-grab mode. One demand-side data point for the physical AI thesis: German robotics maker Agile Robots raised $500 million at an $8.5 billion valuation, led by SoftBank, with Nvidia, Hitachi and T.Capital in the round; the company just opened a Munich factory and expects to produce about 2,000 machines this year. Nvidia wrote that check days before its own leadership imploded, which rather proves Lessin’s point: the buildout’s momentum doesn’t live in one man’s calendar. And one from Meta that is either adorable or ominous: they’re testing an internal prediction-market app called Takes, where employees wager a currency called cred, short for credibility, on outcomes and events. No real money, unlike Kalshi or Polymarket, and it may never be released publicly. The company that bought its way through every product cycle now wants house odds on its own roadmap. If the employees are any good at predicting, Meta just built the world’s most honest product-planning tool. If they’re not, it built a casino.

Quick hits

In Washington, the midterm wave has one more scalp: the Associated Press has called Lauren Boebert’s Colorado seat for Democrat Eileen Laubacher, 48.8% to 47.9% with 99% counted, in a district Trump carried by 18 points in 2024. Laubacher is a retired Navy rear admiral who served on President Biden’s National Security Council; Boebert was seeking a fourth term. In gaming, with Grand Theft Auto VI landing November 19, staff at Rockstar Games in the UK are voting right now on whether to form a union, per Jason Schreier; the labor story of the biggest entertainment launch in history is happening in real time. And a16z published its big-pharma thesis, and the numbers are better than the argument: Eli Lilly alone is worth more than a trillion dollars, nine public pharma companies sit above $200 billion versus sixteen in software, the top thirty pharma companies are $5 trillion of market cap, and fewer than 10% of drugs entering human trials ever reach patients. Their bet: AI turns a hits business with terrible odds into something with software-like development economics, and the winners will be AI-native drug companies, not just vendors selling picks and shovels to Pfizer. For an India lens, that thesis travels: the same math applies to every high-cost, high-failure, decade-long development cycle, from agrochem to battery materials.

The scoreboard as of Saturday morning: Jensen Huang, resigned Wednesday night, investigation ongoing, Paul Weiss retained, sworn Congressional testimony before Thanksgiving. Nvidia under Colette Kress reports earnings Tuesday, and she leads her first all-hands Wednesday. Anthropic’s book is six times covered and it prices the largest IPO ever into the teeth of all this. The FAA is debugging the national airspace, Microsoft is debugging Outlook, and the swaps market is quietly repricing the cost of the entire AI buildout. And the frontier’s price tag fell 20% while nobody was looking. The thread connecting all of it is the one we’ve pulled all week: the myth layer of this industry, the leather jackets, the legends, the irreplaceable men, is being stripped away, and what’s underneath is boring, mechanical and wildly consequential: chips, contracts, swaps and tokens.
