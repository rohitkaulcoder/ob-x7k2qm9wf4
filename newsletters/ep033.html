The first draft gets cheap. Responsibility does not.

Coding agents change the quantity of software a team can attempt. They do not automatically change the quantity it can safely ship. This is the useful argument in Gergely Orosz's state of the tech industry, not the universal claim that nobody writes code by hand anymore. His frontier interviews describe engineers running five to ten agent sessions. The scarce resource shifts toward validation, attention and ownership.

A harness supplies the model with context, tools and checks. A worktree lets tasks proceed in separate working copies. Neither removes the need to decide what correct means. If the same agent misunderstands a requirement when writing the code and when writing its test, the agreement between the two is not proof. A complete golden path through the product can reveal failures at the joins that isolated components miss.

The investment implication is a hypothesis: verification inside a specific business workflow may become more valuable as implementation becomes less scarce. Count accepted changes and subsequent repairs, not generated lines. Orosz's interviews still put engineers at the production gate and still find teams and planning. We omit a platform-volume statistic whose newsletter passage contains a date inconsistency.

A standardized hedge contains someone else's choice

The standard US Treasury bond future allows its seller to choose among eligible bonds with roughly 15-25 years remaining. Conversion factors adjust the payment using a fixed 6% yield convention. The future therefore follows the bond that is cheapest to deliver relative to its adjusted payoff, not one immutable security.

Matt Levine's example uses a December futures value of about $102,188. A 2045 bond with conversion factor 0.6179 gives an approximate delivery payment of $63,141; a 2050 bond with factor 0.4086 gives $41,754, before other adjustments. The seller compares those payments with the cost of obtaining each bond. Below the conversion convention's 6% yield, shorter-duration eligible bonds tend to win; above it, longer-duration ones tend to. This is a tendency, not an exact threshold rule.

As long yields approach 6%, a cheapest-to-deliver migration can increase the contract's interest-rate sensitivity. A manager maintaining a duration target may reduce a long futures position. A basis trader on the other side may unwind cash bonds. That selling can amplify the initial rate move. Bank strategists warn about the mechanism; it is not a forecast of an inevitable crash.

The transferable question is who owns the choices inside an apparently fixed product. A hedge, an insurance policy or a software service can change its effective economics without changing its name.

Uber buys another occasion

The Information reports Uber's $2.3bn purchase of business catering platform ezCater, weeks after DoorDash's $300m college-campus meal-delivery purchase from Wonder. Geography is not the only expansion axis. A workplace buyer purchases a different workflow from a person ordering dinner.

The Briefing resists the easy claim that acquisitions mask a collapsing core. DoorDash's bookings growth had recovered before Deliveroo closed, although that deal contributed to the subsequent acceleration. Uber's food-delivery growth has accelerated while ride-hailing slowed. Its Delivery Hero transaction remains pending, according to this issue.

The deal price is the opening question, not evidence of attractive unit economics. Does the target bring demand the buyer could not cheaply create, and can the network improve the service without disrupting it? For founders, owning a distinct buying workflow is more interesting than adding a replicable feature.

Nykaa: better growth, a higher bar

Nykaa's provisional Q2FY27 update points to consolidated GMV growth around 30%, net sales value in the early 30s and revenue in the late 20s. These are expectations ahead of final results. Mint reports 14 additional stores, a total of 338, and existing-store sales growth in the early 20s, the strongest in six quarters.

Fashion grows faster than beauty but faces a different competitive and profit equation. A lower-margin B2B distribution mix can also dilute consolidated economics. GMV, net sales value and accounting revenue answer different questions; volume alone is not cash generation.

Q1 EBITDA of Rs 236 crore and an 8.5% margin are reported actuals. JM Financial's Q2 EBITDA estimate of Rs 256 crore is a forecast. With Mint reporting the stock up 5% since the update and 28% this year, expectations have moved too. Repeat buying, discount dependence and margin durability are the useful diligence questions.

Cross-border fintech is a stack of permissions

Wizz Financial wants India and its diaspora at the centre of a broader financial platform. Its CEO says India supplies about 60% of business and revenue, with the Gulf accounting for about 55% of its India business. A 2027 capital raise and 2029 IPO are plans, not completed events.

The Indian Wizzmoni operation, formerly Unimoni, has 368 branches in 21 states, according to Mint. Foreign exchange, money changing, non-bank lending and prepaid wallets require different permissions. Joining them into one interface does not make their risks identical.

Mint reports a stablecoin-based treasury solution for inward remittances. That is a reported capability, not proof of lower costs on every corridor. The article does not supply a corridor-level comparison or reserve details. It also reports Indian lending disbursements rising from Rs 525 crore to Rs 623 crore in FY25 while net profit fell from Rs 31 crore to Rs 9 crore. More product breadth deserves a sharper economics question, not a more generous adjective.

Morning Brew buys a relationship

Feed Me reports Morning Brew's all-cash purchase of Express Checkout, a consumer-brand newsletter with over 12,000 Substack readers. The founders become employees. The material read does not disclose a price, so there is no defensible deal multiple to calculate.

A specialized readership can include both professionals and curious consumers. Its value depends on who returns, who pays, and what decisions advertisers want to reach. Readers are not automatically paying subscribers or budget holders.

A buyer can add distribution and advertising sales while leaving the editorial relationship intact. It can also erase the reason readers chose the product. The same diligence question runs through today's stories: what is inside the visible product, and what must remain true for its value to survive?
