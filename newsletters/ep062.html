The wave was real - and so is what’s still out

Yesterday’s discipline paid off; today the calls exist. Democrats have won both chambers - a comfortable Senate majority, a House majority expected to rival Obama’s first-term size, with projections landing near 55 Senate seats and 250-plus in the House. All seven battleground governorships. Texas twice: Gina Hinojosa beat Greg Abbott 52.8-45.7, and James Talarico beat Ken Paxton for the Senate - the first Democratic statewide win in Texas in 32 years. Georgia flipped to Keisha Lance Bottoms, 54.3-45.7. Florida was the industry’s one hold: Byron Donalds by a point. California elected Xavier Becerra. The honest remainder: Alaska is still counting under ranked choice (Kreiss-Tomkins leads 49% at 74% in - uncalled), and Proposition 40 is leading toward defeat but not called - No up 55.3-44.7 with only 61% counted and the late count trending Yes. Seven Senate seats changed hands: Iowa, Kansas, Maine, North Carolina, Ohio, South Carolina, Texas. The coalition: independents, young voters, Latina women, and a near-20-point margin with college-educated whites. The Dow had its best day in 18 months, up 1,569 points.

AI was on the ballot, and it lost

The third factor behind the sweep, after affordability and Iran, is the one this newsletter tracks: AI became a voting issue. NBC’s 45-state exit poll: 23% of voters called data centers crucial to their vote; 87% called them a factor at all. Forty-three of 44 local ballot initiatives to restrict data centers won, many above 70% - an 80/20 issue, and Democrats ran toward it. The money that tried to stop them failed: Elon Musk spent roughly half a billion dollars, the largest outside spend in US election history, including $150M on Paxton in Texas alone. His post-election comment - “Data centers in space looking pretty appealing right now” - is the epitaph. Bloomberg Businessweek’s frame is the sharp one: voter anger aimed at politically connected businesspeople found the most connected one alive. Meanwhile Vance blamed the loss on the Iran War in public, Trump said he’d work with Democrats, and Mike Johnson declined to run for minority leader.

The data-center map, redrawn

In 12 states the incoming or leading governor backs a pause on data-center development: Alaska, Georgia, Kansas, Maine, Michigan, New Hampshire, New Mexico, New York, Ohio, Oregon, Texas and Wisconsin. Of the other 24, 22 back at least one restriction; 34 of 36 support ratepayer protections; 15 want to end the tax breaks. Those 12 sit atop 500-plus local bans and pauses - up from about 40 in January. Now look at the industry’s map against it: Stargate sites in Texas, Michigan and New Mexico; SoftBank’s SB Energy building 8 GW for OpenAI in Ohio, with an IPO filing that already warns permit delays could trigger tenant termination rights; Atlanta just past Northern Virginia as the busiest construction market in America, now with a governor who campaigned on a construction pause. The mechanics of a moratorium matter: it stops approvals, and projects in progress may be grandfathered - but none of the rules are written yet, and the details decide everything. The ripple runs developer to lender to AI company: interest on unrented buildings, delayed repayment, contracted compute that never arrives.

Bad for the landlords, great for the incumbents

The market’s first reaction was the obvious one - CoreWeave down 9%, Nvidia and the memory names down - and the second reaction was the interesting one: Amazon up 7%, Microsoft 6%, Alphabet 4%, Apple 6%. The Information calls it the Voters’ Gift to Big Tech, and the logic has two layers. Layer one: moratoriums stop approvals, not construction, so hyperscalers keep the gigawatts underway while the law caps their future capex - discipline investors were already begging for. Layer two is Tracy Alloway’s genuinely contrarian point: if the buildout is Dotcom 2.0 - too much compute supply, like too much telecom cable in 2000 - then a slowdown is not bearish. Constrained supply plus growing demand is pricing power, and weaker neoclouds mean less compute competition for the three clouds that matter. Even Apple wins if memory prices stabilize. Caveats apply: CoreWeave says no political shift touches its existing commitments, Cantor’s CJ Muse argues communities will soften once they price the jobs, and all of this is one day old.

Regulation gets a deadline, not a law

Precision matters here: 55 seats does not clear a 60-vote filibuster, and the veto pen remains. What changed is the pressure. Brad Carson, who runs the Anthropic-backed Public First network, expects a sweeping frontier-model bill - Jacobs-Beyer style, with independent audits of frontier models and catastrophic-risk guardrails - could pass by spring; that is his forecast, not a done deal. Jay Carney’s scenario is conditional but real: majorities this size make veto-proof, two-thirds legislation conceivable because many Republicans want to vote for AI rules too. What is not conditional: the gavels change hands in January, subpoena-driven oversight begins (Markey: “plant new forests”), and Michigan’s El-Sayed, who ran on AI safety, joins the Senate. Data-center rules stay with the states; Washington is coming for the models. Nothing is law yet - but self-regulation now faces a much stronger legislative challenge.

The gamblers were the informative ones

Election night vindicated this week’s running theme: markets price reality faster than institutions describe it. Joe Weisenthal flipped between the networks while watching Kalshi and Polymarket; the first Indiana ballots landed around 6:30 PM Eastern, the markets priced a blue tsunami almost immediately, and television took hours. Matt Levine’s version is more honest - Kalshi had a Democratic Senate at 98% by 8:30, so he went to bed and let the gamblers do the synthesis. The structure behind the prices is serious now: Hudson River Trading, one of the largest proprietary trading firms on earth, is moving into prediction markets. Across two days the pattern holds: an offshore crypto exchange priced Anthropic’s IPO pop more precisely than its bankers, and a betting site called a landslide before the networks. There are good journalistic reasons to move slowly and good gambling reasons to move fast; only one of them was informative on Tuesday.

The IPO rolls on, and the tax bill doesn’t fall

Through the wreckage, the Anthropic IPO machine keeps moving. Investors talking to Newcomer describe “infinite demand” from long-term funds and a company “bulldozing right through,” with a listing expected before Thanksgiving; OpenAI, in the same conversations, is “thirsting for a comeback story.” Michael Burry notes the amended S-1 carries Q3 but conspicuously leaves the October run-rate blank - the one number everyone wants. The human angle is Cerebras: priced at $185 in May, popped to $311, now around $92. Employees were taxed at the IPO price - $416M wired to the IRS via withheld shares - on stock now worth half that. The staggered lockup was the release valve: eleven selling windows, starting day one. Anthropic’s structure lets insiders sell 10% immediately, which frames the same tradeoff for its employees: liquidity early with the tax hit, or upside later carrying both price risk and a tax bill pegged to day one. The Cerebras lesson is not that the stock fell. It is that the tax bill didn’t.

The biggest AI landlord isn’t a cloud company

Apollo is assembling a $75B pile of AI chips - more than any neocloud, behind only the hyperscalers. The structure is the story. In the $40B SpaceX deal, an Apollo subsidiary buys Nvidia Vera Rubin chips and SpaceX leases them; June’s $35B raise with Broadcom and Blackstone does the same with Google TPUs for Anthropic. The mechanism, pioneered by CoreWeave and Blackstone: the chips sit in a special purpose vehicle - a legal wrapper that owns exactly one thing - and the AI company’s lease payments flow through it to service the debt. Apollo’s real customer is not SpaceX; it is the insurance companies whose annuity money wants high-grade yield backed by something real. The chips are the something real. Not on the chart: last week’s $63B Anthropic-Broadcom financing arranged by Citi, BofA and Blackstone, because nobody will say who owns those chips. The question for the morning after a slowdown election: are those leases as uncancellable as Anthropic’s own $417B in commitments?
