Day two, and it spread

The week ended the way it started: with selling. Friday was the second straight down day since Anthropic’s debut, and this time the damage spread well beyond one stock. Anthropic itself fell another 6% in German trading, and the mood carried into New York: the Nasdaq has now given back all of its gains for the year, and every one of the Magnificent Seven finished lower. By the Wall Street Journal’s tally, more than $4 trillion of market value disappeared this week alone. Asia was uglier. Taiwan’s Taiex closed out its worst week since 1991, Korea’s Kospi lost 8% across the week, and even copper fell to its lowest since March - the market quietly marking down the real-economy story too. Two weeks ago the consensus line was that the Anthropic listing would reignite the AI complex by proving demand. So far it has done precisely the opposite, and the speed of the reversal is the story. Part of the mechanism is supply: a hundred-billion-dollar IPO forces every fund manager to sell something to make room, and what they sold was the rest of the complex, because that is where the gains were sitting. Part of it is the discount rate: rate-cut expectations got re-priced again, and the AI trade is the longest-duration trade in the index - almost all of its value sits in cash flows years away, so a rising long end turns the present-value math vicious without a single thing going wrong at any company. Nothing about Nvidia’s quarter or Anthropic’s revenue changed this week. The price of waiting changed.

Effect into cause

The most useful frame for the week comes from Odd Lots, which has been revisiting a note strategist Jay Saut wrote in 2007, right as the housing bubble peaked. His observation: bull markets don’t end with a bang, they end with a shrug. Prices stop rising for reasons nobody can quite articulate, and only afterwards does everyone reverse-engineer the cause - effect into cause. Some event gets blamed, the narrative hardens, and the narrative then drives the selling it purports to explain. Layer on the structural problem this market has danced around for months. Walk the loop slowly: Nvidia puts equity into OpenAI, OpenAI signs a cloud contract with Oracle, Oracle uses the contract to justify buying Nvidia chips, which shows up as Nvidia revenue, which supports the valuation, which funds the next equity check. A dollar of investment becomes a dollar of revenue becomes several dollars of market cap, and it spins until something interrupts it. Flip the arrow and the same loop runs in reverse with the same leverage. At that point correlation isn’t a risk factor inside the system - correlation is the system. Then the political economy wrinkle: Paul Kedrosky argues Washington is quietly terrified of the boom ending, because AI data-center construction has become a private-sector stimulus program no politician voted for and none could easily replace. If he’s right, support arrives when things crack - but the dependence is itself the tell that things can crack. And the detail that should make bulls nervous: the pop in Anthropic’s own shares has nearly erased already. The landmark excitement lasted about a day. Saut would say the shrug has begun.

Bloomberg Odd Lots, “A Week for Big Questions,” Nov 20 (newsletter text as delivered; Saut 2007 note, correlation/circularity, Kedrosky’s argument - his interpretation, not a finding).

Inside the final lap

The Information published the definitive inside account of how Anthropic pulled the listing off, and the texture reframes the whole week. The effort started in summer 2025 with the company at roughly $5 billion of annualized revenue and the realization that venture funding alone could never pay for the chips. They hired the firm that took Apple and Google public, lined up Goldman and Morgan Stanley, and code-named the plan Lantern. By January they were projecting 2026 sales would quadruple to as much as $18 billion - and still had to push cash-flow breakeven out to 2028, because running the models costs almost as much as selling them brings in. February brought the fight: Anthropic told the Defense Department it would not allow Claude to be used for mass domestic surveillance or fully autonomous weapons, the department declared it a supply-chain risk, and a leaked Amodei memo about refusing “dictator-style praise” made it worse. Revenue doubled anyway: past $19 billion annualized by March, with Claude Code demand so intense it was rationed at peak hours - an opening OpenAI used to promote Codex’s limits. The control question got settled in late September: the seven co-founders, set to own less than 12% combined, asked shareholders for a special voting class and got over 90% approval. Then came the leaks - including confidential financials published the night a nearly final filing went to the banks - and it ended Wednesday night at Goldman’s headquarters over Gramercy Tavern takeout, where executives looked at demand in the hundreds of billions, moved the price from the $70-78 range to $83, and upsized. The celebration was deliberately muted next to SpaceX’s extravaganza: employees watched a livestream, and the founders rang the bell wearing pins of Clawd, Claude Code’s pixelated crab. Best detail: the advisers used Claude itself to rehearse for regulators’ questions.

The compute tab

The same reporting puts hard numbers on the appetite we covered yesterday, and this is where the skeptical case writes itself. Anthropic has assembled compute contracts totaling close to $900 billion. One of the strangest sits with a rival’s sibling: co-founder Tom Brown approached Elon Musk about renting capacity in Memphis, and Anthropic committed to spend well over $120 billion with SpaceX through the end of the decade. There’s also a six-year deal with neocloud Nscale in West Virginia, struck in about five weeks after Microsoft walked away from the same site. The company has already told public investors it will likely need more short-term compute, which costs significantly more. Amodei has been disarmingly honest about the bet: overbuy compute and miss revenue, he said on a podcast in February, and there’s no hedge on earth that stops him from going bankrupt. That sentence reads very differently now that it’s everyone’s portfolio problem.

A beat that wasn’t enough

Nvidia reported its first hundred-billion-dollar quarter and beat expectations - and the stock still fell about 3% on Friday to close near $146. A beat that sends a stock down confuses people, so the mechanics matter: when a company is priced for perfection, expectations drift above the published consensus, and you have to beat the number everyone secretly believed, not the one they wrote down. Nvidia beat the first and missed the second, inside a market-wide multiple compression. Governance is hardening too: chair Harvey Jones has ruled out reinstating Jensen Huang even though at least three directors privately pushed for it; Brad Gerstner’s much-parsed letter was careful to be something other than an endorsement; and CFO Colette Kress is reportedly dialing back the dealmaking machine that signed 121 venture deals this year - a board trying to prove it can be a normal company. In the rumor category, Mark Gurman floats Apple silicon chief Johny Srouji as a long-term successor candidate. That the name comes from outside the building tells you how unsettled the bench looks.

Google’s quiet rehab

One company had a quietly excellent week: Google. Gemini 4, code-named Neon, is landing well with the people who test these models for a living, and in a genuinely rare move the research shop SemiAnalysis retracted its earlier verdict that Google was cooked. Keep the leadership subplot straight: Demis Hassabis stepped back as DeepMind’s CEO in August, and this week his own company, Isomorphic Labs, raised $5 billion co-led by Greenoaks and Sequoia with Alphabet participating, to push AI drug discovery. So when you read breathless takes about DeepMind in turmoil, hold two facts at once: the founder left months ago on his own terms, and the lab he left just shipped its best-received model in years. Both are true; the second matters more for the stock.

Newcomer, Nov 20 (Neon reception, SemiAnalysis retraction). CNBC, Nov 17 (Isomorphic $5B Series C; Hassabis stepped back three and a half months earlier).

Washington, India, and the rest

President Trump is floating what he calls a tremendous tax on data centers built overseas - aimed at companies siting AI infrastructure abroad to dodge American power queues and permitting. Whether a tariff on buildings is workable is open; the direction is not. Compute is being treated like steel was last century. Separately, Senator Tom Cotton pressed Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick on the China chip posture, and the administration opened more than 31 million acres of seabed to mining. In India: Lenskart is up about 80% since listing with profits nearly tripling - a rare consumer-tech IPO doing what the prospectus promised. The government approved viability gap funding of Rs 14,500 crore for storage - 30 GWh of batteries and 60 GWh of pumped hydro - inside a program now totaling Rs 97,600 crore; storage, not panels, is the real bottleneck for the solar buildout. Mahanadi Coal’s IPO priced at just 8.4 times earnings. COP31 heads to Turkey; India slipped to 28th in the climate performance index. Quick hits: GTA VI is out to rapturous reviews, and Bloomberg’s Jason Schreier found the secret - 21% of Rockstar’s staff, around a thousand people, have been there over a decade. Nikhil Gupta was sentenced to about twenty years in the Pannun murder-for-hire plot. Citigroup is raising $35 billion for AI data-center financing. And the security crowd warns AI-discovered vulnerabilities now get exploited so fast that the rush to patch is itself causing outages. That’s the week.
