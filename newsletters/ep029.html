Ep 29: A Decision, and How Sure You Are

Overnight Brief, written edition · Sat, Oct 3, 2026 · audio: 17:28
🎧 Listen to the audio episode

A decision is a different product from an answer

Most AI talk is about models that write. A decision model does not. It reads unstructured input, a support ticket, a contract, an agent's proposed next step, and returns a choice and a confidence. Is this customer threatening to churn: yes, 87 percent. Which team gets this ticket: billing, 92 percent. How urgent, one to ten: 3, 78 percent. This is not really a story about cheaper models. It is a story about a new layer in the stack.

Start with the mechanism. A language model ends in a score for every word in its vocabulary, tens of thousands of them. A step called softmax turns those scores into probabilities that sum to 100 percent. The model picks a word, appends it, and runs the whole network again. A decision model keeps the machinery and shrinks the menu to two items, or five, or ten. The probability comes out of the last layer. It is not an add-on. What takes work is making it honest. A model is calibrated when it is right about as often as it says. If it claims 87 percent on a hundred cases, about 87 should be correct. A confident number that is wrong 40 percent of the time is worse than no number.

The price follows from the shape. No long output to generate, and per Jamin Ball's Clouded Judgement, Jev from TypeSafe is quoted at $0.042 per million input tokens with free output. Take a million support tickets of about 500 tokens each. That is 500 million tokens, so about $21. Illustrative arithmetic on the quoted price, not a measured bill. Ball is careful about the headline: more than 100x cheaper than leading models, but closer to 4x cheaper than a small general model such as GPT6-Luna, which is the fairer comparison. Accuracy and latency matter as much as price. A workflow of many if-then steps, each a classification, only feels instant if each step takes milliseconds.

Adoption has been fast. Per the piece, Jev was used by about 13 percent of paid teams within 24 hours of launching on Vercel's AI Gateway, the fastest uptake in its history, and The Information reports talks at a $10 billion valuation. This week OpenAI announced a Decisions API, Databricks added an ai_decide function, and Perplexity released an open-weight decision model. The use cases are control functions: route a prompt to a model, decide whether an agent should continue, stop, retry or ask a human, judge an output against policy, flag unusual contract terms. Ball relays an AI's guess that 10 to 15 percent of tokens could be replaced. It is a guess, and he presents it as one.

The second-order point is about price times quantity. Quantity of tokens is rising by orders of magnitude. Price was always expected to fall, but without a mechanism. Open-weight models were the first real one. Decision models may be the second. Cheaper work gets automated more, which can lift total spend. It also takes the easy work away from frontier models and leaves them the hard work. For an India-focused investor, the interesting companies sit above the model: the policy and calibration layer that decides when a number can be trusted.

Anthropic's IPO is a leap of faith with a fixed price

Newcomer and Big Technology both treat Anthropic's coming listing as a test of the AI cycle. Reuters obtained what appears to be a draft prospectus. Revenue was about $4.6 billion last year, up from $400 million in 2024, against an operating loss of around $8 billion and $7.3 billion of infrastructure spending. It expects to spend $518 billion on infrastructure over the next decade, and per Big Technology about 80 percent of that is non-cancellable, including roughly $111.1 billion to Alphabet, $110 billion to Amazon and $31.4 billion to Microsoft, committed regardless of usage.

That last phrase is the point. It is a take-or-pay structure, like leasing a stadium: the rent is due on empty days too. The bet is that revenue growth outruns fixed costs. Newcomer notes the leaked numbers predate Opus 4.6, which kicked off the agentic coding boom, and Big Technology expects about $100 billion of annualized revenue this year. The counter-risks are real. Non-frontier models are good enough for many products, businesses are trimming AI spend, and data centers are drawing political opposition.

The reason this reaches beyond one company is breadth. Goldman, via Big Technology, says the S&P 500 has its worst breadth since the dot-com bubble: the median stock is 16 percent below its high while the index is up about 13 percent, Nvidia added over $1 trillion in 2026, and half of earnings growth comes from AI investment. Anthropic has moved its target from October to November. SB Energy delayed its IPO and Oura postponed indefinitely. OpenAI is raising privately, at least $30 billion at $1.4 trillion, with reports of annualized revenue near $70 billion. If the first public numbers show growth still booming, the cycle gets runway. If not, a lot of balance sheets sit on top of the shortfall.

The financing is moving off the balance sheet

Bloomberg's evening briefing reports Broadcom seeking $60 billion of AI chip financing to benefit Anthropic and others, led by Blackstone, with a $42 billion senior secured tranche about to be syndicated. Separately, per the Financial Times, Amazon is exploring moving about $8 billion of Nvidia Grace Blackwell chips across more than a dozen US data centers into a special-purpose vehicle funded by outside investors.

An SPV is a separate company that owns the chips and rents them back. The borrower's books look lighter. The vehicle's investors get steady rent from a strong tenant. It works while everyone agrees on what the chips are worth, which returns to last week's question about useful life. The market is selective, not closed: Firmus Grid priced its Australian IPO at A$11, seeking at least $5 billion at roughly a $30 billion valuation. A good test for any structure in this stack is to ask which number in the model is somebody's opinion.

India's subsidy paid where production was already easy

The Core's Plinth analysis looks at the production-linked incentive, launched in 2020: choose a listed product, invest a minimum, sell more than in a base year, and receive a share of the extra sales for five years. It grew to 14 schemes worth Rs 1.97 lakh crore. By June 30, 2026, Rs 36,754 crore had been paid, 18.7 percent of the amount announced, in year seven of a five-year programme.

Payouts are concentrated. The phone scheme has paid Rs 19,091 crore, more than half of the total. Pharma has paid Rs 6,662 crore and food processing Rs 3,271 crore. Those three make up 79 percent. The Rs 17,000 crore IT hardware scheme has paid Rs 98 crore. The reason is structure: investment and sales targets must be met in the same year, sales are measured against a fixed base year, some schemes require local value share, claims are checked against tax records, and money arrives after the year closes. Miss one gate in a year and, in most schemes, that year's payment is lost for good. A company can build the plant, hire the workers, start production and collect nothing.

India has started round two before closing round one, with a Rs 62,500 crore phone successor approved in July. For founders, treat the subsidy as a conditional payment, not revenue. For policy, the lesson is that money flowed where manufacturing was already viable. Separately, The Core's number of the day: Maruti sold 1.81 lakh passenger vehicles to dealers in September, up 37 percent, ahead of a festival season that starts a month later than last year.
