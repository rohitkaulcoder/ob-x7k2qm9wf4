Seven to ten

Ask data center executives how many of the hundreds of AI cloud startups will still exist in a few years and the answer is seven to ten. Gartner says 80% of independent neoclouds will cease independent operations by 2030; Bernstein thinks only 35% of announced North American capacity ever gets built. The markings have started: Firmus pulled its IPO, CoreWeave and peers shed about 20% in a week, and Microsoft scrapped a planned UK site Nscale was building after power delays, right before Nscale’s IPO this month, where investors are already pressing to cut the $30 billion valuation target.

Two failure modes matter. A leasing cloud like CoreWeave can fail without derailing the buildout; its sites find new tenants. A builder like Nebius, IREN or Nscale failing stalls the buildout itself, and Nvidia’s sales with it, because chip sales are capped by powered data centers. Which is why Nvidia behaves like a central bank for its own customer base: $40 billion, 45% of July-quarter data center revenue, from the everyone-else bucket; $20 billion of its own leases to pass down; a team led by Jensen Huang’s son-in-law scouting powered land; credit support already restructured once after bigger players passed. Huang saying in Stockholm that CoreWeave needs no further backstop, with a $6.3 billion GPU rent-back agreement already disclosed, is the kind of reassurance one gives when reassurance is needed. Add one investor’s claim that Anthropic has deliberately over-committed leases, and Wednesday’s Meta print, $40.5 billion capex, a first negative free-cash-flow quarter at -$10.3 billion against $90 billion of cash, becomes the week’s referendum on whether the debt still believes itself.

The movie India already watched

Asianometry’s new history of Indian IT outsourcing is the week’s best long read, and the plot is a machine: cost arbitrage, a communications link, and a policy regime that eventually gets out of the way. TCS begins in 1966 in one of Bombay’s first skyscrapers, survives on outside clients, gets F.C. Kohli in 1968. FERA’s 40% cap forces IBM out entirely in 1977 and Burroughs into a Tata partnership; the Software Export Scheme demands double earn-back on imported hardware, so the early industry is porting COBOL line by line, partly delivered by engineers flown to America: body shopping. Ramadorai types letters in New York until a bank consortium bites; Indian engineers work while New York sleeps. Murthy leaves Patni in July 1981 with six colleagues and his wife’s $250, and Infosys’s first move is to win his old client.

The unlock was not talent, India always had talent; it was communications. Up to 80% of late-eighties export earnings were still body shopping because a landline took years. Texas Instruments’ 1985 satellite link to Bangalore, pushed through by officials who boasted of breaking twenty-six rules, plus technology parks, tariff cuts and the 1991 liberalization, created the offshore model. Then Y2K industrialized it: TCS’s Chennai Y2K office ran a thousand programmers through two million lines of COBOL a day, the industry made $2.3 billion, Infosys listed ADRs in 1999 and went up tenfold in a year. Backlash followed by 2004. The uncomfortable question the piece asks today: when offshoring comes for services again, the low-end work is exactly what models eat first. Own the client, or own the leverage; last time the window was twenty years, this time maybe four.

What a finished exit looks like

Affirma Capital has fully exited TBO Tek: 12x in dollars, 49% IRR, $468 million of gains on $42 million over eight years. The construction is the lesson. A 49.9% secondary in 2018; a third sold to General Atlantic pre-IPO at over 9x; a slice in the 2024 offer-for-sale at about 11x; the rest this year at roughly 17x, finished in the sixteen-hundreds even after the stock round-tripped from a 55% listing premium to a halving. In rupees: 14.9x, 54% IRR. In a market where DPI is the only metric LPs still trust, a completed exit of this quality is marketing collateral for the whole India venture story.

Good enough wins the volume

The Information’s Pro report pairs OpenRouter token data with Artificial Analysis quality ratings: the ten highest-quality models now carry 14% of developer tokens, down from 55% last year. The method is borrowed from a FanGraphs postmortem of the Giants’ season, actual performance versus projections, applied to token share versus quality ratings. Caveats: developer-platform usage only, and quality still pays on long verifiable tasks. But if the commodity dynamic holds while 45% of Nvidia’s revenue assumes endless frontier demand, the bear case is not that AI fails; it is that AI succeeds at a lower price point than the debt was underwritten at. Note the same week’s supporting cast: DeepSeek shipped a faster, leaner model, and Beijing drafted export controls treating those commoditizing models as strategic assets.

Quick hits

Venture-backed upstart banks, Erebor, Openreserve, Augustus, face a treadmill: the tier-one leverage ratio means growth consumes the balance sheet, so success forces dilution; banking is the one startup category built that way. Netflix reports Tuesday without its chief content officer, Bela Bajaria out, Peter Rice in, shares down 25% this year at 5.6x forward revenue to Disney’s 2.2x. Tesla reports Wednesday: deliveries down 2%, a Roadster revealed Thursday that ships in 2028, and open merger-hint season with SpaceX. Anthropic is endowing a Public Benefit Fund with at least 5% of its shares. Bloomberg finds AI data work gone white-collar, expert trainers at up to $400 an hour, and Micro1 spending $1 billion to buy operational data. Lenny’s Podcast has Anthropic’s Emmanuel Ameisen on how models actually think.
