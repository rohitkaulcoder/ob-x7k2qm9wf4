The day money divorced votes

Two of the biggest stories of the night are the same story. Meta announced a new class of nonvoting stock, Class C, effective January, so it can sell equity to fund the AI buildout without Mark Zuckerberg surrendering a single vote. Elon Musk used SpaceX’s earnings call to call a merger with Tesla “a good idea” - a deal that would fold Tesla’s single-class shareholders, the only Musk shareholders with real votes, into a dual-class company where he holds 84% of the vote and appoints the board. This is not really about financing. It is about the price of capital falling to zero for founders who keep control. The AI buildout needs more money than any operating business generates - Meta just posted negative free cash flow of $2.1 billion against capex guidance of $135-145 billion - and the market’s answer, on the same night, from two different companies, was identical: here is money with no strings, because the strings are the product.

The details repay attention. Meta’s Class C arrives as a one-for-one dividend, so nobody is diluted on day one; the dilution is scheduled for later, when the equity offering comes. Musk’s version is stranger: a roughly $3.5 trillion merger of equals in which, as Levine notes, a standard banker fee would be $3.5 billion for a negotiation one man could hold with himself in five minutes - except he legally cannot be in the room, so two special committees perform what Levine calls an odd kabuki dance while he looms. Nobody has explained what happens to his two near-trillion-dollar pay packages when he owns both halves. Also lost in the noise: a New Mexico judge ordered Meta to pay $25 billion over Cambridge Analytica, one of the largest verdicts in US history, on the same day as the record quarter. Record revenue, a $40-billion-ish legal tab, a new currency to print. The market said: up two percent.

The stack built for a customer of one

SemiAnalysis’s deep dive into the Huawei-DeepSeek symbiosis is the week’s most instructive piece of engineering journalism. The trade: DeepSeek has frontier models and cannot buy compute; Huawei has silicon and no frontier lab to prove it. Each supplies what the other lacks, and the exchange runs through kernel code - DeepSeek’s entire open-source stack now supports Ascend. The fingerprints of constraint are everywhere if you know how to read them. Nvidia’s yields are good enough that it bins compute and ships one memory configuration; Huawei bins both dies and HBM stacks, pairing weaker memory with weaker compute across a family of SKUs, the mark of a fab fighting yield on two fronts. The software is real but young: of six released libraries, only TileLang, the kernel-authoring language, is usable today. And the serving traces are genuinely elegant - fused compute-and-communication kernels, speculative decoding, a lookup structure offloaded to cheap DRAM without speed loss, the main compute stream busy 89% of the time.

The second-order point is the one to keep. The ecosystem is being built for DeepSeek’s exact configuration: hardcoded ARM headers, no x86 support that works out of the box, patches required. That is a moat for DeepSeek and a trap for everyone else, including any Indian team tempted to build on a cheap Chinese stack. Pair it with a16z’s history essay on the killer micros: in the 1990s every specialized architecture - Lisp machines, Thinking Machines - died not because the specialists were better at unimportant problems but because the general-purpose chip beat them at their own game and then absorbed their ideas. Nvidia is the generalist now. The question of the decade is whether AI workloads are different enough that specialization sticks, or whether this is 1994 again with better marketing.

Tokens and a harness

The Information’s CyberCartel exclusive is a genuine first: a crime syndicate running fully automated AI attacks on more than 400 Latin American banks and payment apps. The architecture is the story. Grok agents mined the web for reconnaissance. Four Claude agents deliberated on strategy. A Claude instance inside Cursor acted as commander. When a model’s guardrails refused, the system cascaded to a smaller Moonshot Kimi model, and failing that, to plain scripted malware. The jailbreak was almost disappointingly simple: tell the model it is running an academic test. Ken Huang’s line deserves to be quoted everywhere: tokens and a harness are all you need - the bottleneck is no longer skill, it is an API budget. This lands the same week the “defender’s window” - the gap where AI helps defenders patch first - is reported closing, with Chinese open models perhaps four months behind the frontier. The same week, Thoma Bravo’s Proofpoint agreed to buy Varonis for $6.3 billion and its cyber partner declared “agentic revenue, not an agentic vision” the new must-have. The burglar and the alarm are both AI now. Both sides of that trade are being priced, and both sides are right.

Stratechery takes the ads

After twelve years of pure subscriptions, Ben Thompson announced Stratechery is now presented by Stripe - the first sponsorship in its history. His reasoning is worth reading in the original: ad sales made no sense for a one-person shop, so he bet on readers; twelve years later the audience is large and attractive enough that he can run what he calls the unified content business model, subscriptions plus advertising, with a team to sell it. The patron saint of paid email concluding that the mature version of paid email includes ads is a data point for every indie media business, Indian ones included. Build the subscriber base first. Sell the audience second. Never the reverse.
