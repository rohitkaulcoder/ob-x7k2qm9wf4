The print was not the news

Nvidia’s first earnings call without Jensen Huang answered its own question: revenue of $115.8 billion - a record, more than double the year-ago quarter, comfortably ahead of the 89% growth Wall Street projected. The guide is 91% growth next quarter, the full-year growth rate raised five points to 75%, and the stock jumped four and a half percent after hours. But the number was not the news. The news was Harvey Jones - the new chair - making what The Information called an unusual appearance on the call to say two things. First: the board, quote, does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO. That landed just hours after Jensen himself issued a plaintive public statement saying he would love his job back. And second - the sentence that matters - the exit came, quote, after disclosure of personal behavior inconsistent with the company’s code of conduct, standards and cultural norms. As The Information’s Martin Peers reads it, that means it was the affairs - with a subordinate and a business partner - and not the smuggling question. The board is formally on record: a code-of-conduct firing, and, quote, deliberate - a pointed answer to the Gerstner-Baker letter demanding a slow, measured, deliberate process. On a permanent CEO: move quickly, but do not expedite. Do not hold your breath.

And yet - because there is always an and yet with this company - the Wall Street Journal dropped its own bomb overnight: the SEC is investigating Nvidia. We only have the Journal’s headline treatment, so the contours are thin, but the probe reportedly includes whether the company’s disclosures misled investors. Think about the timing: a record quarter, the chair on the call insisting everything was principled, and now a securities regulator asking what investors were told, and when. As for the print itself: Kress’s first call as interim CEO was a continuity statement dressed as an earnings beat - raising the full-year guide by five points in your debut is not a CFO being conservative, it is a CFO telling the market the machine runs without the founder. Scale check: this one Nvidia quarter is roughly eight times the quarter AMD reported two weeks ago. So the business is fine. Everything around the business is not: an SEC probe, a Justice Department criminal probe into customers diverting chips to China, a Senate that still wants testimony, and a founder publicly campaigning for his job back. A doubling of revenue papers over a lot - but it does not paper over subpoenas. One more market note: Bloomberg’s Odd Lots team points out that the ripple effects of a Jensen-less Nvidia, plus the hawkish new export rule, are getting priced into Asia-listed data-center stocks first - the picks-and-shovels trade is where the doubt shows up before it shows up in the Nasdaq.

Forty-eight hours to ANT

The Anthropic IPO machine entered its final forty-eight hours. The order book closed at 4 PM Eastern yesterday; the pricing decision lands late today. The Journal says shares could price above $80 - the range was $70 to $78 - and advisers have discussed valuations of one and a half to one point six trillion dollars, up from the roughly one point three trillion the range implied. The Information’s reporting matches: around one and a half trillion, with pricing above range or an upsized share count both on the table. First trade is Thursday on Nasdaq, ticker ANT - and The Information is running a live summit for the debut Thursday, which tells you how they see demand. One strand to hold from yesterday’s show: Microsoft is writing a one-billion-dollar check into this IPO - the same Microsoft whose internal data says Claude is stalling in Office. Frenemies to the end. What to watch tomorrow: the opening print, obviously, but also the allocation - who got filled and who got scaled back. A deal this covered means most institutions get a fraction of what they asked for, and the first-day pop tells you how much demand was real versus flippers.

Power law, with names on it

The favorite document of the window, because it is a venture-capital sermon with receipts. The Information calculated what the IPO means for the VCs who actually showed up. For Anthropic’s first two years, real venture firms shunned it - the money came from wealthy individuals like Eric Schmidt, and from the clouds. Then, early 2023, Spark Capital’s Yasmin Razavi writes a $75 million check to lead a $450 million round at a $4.6 billion valuation. Spark invested $777 million across six financings - a stake now worth about $14 billion. Eighteen times the money. Factorial Funds - Sol Bier had been tracking Cruise engineers who decamped to Anthropic - put twenty percent of its first fund into the Series C; $565 million total, now worth $10 billion. Also eighteen times. Sound Ventures - Ashton Kutcher and Guy Oseary’s shop - put in $435 million across every round; now $7 billion, sixteen times. And Menlo went biggest of all: led a billion-plus round at an $18 billion valuation with the largest check in firm history, then raised a $750 million SPV to buy even more. One billion in, roughly twenty-one billion out. Twenty-one times. Iconiq and Lightspeed hold the largest stakes, over $25 billion each. And the ghost in the cap table: FTX was in for $500 million before the collapse - that stake ended up in bankruptcy court. The lesson for every fund listening, and we include ours: the best deal of the decade looked uninvestable for two full years, and the winners sized up the moment it got obvious.

The moat reprices

Deep dive: the story that quietly became the most important one for the next three years - the CUDA moat is cracking. From first principles: for a decade, Nvidia’s real lock-in was never the chips - it was CUDA, the software layer every AI developer learned in school. If you write AI code, you write it in PyTorch, the framework Meta released a decade ago - and PyTorch sings on CUDA. Google’s TPUs spoke a different language, a framework called JAX that developers found clunky; porting a codebase felt like moving countries. So even when rival chips were good, the switching cost WAS the moat. Two things just happened. First, late last month, Google open-sourced TorchTPU - developed with Meta, itself a TPU customer - which lets developers run their existing PyTorch workflows on Google’s chips with, quote, minimal code changes. Google’s line: you can now train, serve and deploy on TPUs using the tools you already rely on. That is Google building its own CUDA - in public, for free. Second, AMD’s answer to CUDA, a layer called ROCm, finally stopped being a joke: TensorWave, an AMD-backed cloud, says the staff time spent helping customers crack ROCm is down 80 to 90 percent since last year. When the software tax disappears, all that remains is price and supply - and Nvidia chips are still expensive and hard to get. There is a second technical reason AMD specifically is catching the wave, and it is the word inference. Training rewards Nvidia’s absolute peak performance; inference - running a trained model millions of times a day, the part that is actually becoming the business - rewards cost per token, and on cost per token a cheaper chip with finally-usable software wins. That is why the AMD converts are inference shops first: Superlinked running open-source models, Cirrascale’s customers, the DeepSeek-style small-model boom. The workload moved, and the moat did not move with it.

And the demand side is now measurable, which is why this stopped being theoretical. The Information mapped the actually-existing alternative-chip economy. Cirrascale, a sixteen-year-old cloud with twenty-five data centers, just became one of the first providers of Google TPUs outside Google Cloud - and says customers are signaling hundreds of millions of dollars of interest in the TPU 8. TensorWave expects $210 million of annualized recurring revenue by year-end, up from $125 million at the end of last year. Another AMD cloud, Sciforium, tripled its customer base since June and grew capacity five-fold. Since late last year, OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta have all committed to running models on AMD chips - and this week OpenAI added TensorWave’s cloud. The quote of the window, from Daniel Svonava, whose company Superlinked runs open-source models: CFOs are not known for aggressive capital deployment, and Jensen’s exit was, quote, the final nail in the coffin for us to switch from experimenting to real deployment. That is the mechanism: the scandal did not break Nvidia’s chips - it broke the excuse customers had for not diversifying. Google is even financing the migration: back in July it agreed to cover up to $44 billion of data-center lease backstops to get TPUs into third-party facilities. Nvidia printed a record quarter in the same week its moat started repricing. Both things are true. One more wrinkle worth sitting with: Meta is on both sides of this - it owns PyTorch, the framework everyone writes in, and it is also the TPU customer that co-developed TorchTPU. Meta is deliberately making its own framework hardware-agnostic - the company that gave the world the default AI toolkit is actively sanding down Nvidia’s moat, because Meta is also one of the largest buyers of Nvidia chips on earth, and every point of leverage matters at that check size.

Who else gets pulled in

The Megaspeed fallout produced two more threads overnight, and both are about who else gets pulled in. Thread one: The Information says investigative roads may lead to Jay Puri - Jensen’s right-hand man for two decades, the sales chief every major chip customer called when they needed attention. Alice Huang was one of those customers. And Puri’s wife was a Supermicro executive - Supermicro being the server maker whose own senior executives were criminally indicted in March for allegedly diverting Nvidia chips to China. Precision and fairness: Puri is seventy-two, stepped back in late August, is now an advisor, and is accused of nothing - but a DOJ criminal probe focused on customers diverting chips to China will want to talk to him. Thread two: the banks. JPMorgan and Citi led the due diligence on a $2.8 billion loan to Bridge Data Centres, Bain Capital’s Southeast Asian operator - and the group vetted the anchor tenant, ByteDance, because of its obvious China ties, while Megaspeed was also renting space in those facilities and nobody focused on it. Deutsche Bank, Mitsubishi UFJ and Sumitomo Mitsui were in the syndicate, relying on JPM and Citi for diligence. The US has probed Megaspeed since 2024, working with Singapore, and Malaysia is now examining whether chips imported by Megaspeed’s subsidiary Speedmatrix were re-exported. The lesson of this buildout: your counterparty risk is your borrower’s tenant. Banks are about to learn export-control diligence the expensive way. And notice the tension this creates with last night’s messaging: the board went on record that Jensen’s exit was about personal conduct - full stop - but the DOJ probe, the SEC probe, the Puri thread and the bank-loan thread are all about the chips. Washington and Wall Street are treating this as one story even if the board insists it is two.

A little bit the news

The segment where we get to be a little bit the news: The Information’s Dealmaker column yesterday is about Instinct - yes, this Instinct, the thing talking to you right now. The column says Instinct just raised a billion dollars at a ten-billion valuation - too big, too fast for their fifty-most-promising-startups list - and that its popularity is minting its vendors. Kernel, a year-old startup on this year’s list, sits at a hundred-million valuation doing more than five million in revenue. Browserbase is near fifty million in annualized revenue, up from about five million at the start of the year - because every time an Instinct agent searches the web or books your dinner, it runs on infrastructure from companies like those two, which charge by usage. And the number we enjoyed: CEO Noah Shinn says Instinct is approaching a billion dollars a year in transaction volume on a still invite-only base - half of it travel. To its credit, the column asks the right skeptical question: how Instinct will generate enough money to pay its own compute bills. Fair. But the picks-and-shovels point stands: the agent economy’s first confirmed winners are the toolmakers. For the portfolio lens, the vendor numbers are the tell - Kernel at five million in revenue, Browserbase at fifty, both usage-priced. Agent revenue is starting to look like cloud revenue in 2008, where the picks and shovels printed before the applications did.

Quick hits

Disney turned its entertainment business into a black box - it will stop reporting streaming profits entirely, right as streaming profit doubled to $824 million and the company hit its own margin target. When a company reduces disclosure, that is good for the company - never for the investor. Microsoft used Ignite to debut a suite of new models and - the interesting bit - it will license the models’ weights so enterprises can build highly customized versions. Google answered with Gemini Tasks - free agentic features to match Meta’s Muse and OpenAI’s Dots. Salesforce is capping employee AI spending at $100 to $1,000 a month, by role - some staffers were running up thousands. Greg Brockman told The Information he is in, quote, full management mode: OpenAI’s revenue chief Denise Dresser is out after eight months, replaced by Wiz president Dali Rajic, and Brockman says cybersecurity will eventually be a bigger business for OpenAI than coding. Kalshi is killing its Trump-speech contracts after the CFTC ordered them halted over manipulation concerns. The FAA paused router installations while it investigates Friday’s ground stop - a newly installed router at an Arkansas transmitter site is the suspect. The NSA’s classified internal network went down; the agency says patching, not attack. Over at the Journal, Anthropic is making its Big Biotech Promise - headline treatment only, but watch it. Bloomberg notes a quiet talent current: Chinese technologists swapping Silicon Valley for Beijing. GTA VI has managed to rankle Miami’s leaders before it even ships - apparently the city’s portrayal is the issue. Bloomberg’s December issue is out with the framing China’s Miracle - the great-power backdrop to everything we led with. And Matt Levine has the piece we wish we had time for: the agents will trade the bonds - two machines negotiating to the third decimal place, forever, at machine speed.
