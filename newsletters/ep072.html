Follow the company, not the man

The Information’s curated timeline of the collapse is the weekend’s essential document, because the paper trail is the story. In 2015 a Singapore entity called 7Road International is incorporated, the local arm of a Shenzhen gaming company. March 2023 it renames itself Megaspeed; Alice Huang becomes managing director with no prior background in AI. Three months later Shanghai Century Litong is founded to rent out AI compute, one founding owner a company tied to Alice’s sister, and within months it is a preferred Nvidia cloud partner, a status that comes with priority access to chips. October 2023, two weeks after the US bans the A800 and H800 for China, Century Litong acquires large volumes of the banned chips still sitting in distributors’ warehouses; its clients become China’s biggest tech companies. November 2023, Megaspeed’s ownership shuffles from a British Virgin Islands entity named for Alice’s Chinese name to a Singapore company, and she formally steps down as managing director, though the Journal says she kept running it. Then the Malaysia layer: February 2024 Megaspeed sets up Speedmatrix, which from June receives more than 200 shipments of Nvidia hardware worth about $2 billion over the following year, much of it traced to a subsidiary of blacklisted server maker Inspur. September 2024, Commerce officials inspect a Megaspeed-linked Malaysian data center and find Nvidia gear still in its boxes. The personal timeline runs in parallel: a Taipei bar photo at Computex in June 2024, and AFP/Central News Agency footage of a May 2025 group dinner showing Alice kissing Jensen on the cheek before they leave in the same car, unremarked at the time. And the sentence that reframes everything: in August 2025, Nvidia’s own general counsel contacted Commerce investigators about a probe into Megaspeed, a probe that later moved to the Justice Department. The compliance function inside Nvidia saw this coming, said so to the government, and the machine kept selling anyway.

The Washington knife fight

The man who sat on that flag is now fighting for his job. The Wall Street Journal’s Saturday front page: Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick is under pressure from Trump aides who blame him for the Nvidia fallout, both for inaction on chip-export controls and for friction with other senior officials. And Treasury is on the record against him: the Journal reports Secretary Bessent says Treasury backed restrictions on Megaspeed and Commerce did not act. That is not anonymous whispering; it is one cabinet secretary pointing at another, by name, on the biggest economic-security failure of the decade. The Senate Banking Committee has asked both Huang and Lutnick to testify. Meanwhile Commerce is finalizing the long-awaited remote-access rule, and Axios has reported Lutnick stalled the executive order on the chip-diversion crackdown. The Information’s Abram Brown frames the week as a knife fight: some factions inside Trump-led Washington pretty obviously worked against Huang, most of the most active reporters this week are in Washington, and whether or not Alice Huang was truly a spy, and he notes more recent reports cast doubt on that, the mere implication was a useful weapon. His aside that Alex Karp is a Democrat again tells you which way the Valley’s political winds are blowing. Underneath sits a structural conflict: Commerce is charged with both promoting and restricting American technology abroad. Those mandates coexisted until a four-trillion-dollar company depended on the first and national security on the second. Translation for the portfolio: export enforcement is now a personnel story, and personnel stories move fast and break supply chains.

What Washington knew

Every word here is attribution. Per The Information’s timeline: in late 2025, President Trump was briefed on US intelligence findings that Alice Huang was a Chinese intelligence asset delivering updates to Beijing on her relationship with Jensen; he took no action. In spring 2026, a classified memo overseen by Secretary of State Rubio warned Alice had been recruited by Chinese intelligence to target Jensen. October 9, the New York Times first reports on Megaspeed; the US and Singapore confirm investigations; the Jensen connection draws little attention. October 29, the Times reports officials found a close relationship and a Chinese spy plot targeting him; Huang tells Bloomberg his dealings with Megaspeed were “completely normal.” November 5, three Congressional committee chairs ask him to attend a closed-door briefing on November 18. November 6, the FT reports officials believe the two had a romantic relationship; soon after, general counsel Tim Teter confronts him at his home and he confesses. November 9, the board hires Wachtell Lipton, the investigation led by Joon Kim, a former acting US attorney who investigated the Cuomo scandal; the same day, Trump says Jensen denied knowing Alice was a spy, calls him “a very honorable man,” and mentions an FBI investigation. Two days later Huang was out of all three of his jobs. The honest summary: an intelligence assessment existed for at least a year, the president knew, the board acted on the affairs rather than the spy question, and the espionage case remains publicly unproven and, per some recent reports, contested. Interpretations are interpretations. What is documented is the sequence.

The wreckage and the defense

The ugly first: Reuters reports a third affair, with another Nvidia employee, beyond Alice and Kimberly Powell. The Journal reports Huang belittled Colette Kress, the executive now replacing him, before his ouster, rhyming with The Information’s reporting that he steamrolled her on risk. Stephen Witt, Huang’s 2025 biographer, writes in the New York Times that he was energized by “arrogance and toxic self-regard,” and resurrects Dawn, early Nvidia’s “underdressed, large-breasted C.G.I. pixie” mascot, memory-holed in 2020 around the time Huang began talking about empowering women; Witt muses about a sequel, “The What-Were-You-Thinking?! Machine.” The defense is not nothing: Vinod Khosla, Ron Conway and Epic’s Tim Sweeney have stood up for him publicly, Conway urging patience for the investigations, and Thursday evening a petition from Nvidia employees circulated online advocating reinstatement. Sit with that: after everything, part of the rank and file wants him back. The Journal’s weekend essay captures the whiplash: they were the indispensable CEOs, until they weren’t; the opinion page is blunter: of course he had to go. Bloomberg’s weekend piece asks the grown-up question: how do you save the next Jensen Huang from himself, because with 3.5% ownership and no supervoting shares there was no structural brake, and the board that finally acted had no chair and decades of friendship with the man. Governance is the brake you install before the crash, not after.

The machine Lutnick runs

The deep dive: export controls, first principles. The system rests on the Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security entity list; land on it and US companies need a presumptively denied license to sell you controlled technology. Inspur is on it, yet $2 billion of Nvidia hardware reached Speedmatrix in Malaysia, much of it traced to an Inspur subsidiary, because the entity list names entities, not behaviors. You cannot buy as Inspur, but a fresh Singapore or Malaysian shell with a gaming-company history buys fine, and by the time investigators map the network the chips are racked and training. A second instrument, the foreign direct product rule, lets Washington claim jurisdiction over chips made anywhere on earth if US technology made them; powerful on paper, slow in practice, because you must still find the chips, name the buyer and prove the chain, and licensing moves at bureaucratic speed while a shell incorporates in an afternoon. Failure one: the list is static, the shells are not. Failure two is geographic: the bans restrict chips going to China, so the industry built data centers next door; Malaysia has power, land, submarine cables and a one-hour flight to Shenzhen, and when Commerce inspectors physically found boxed Nvidia gear in a Megaspeed-linked facility in September 2024, nothing happened, because inspection is not interdiction. Failure three is the one the new rule attacks: even without a chip crossing a border, a Chinese lab can rent compute on Blackwells sitting in Kuala Lumpur. The chips stay legal, the model gets trained, the control achieves nothing. Three failure modes: a static list, a movable warehouse, a borderless customer.

Failure four is political

When Treasury backed putting Megaspeed on the restriction list, Commerce did not act, per Bessent, and per Axios the crackdown executive order sat stalled. Every enforcement gap above is a choice someone made, and the Senate Banking Committee now wants to ask both men about those choices. The Hill is not waiting: a tougher export-control bill has passed the House and could ride the defense authorization bill in coming weeks, and Singapore has barred Alice Huang after concluding she gave inaccurate information in its Megaspeed investigation. Three things to watch. One, the remote-access rule’s final text: does it cover rental by subsidiaries and shells, or only named Chinese entities, because the Megaspeed lesson is that the named-entity approach loses. Two, whether Commerce gets seizure or blocking authority for the Malaysia-pattern diversions, because inspections without consequences are tourism. Three, Nvidia’s own exposure: its general counsel turned whistleblower-in-house in August 2025, and the $7.5 billion, 240,000-chips figure is the scale of what flowed. Tuesday’s earnings call is now a compliance event as much as a financial one: Kress faces analysts twenty-four hours before her predecessor testifies, and the circuit-breaker question is whether guidance assumes anything about enforcement tightening into 2027 orders. The chips are sold out through 2028, but sold out to whom, under which rules, is the number inside the number. And Bloomberg has the largest IPO in history pricing this same week, book six times covered, straight into the crossfire: the rotation trade is now literal, with managers saying they will sell AI infrastructure names to fund their Anthropic allocation.

A palace coup with superheroes

DC Studios co-CEOs James Gunn and Peter Safran are out December 1, replaced by former Marvel executive Jeremy Latcham, a producer on The Avengers and Spider-Man: Homecoming, which is a strange choice if your goal is escaping the cinematic-universe playbook, because Latcham is the cinematic-universe playbook. The weekend’s most instructive entertainment number comes from Kyoto: Nintendo moved 5.2 million copies of a Zelda remake in four days. A twenty-eight-year-old game, remade, outselling most new universes. Catalog with love beats universe with debt. Bloomberg Opinion’s bear case: Skydance carries roughly $80 billion of net debt from the Paramount deal, and the shared-universe model is the most capital-intensive way to make movies, a decade-long chain of $200 million bets where each weak link taxes every sequel after it. The counterargument: Gunn and Safran had just been elevated onto Skydance’s leadership team post-merger and Gunn’s Superman relaunch was the one working asset, so this reads less like strategy than politics. And the bull case is one sentence: a good movie is always in fashion. For the VC lens, it is the founder-control episode in another costume: new owners, legacy leadership, an $80 billion debt load, and a creative bet that takes a decade to pay off. The debt doesn’t care about your ten-year plan.
