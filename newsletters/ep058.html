The robot Meta cannot sell

The Information’s exclusive is the best kind: a fact pattern that tells you the strategy. Meta built a humanoid called Max with Fourier, a Chinese robotics firm, in three months. It is 1.5 meters tall, 106 pounds, runs Nvidia’s Jetson Thor, and was specced like a houseguest - recognize a voice at five meters, read a wave at three, hold eye contact, and visibly signal listening, thinking, speaking and uncertainty. A robot that can look unsure is a design philosophy, not a feature. Version two started in September. The business reading is in the margins: robotics chief Marc Whitten left in August after 18 months, Bosworth is personally in the meetings, the humanoid is described internally as the physical embodiment of the Muse agent, and Meta is hiring in Shanghai and Shenzhen for “prototype through mass production.” Then the constraint that makes it a story: the FCC barred new Chinese humanoid imports in July, the research exemption allows 4,000 units, and an August proposal would cut that to 40. Meta’s statement - “no plans to sell these units” - is the legal architecture. The prototype is legal precisely because it is not a product. Meta is learning Chinese manufacturing at the moment US policy is designed to stop the transfer. Something gives, and it is more likely the robots than the policy.

The paper-fortune tax

California votes Tuesday on Proposition 40, a one-time 5% tax on billion-dollar fortunes for healthcare and education. The politics have flipped: strong support earlier this year is now a Politico 45-43 split, with prediction markets at ~23% for passage. The opposition coalition is the tell - Planned Parenthood, the California Teachers Association and the California Medical Association, all Democratic-aligned, all opposed, partly because they doubt Sacramento will deliver the money as promised. Newsom opposes. The tech elite are in open revolt (Parker Conrad on X); the donor-class reputation problem is captured in one source’s line about contacts with ordinary people being “the conversations they have with staff on the way to their Porsche.” The mechanism question is the one that matters for founders: a 5% levy on illiquid equity means selling into your own float or borrowing against paper, at the market’s choice of moment, not yours. Nothing is ever one-time; TI’s source expects copycat measures in other states. If a wealth tax fails in California during a blue wave, the ceiling is located.

Paywalled for readers: The Information Weekend, The Billionaire Tax Might Fail - for Now, Oct 31 (full text of email read).

Aftermarket autonomy

Page one of Saturday’s Journal: a $1,000 device enabling hands-free driving has drawn scrutiny after fatal crashes. (Labeled: session access unavailable, headline-level.) The frame matters more than the details we cannot yet read. Aftermarket autonomy is not a car company shipping a supervised system with validation fleets and a recall process; it is a gadget with a price point that puts it in reach of everyone, and the liability question - driver, maker, or regulator - has no clean answer. The pattern is the same one this year’s AI tooling keeps teaching: capability gets cheap before accountability does. Fatal crashes turn gadget stories into liability stories, and liability stories into regulation.

The margin logic of DoorDash-Instacart

Bloomberg Businessweek’s weekend letter supplies the economics under Thursday’s $17B deal (labeled - CAPTCHA-walled). Delivery got more expensive, not cheaper, with scale: inflation, gig wages, and investor pressure for revenue. The two companies diverged instructively - DoorDash turned a profit by adding categories and raising prices (the scale playbook), while Instacart grew slower but carries better margins, built on software for grocers and advertising for brands (the margin playbook). The deal is the two playbooks combined, plus a grocery defense against Amazon and Walmart. The $17B price looked like a bargain against the $40B talks of five years ago; the margin structure is the other half of that argument. Marketplaces with software margins get bought at premiums. Marketplaces without them get bought at discounts.

Bloomberg Businessweek Daily, Oct 31 (newsletter text, labeled - CAPTCHA-walled).

The authenticity tax

Also from Bloomberg’s weekend letter (labeled): people are rewriting their own prose to sound less like AI, because detectors like Pangram have gotten good enough that natural human writing now fails the test. Humans are deliberately adding quirks and breaking rhythms to pass as human. We taught machines to write like people; people now pay a tax to prove they are not machines. The uncanny valley has a toll booth. Somewhere, a model is being trained on the broken-rhythm prose of humans pretending to be humans, and the loop closes.
