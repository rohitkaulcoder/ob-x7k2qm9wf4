The fully covered deal

Marketing for the Anthropic IPO kicked off Tuesday morning, and by day’s end the deal was fully covered: orders for every available share, at launch. The filing targets a valuation of $1.26 to $1.4 trillion, raising as much as $90.4 billion across the IPO and a parallel private placement, with pricing next week and the FT reporting the final price could clear the range. The most read-worthy artifact of the day is the amended prospectus filed on day one of marketing: after the September executive order renaming artificial intelligence “super intelligence,” Anthropic now defines SI, declares the terms interchangeable, and halfheartedly search-and-replaces some but not all occurrences. The cover page still says AI. Page six says super intelligence. The lawyers complied in the most literal way available, which is its own kind of protest.

Why a share is worth more to Google than to you

The mechanics of this IPO are the story, and Matt Levine lays them out cleanly. Anthropic has committed to spend at least $121 billion with Google Cloud through July 2033, regardless of usage, plus up to $150 billion more on TPU capacity; at least $110 billion with AWS through 2036; $49 billion with Microsoft; up to $120.5 billion with SpaceX. A strategic investor’s dollar therefore comes back as revenue, with the stock attached. Yours just gets the stock. Venture capitalist Apoorv Agrawal asked the follow-up in 2024: if a strategic dollar buys a dollar of stock plus a dollar of revenue, what does the valuation even measure? Now the structure: Google and friends have committed to buy $26 billion of stock at whatever price the IPO clears. No price sensitivity, no fees, and no practical ability to sell for about six months since the shares are unregistered. Roughly a quarter of the deal is spoken for by price-insensitive long-term holders before the roadshow begins. Constrained supply, guaranteed demand. The payoffs: Amazon’s theoretical 20% stake, self-capped at 12%, is worth about $258 billion at the midpoint on $18.6 billion invested; Google’s 10% is worth $132 billion on $33 billion. Neither holds voting shares, which keeps the antitrust cops home. All seven co-founders are selling $1.75 billion between them and end up worth about $18 billion each. The honest caveat is Levine’s: $26 billion is small against Anthropic’s future compute needs, so the effect fades as the company grows. For pricing week, though, it is a dream cap table.

The twelve percent gap

OpenAI’s last private round: $1.25 trillion. Anthropic’s ask of public investors: only about 12% more at the top of the range, despite generating roughly 70% more revenue in the third quarter and growing faster all year. Anthropic’s Q3 revenue was nearly $17 billion, up 47% sequentially, with an annualized pace of $81 billion in October, six times February’s. OpenAI told investors Tuesday its Q3 revenue jumped nearly 50% to $10 billion, 2.6 times the year-ago quarter, with enterprise now the majority of revenue; its consumer side remains vast and mostly free (1.2 billion weekly users, nearly 100 million paying, ads at a $1 billion annualized pace). The divergence that matters is on the other line: OpenAI’s operating loss widened to $10.9 billion while Anthropic’s was $567 million, and that included a $2 billion employee donation match. One company is within sight of breakeven; the other is losing eleven billion a quarter. Anthropic’s advisers are guiding low to guarantee the pop. The referendum arrives next week, with OpenAI’s own $9 billion second installment still to fill in the background.

The back door with a preferred-partner badge

Day three of the Huang saga brought the most damaging reporting yet. The Information reconstructs how Alice Huang (Chinese name Huang Le), her sister Huang Yu and her husband Kang Wei built a network of companies across China and Singapore that facilitated at least $7.5 billion of Nvidia chip shipments to Chinese companies since 2023. The centerpiece, Shanghai Century Litong, was founded in June 2023 and landed on Nvidia’s preferred cloud partner list by October, two weeks after Washington banned the A800 and H800. Preferred status meant priority allocation of the chips already sitting in distributor warehouses. Of the 240,000 banned chips she allegedly helped move, mostly H100s and H200s, some ended up serving Alibaba, Tencent and smaller Chinese AI labs. The paper trail is the tell: she ran Megaspeed as managing director through a BVI entity called Huang Le Limited, then moved ownership to another Singapore vehicle in November 2023; Megaspeed itself was the renamed Singapore arm of a Shenzhen gaming company. Century Litong denies any dealings with her; Nvidia cites its compliance record and supported prosecutions. Accountability is now institutional: the board hired Wachtell, the FT reports Commerce delayed blacklisting Megaspeed, the NYT says Rubio told Trump during the Beijing trip, and a closed-door congressional briefing lands next week. Nvidia fell for a second day. None of this is a finding; all of it is now under investigation by people with subpoena power.

The format problem

Google’s search advertising grew 12% in Q3, down from 17%, and the stock fell nearly 13% on October 29. The $224.5 billion business has its first real crack, and it is a format problem, not a demand problem. Ads in AI Overviews and AI Mode run alongside conversations, not keywords; advertisers can neither target nor exclude the AI placements; AI Mode queries run three times longer than classic searches. Six advertisers and agencies told The Information they are moving budget to Meta, whose ad revenue is growing more than twice as fast, while OpenAI enters the market. The bull case is the Reels precedent: Meta ate a monetization dip in 2022 and recovered within a year. The bear case is that Reels kept users inside the app, and AI Mode’s job is to answer without a click. Google is striking data partnerships and exploring AI creative-tool acquisitions while it relearns its own business.

Tokens are the new headcount

The enterprise has split into two camps on AI spending. No caps: Amazon, Palantir, Snowflake, HubSpot, and the frontier labs. HubSpot went from 15% to over 90% engineer adoption of its coding tool this year and calls cost a non-issue against the productivity. Bain Capital Ventures and S3 Ventures report no caps across their portfolios; S3’s partner notes portfolio companies are slowing hiring, and against the cost of one more engineer, heavy AI spend is not a big deal. The cutters: Apple ($300 to $200 a day), Microsoft (monthly budgets down a tenth), Disney (requests paused), Walmart, and Blackstone ($5,000 a month for power users, $250 for light). Job candidates now negotiate token budgets in offer letters. The optimization corner shows where this settles: AWS routes each request to the cheapest capable model, cutting some engineering costs 40%, and Booz Allen switched its defense and cyber workloads from Anthropic to Nvidia’s Nemotron while still building with Claude Code. On OpenRouter, Anthropic takes 31% of spend and Nvidia under 1%; on coding-focused Kilo Code, Nemotron doubled to 17% of tokens in a month. The model is becoming a commodity input, purchased like compute.

Quick hits

October CPI came in hot, the ten-year hit 4.99%, and Fed officials are talking about raising, not pausing: Lorie Logan sees at least two quarter-point increases and possibly a faster pace; Waller is open to a jumbo bump. Every basis point lands on the data center buildout’s rate-sensitive debt. CoreWeave beat and raised, but the tell is the funding model: prepayments on 70% of deals, up to 100% of contract value upfront, and no unsecured debt at current yields, eight months after bragging about reducing prepayment dependency. SoftBank wrote off $3.7 billion of non-refundable equipment advances. In deals: Nidus raised $30 million (Costanoa pre-seed, Kleiner Perkins seed) for defense-manufacturing robotics, starting with wire harnesses, past $1 million in bookings in four months, betting general robotics labs will not bother with unglamorous precision work. OpenAI acquired AI travel planner Odessia. Ring shipped its $250 flying camera, six years late. IBM is ending immigration sponsorship for some roles in its largest division.
