We said it wasn’t a story. It was the story.

An admission first. On Saturday we called the Jensen Huang restoration campaign a fan club - the signature counts, the open letters, the bylaw math all said it could not happen. Yesterday morning, eleven days after Nvidia’s board forced him out over what it called personal behavior that violated the company’s code of conduct, that same board voted unanimously to reinstate him as president and CEO. The mechanics, per The Information’s reconstruction: over the weekend, Attorney General Todd Blanche told President Trump the Justice Department had found no evidence of wrongdoing by Huang; at Trump’s direction, Vice President Vance relayed that directly to the board; the board then announced it had assurances from “senior levels of the United States government” that the reinstatement poses no national security risk. Trump posted his approval within minutes and told CNBC, “I spoke to them, let me put it that way. And I convinced them.” The White House added: “We have to protect our geniuses.” A sitting president personally brokered the rehiring of a private company’s CEO and boasted about it on television. There is no modern precedent - the historical parallels all run the other way.

Why the president cared

The motive stack is worth unpacking, because it maps how power flows now. The Information reports Trump and Vance saw Huang as a strategic counterweight - the man keeping open-source AI models alive so Anthropic and OpenAI “can’t have a monopoly.” The President of the United States intervened in a boardroom, in part, over the competitive structure of the frontier model market. Behind that: a week of furious lobbying by tech CEOs including Musk, arguing Trump had lost control of forces inside his own administration. The administration had genuinely split - Bessent publicly demanded an outside replacement on November 15, then by Sunday was asking “where the buck stops” and calling the Alice Huang angle “kind of a canard” - a chaos The Information traces partly to chief of staff Susie Wiles’ November 5 departure. And the market was screaming: $2.4 trillion shed from the early-October peak to Friday’s low, with Friday’s selloff dragging the whole AI complex. Monday’s reversal added back nearly a trillion dollars in one session; the S&P 500 rose almost 5%. Trump, who owns Nvidia stock personally, decided the cost of Huang’s absence exceeded the cost of his return.

The governance debt

Nothing about Huang’s conduct changed in the six days between the board ruling out his return and voting him back in. What changed was the stock price and the phone calls. Bloomberg reports the board decided without consulting the company’s own management. Senator Ricketts, a Republican, noted a board that described Huang as lacking candor cannot logically reinstate him; Yale’s Sonnenfeld called it a complete degradation of corporate governance and said the board should resign. Hold onto the load-bearing fact: none of the investigations have gone away. The board’s own Wachtell Lipton probe continues. The SEC is reportedly investigating Nvidia’s China-sales accounting. The DOJ’s criminal probe into chip diversion continues. Huang still testifies behind closed doors to House committees in early December, and publicly before Senate Banking on December 3. Matt Levine framed the absurdity: the funniest outcome is the investigation finds more bad conduct and the board fires him again in a month. That probability is not zero.

Riemann, verified

Friday we told you an Anthropic mathematician, Levent Alpöge, claimed Claude had helped him prove the Riemann Hypothesis - and that the claim needed independent verification. It got it. On Saturday, two independent groups, the Lean Focused Research Organization and the Mathlib Initiative, formally verified the proof as correct. And this is not two professors skimming a PDF: Lean is a proof assistant that checks every logical step mechanically, the way a compiler checks code. The 167-year-old conjecture about the hidden pattern in the primes now has a machine-verified proof that a frontier AI model materially helped produce. The build-up matters: Anthropic made progress on a related problem in August; OpenAI solved a weaker version in October. In the same week, METR reported frontier models’ task horizons are still doubling every 3.6 months, and OpenAI’s chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, publicly called for industry-wide development limits until monitoring catches up. When the people building the models ask for a speed limit in the same week the models prove Riemann, pay attention. Anthropic’s stock slipped about 1.5% Monday to $87.64, roughly $1.6 trillion fully diluted.

The AMD premium

Anita Ramaswamy’s valuation check on AMD: up 98% this year even after Friday’s rout, trading at roughly 25x forward EBITDA as of Friday - against Nvidia at 7x and Broadcom, with better gross margins than Nvidia, at 14x. The market is paying a premium for the number-two player on the theory that Nvidia’s turmoil hands it share. The bull case is real - server CPU revenue expected to roughly double next year as agentic workloads demand more general-purpose compute per AI chip; the OpenAI and Meta deals are genuine volume. The bear case is the balance sheet: roughly $28 billion in credit guarantees and lease support to customers, plus $48.8 billion in supplier purchase commitments due by end of next year, against $6.5 billion of excess cash. Short-seller Hunterbrook noted AMD’s China ties were not that different from Nvidia’s - 22% of revenue last year - which took 8% off the stock Thursday before Friday’s 13% slide. Even KeyBanc’s bull warns any moderate downtick in expectations carries substantial risk at these levels. The same circular-financing exposure as the rest of the ecosystem, concentrated in a smaller company at a richer multiple.

Drilling for certainty

The power crunch thread from yesterday’s Amazon story continues underground. Tech companies now hold contracts for 865 megawatts of next-generation geothermal - more than utilities and all other buyers combined. Last week Google signed a $500 million deal with Quaise Energy for 50 megawatts, drilled three miles into hot rock under central Oregon. The logic is yesterday’s logic: data centers need power around the clock, the grid queue runs past five years, and even gas has shortage warnings for 2028. Enhanced geothermal borrows horizontal drilling from shale to crack hot rock almost anywhere. Fervo is the bellwether - two-thirds of contracted next-gen capacity, drilling time per well cut from 70 days in 2022 to 21, yet just 36 megawatts operating and a stock down nearly 70% since May. Strategic value and shareholder value are different things. The economics remain brutal: contracts above $100 per megawatt-hour against $75 for wind and $60 for solar. What the premium buys is uptime near 90%, day and night.

Quick hits and what to watch

Palo Alto Networks is buying Applied Compute - seventeen months old, last valued at $3.25 billion - for $4.5 billion in stock, a bet that securing the inference layer is a business after the Fireworks hack. Nvidia, mid-turmoil, is in advanced talks to acquire language-model startup Inception for at least $500 million. Oracle says it has secured all the funding for its OpenAI contract, roughly $20 billion in fresh equity and debt. Databricks is in early talks to raise several billion at around $225 billion. Simplexity Robotics raised $100 million at $1.6 billion in a pre-Series A led by Ant Group; Pragmatik Labs, from Alibaba’s former AI chief, raised at $5 billion. Nvidia halted all H200 sales into China this week - the backdrop for the December testimony. In Asia: Nothing launched its first device designed for the AI era overnight, worth watching in India, one of its strongest markets.
