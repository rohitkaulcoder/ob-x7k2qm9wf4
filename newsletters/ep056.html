The glass turns

Two days, two mega-cap reports, one message. Alphabet had its worst day ever as a public company on Thursday: down 12-13%, half a trillion dollars of market value gone, because Google Services missed and search grew just 12% even as the cloud doubled. Hours later Amazon reported AWS up 39% to $45.98B, its fastest growth in nearly four years, with Jassy forecasting 40%+ through 2028 and musing about a trillion-dollar run-rate, plus total sales of $201.9B and capex guidance held at $220B. The stock fell 6% anyway. Nothing about the businesses changed this week. What changed is the lens: the market stopped rewarding AI spend and started discounting it, because nobody produced a proof point that AI revenue is outrunning AI depreciation. This is a repricing of certainty, not of growth, and it transmits through debt: the whole buildout is financed on the assumption that capital stays cheap. When the safest cash flows in tech get marked down for opacity, everything further out the risk curve gets repriced by formula. The Information’s Wingfield has the line: the AI glass was half full until Wednesday. It is now half empty.

Nvidia, lender of last resort

Nvidia is in talks to provide billions in credit guarantees to Nscale for the debt on a 460MW West Virginia data center that Anthropic has committed to rent for $45B. The guarantee, likely north of $5B, is structured as a residual value guarantee on the building: Nvidia promises the lenders the shell will be worth something, so an unproven startup can borrow investment-grade. If Nscale defaults, Nvidia takes the facility and re-lets it. This is the escalation of a pattern: $170B of accumulated credit support and investment across the ecosystem, a team co-led by Jensen Huang’s son-in-law scouting power sites, a revenue-reporting change to spotlight neoclouds, and talks to invest in Firmus after its IPO was pulled. The logic is defensive. Nvidia’s biggest customers are all making their own chips, so the neoclouds are demand insurance, and Anthropic, the rare lab that spreads across chip vendors, is worth keeping close. Vendor financing was round one. This is a central bank.

Deals before the midterms

DoorDash bought Instacart for about $17B, five years after walking away from talks above $40B over regulatory fears. Waiting saved $23B and bought a healthy business: Q3 sales up 17%, $300M+ of cash generated, rudderless since Fidji Simo left for OpenAI. The same day, Josh Kushner’s new Thrive Holdings bought ADT for $7.6B on the AI-spruce-up thesis. The politics are doing as much work as the strategy: with Democrats favored to retake Congress next week and Mayor Mamdani already attacking the Instacart deal, bankers expect a rush to close while reviews stay friendly, and the discount rack behind these two deals is long - Zoom, Duolingo, Coursera, and Bumble, already gone at $6 a share against a $75 history. When Goldman’s co-head of banking says CEOs are leaning in, believe the pipeline.

How a law firm dies, and who buys the body

Weil, Gotshal & Manges is winding down, and Levine’s Money Stuff explains the mechanics of how a profitable law firm collapses: it is a bank run in slow motion. Operating leverage means a $50M rainmaker generates $42M of profit but keeps perhaps $15M; a rival offers $30M; she leaves; fixed costs spread over less revenue make the next partner’s calculus worse; the first out take their clients, the last hold the rent. Morley’s research adds the selection bias - the first to leave have the best options, so the pool deteriorates, so the run accelerates. Weil was profitable when it died; the September defection of Michael Aiello’s team to Cravath was the trigger. Then the modern coda: Crosby, a two-year-old legal-AI startup (50+ attorneys supervising AI-assisted work, $80M raised at a $400M valuation), has bid for Weil’s internal data, trademark and brand, hoping to hire from its 1,200 lawyers. The data is the prize: merger templates and negotiation histories are fine-tuning gold, and there is now an established market in corporate corpses - Google, Mercor and Micro1 bid for Spirit Airlines’ internals. The same week, White & Case admitted it is holding back Legora’s newest agent because its lawyers hesitate. The incumbents cannot adopt the disruptor fast enough, so the disruptor buys the brand at auction.
