Ep 13: Compute Needs a Down Payment

Overnight Brief, written edition · Thu, Sep 17, 2026 · audio: 17:26
🎧 Listen to the audio episode

Customer financing, with GPUs

The Fed raised rates for the first time in three years. The technology story is that AI compute now needs a down payment.

Iren says recent customers prepaid as much as 55% of GPUs and related equipment. At Nebius, roughly 70% of new deals required money up front. It expects more than $9B of prepayments this year against projected revenue of no more than $3.4B. This is not a subscription. It is customer financing.

A cloud company reserves power, networking and accelerators before the first token is sold. Lenders ask who will use the cluster and whether they will pay. About $3.6B of Iren GPU financing tied to Microsoft cost roughly 6%. Another $2.4B for non-investment-grade customers cost about three points more. The lender underwrites the tenant as much as the chips.

Higher rates widen the divide. Big Tech fixed debt costs earlier. Young neoclouds need more equity, larger deposits or stronger guarantees. The old playbook said growth first, infrastructure later. AI asks for the infrastructure deposit before kickoff.

Venture becomes working capital

Prepayments transfer risk from provider to customer. A 55% deposit on a $100M cluster means a startup wires $55M before earning the revenue that justifies it. The provider needs less debt. The startup receives capacity and loses optionality. A newer chip or a weaker usage curve arrives after the decision is funded.

The Fluidstack structure shows how many balance sheets can sit behind one rack. A Coatue-linked vehicle raised $5.7B to buy land and build an Indiana campus. Fluidstack will lease it. Google guarantees Fluidstack’s obligations. The package does not cover the chips.

AI looks like software in the demo and project finance in the footnotes.

For investors, three questions move up the list. What share of the round becomes a deposit? What happens if utilisation misses plan by 30 points? Who owns the hardware’s residual value? “We will grow into it” is not a software answer. It is a speculative lease.

Private credit moves earlier

Apollo is investing equity in AI and hardware startups because the larger opportunity comes later: debt, asset-backed loans and structured finance. It put a low tens-of-millions cheque into Mercor’s $20B-valuation round and has backed SiFive and Hadrian.

The equity is a relationship ticket. Hardware companies need factories, inventory and chips before revenue. Apollo can arrange financing far larger than the venture cheque.

The analogy is aircraft leasing, with one problem. GPUs depreciate economically faster than planes. A new generation may produce far more tokens per watt while the old chip still works. Lenders therefore care about lease length, customer credit, manufacturer support and secondary markets. A Microsoft contract can make a GPU bankable. An uncommitted rack is a commodity bet.

This is not private equity becoming venture capital. It is private credit moving earlier so it can shape the assets it will later finance. The cheque buys information. The loan earns the spread.

Apple borrows the road

Apple is considering enterprise servers for 2029 using two or four M8 Ultra chips and possibly NVIDIA’s NVLink Fusion. Apple would supply the compute. NVIDIA would supply the road between chips.

Apple silicon combines compute with large pools of unified memory. This is useful for inference when model size is the constraint. But several chips must exchange activations and cached context with high bandwidth and low latency. Apple’s internal links are reportedly too slow and costly at scale. NVLink Fusion packages switches, chiplets and software so rival accelerators can use NVIDIA’s fabric.

Networking is 10% to 15% of AI data-centre hardware cost and accounted for one-fifth of NVIDIA’s data-centre revenue in its last reported networking quarter. The hedge is clean. If custom chips win, NVIDIA taxes communication.

Apple has demand. AI labs have bought tens of thousands of Mac minis and Studios. Mac revenue rose nearly 29% to $10.4B last quarter. The company already operates Private Cloud Compute servers. It is considering productising demand that arrived without permission.

The enterprise trap

Four chips in a box do not make an enterprise business. Apple abandoned Xserve because customers need road maps, service contracts, integration support and someone to answer when the rack fails. Apple lacks a large enterprise server organisation today.

NVIDIA’s moat is also CUDA. Apple has MLX, but the team is small and its leader left for Anthropic. A 2029 product needs components reserved years ahead, mature tooling and a government-grade support channel.

The logic still works. Privacy-sensitive buyers want on-premise inference. Apple can offer unified memory and an integrated box while borrowing the missing network. NVIDIA earns revenue even when it loses the accelerator socket.

The profit pool goes to the component that remains necessary when the rest changes.

Zipline prices the network

Zipline is discussing a $1B round at roughly $20B, more than twice its prior $7.6B post-money value. It began delivering blood in Rwanda because US rules restricted flights beyond a pilot’s sight. The regulatory constraint pushed the company to a market where need and permission aligned.

The FAA opened commercial beyond-visual-line-of-sight delivery in 2023. Zipline expanded into food and retail. Its Uber partnership plans Dallas and Houston launches and targets one million daily drone deliveries by the end of 2029.

That target is the valuation bridge. At scale, Zipline is not a drone maker. It is a network of aircraft, launch sites, maintenance, routing and regulatory approval. Unit economics depend on density. More orders spread fixed local infrastructure across more flights.

Uber supplies demand without Zipline acquiring each consumer. It also gains bargaining power. Owning the aircraft is useful. Owning the order flow may be more useful.

Cost of capital per token

The day is a set of down payments. Startups prepay for compute. Apollo writes equity to earn lending relationships. Apple considers component commitments years before shipment. Zipline raises now for a network it hopes will fly a million times a day.

The decisive metric is no longer only cost per token. It is cost of capital per usable token. A strong cluster financed at 9% and half utilised can lose to an ordinary cluster financed at 6% and full. The interest clock runs while the rack is idle.

Founders now need capital choreography. Who prepays. Who guarantees. Who owns the asset. Who takes residual risk. How quickly fixed cost becomes revenue.

The financing architecture is becoming part of the product architecture.
