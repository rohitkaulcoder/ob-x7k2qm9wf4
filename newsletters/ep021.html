Ep 21: Memory You Can't Rewrite

A recipe book you never rewrite

An AI model answers in two phases. Prefill reads the whole prompt in parallel and is limited by arithmetic. Decode writes one token at a time, and for every token it rereads the model's weights and the KV cache, the model's notes on the conversation so far. Decode is limited by memory. The GPU waits.

Both things it rereads are growing. Kimi K3 has 2.8 trillion parameters, about 2.8TB at 8 bits. DeepSeek V3 was 671GB. Long-running agents fill million-token contexts and swell the KV cache.

HBM, the fast memory beside the GPU, is built by stacking DRAM dies and running vertical wires through them. The wires are finite. Stack taller and each gigabyte gets a smaller share of bandwidth. Capacity costs speed.

High Bandwidth Flash applies the same stacking to NAND flash. One HBF stack holds about 512GB against roughly 36GB for HBM4, with headline bandwidth up to 3TB/s. Asianometry's example: a GPU with eight 24GB HBM stacks has 192GB. Swap six for HBF and it has about 3.1TB.

The catch is that flash is not DRAM. It is roughly a hundred times slower to read and write, it wears out after thousands of writes, and it runs hot. So it suits data that is read constantly and changed rarely. That is a description of model weights. The design writes itself: working notes in HBM, the frozen recipe book in HBF.

Source: Asianometry, High Bandwidth Flash Is Coming, Sep 24, 2026 (member)

The ghost of Optane

This has been tried. Intel's Optane sat between DRAM and flash and was pitched for the same "warm data". The memory cells worked. The product died: it needed software rewritten for it, cheap 3D NAND undercut it, and it had one supplier.

HBF's CTO at Sandisk ran Intel's Optane group. He knows where the bodies are buried. Two things differ this time. There is a public spec, with SK hynix as a second source, Google and Tenstorrent in the consortium and Samsung building its own version. And inference software is written by a few highly motivated companies rather than thousands of database vendors.

But cheap capacity is not cheap tokens. If the GPU waits on flash, output falls. Mixture-of-experts models, which store many experts and read few, may be the natural fit. Samples arrive in late 2027 and volume in 2029. The cost of serving large models will fall in steps tied to hardware like this. Plan for the curve. Do not bet on the date.

Control without ownership

Anthropic is asking shareholders to approve a share class giving its seven cofounders 50.1% of voting power on most matters, modelled on Palantir. The shares carry no extra economics. Dario Amodei reportedly owns about 2%.

The Long-Term Benefit Trust, a group of non-shareholders, keeps the right to appoint most of the board. Founders go from two seats to three. Employees get tie-breaking stock. Anthropic was valued at $965B in May and may seek $1.5T in an IPO now likely in late October or November.

This is not really a share structure. It is a constitution. Public investors supply capital and get economics. Founders with small stakes get votes. Outsiders pick the board. Employees break ties. The unanswered question is who removes the founders if they are wrong. At this size, index funds will buy anyway. Founder control is being normalised at mega-scale, and Indian founders heading to IPO will notice.

Source: The Information, Sep 24, 2026 (subscriber)

Hiring a novelist to tick boxes

TypeSafe AI, maker of the Jev model, is reportedly fielding offers at $10B or more a week after raising at about $200M. Jev outputs numbers and confidence estimates rather than sentences. TypeSafe claims up to 200 times the speed of leading models at a four-hundredth of the cost. Atlassian uses it for classification, including tasks it never automated because an LLM was too slow and expensive.

Fifty times in a week is a stampede, not price discovery. The idea underneath is sound. Most business work is deciding, not writing. A cheap, fast, confident classifier makes new tasks worth automating. Classification is also where moats are thinnest. For cost-sensitive Indian enterprises, price is the product.

Source: The Information, Dealmaker, Sep 24, 2026

The old loans are hedged. The new ones are not.

GPU buyers borrow through delayed draw term loans: a committed credit line drawn as chips are bought, with interest only on what is drawn. Much of it floats on SOFR, 3.87% on September 23, plus spreads of roughly 2.25 to 5.5 points. At 3.9 plus 4, a one-point rise in SOFR lifts the floating interest bill by about an eighth.

So lenders require swaps that turn floating into fixed. CoreWeave's $8.5B facility must hedge at least 95% of anticipated floating borrowing. Nscale's $1.85B Texas loan requires 90-110%.

The risk is not a blowup. It is that every new facility is priced in today's rates, with AI debt spreads already wider. That lands hardest on neoclouds without a creditworthy customer. Oracle reportedly sending a force majeure notice to the Blue Owl unit building its New Mexico site is what pressure in the chain looks like.

Sources: The Information, Finance newsletter, Sep 24, 2026; Bloomberg Morning Briefing, Sep 25, 2026

Briefly

Meta shares are up 27% since the Muse launch on September 8. Rosenblatt estimates Muse needs far cheaper compute, or users spending over $1,000 a month through it, to be profitable. The Information suggests Meta sell stock while it can.

Cloud CPU spot discounts of up to 90% have largely vanished and reservations need months of notice, partly because reinforcement learning eats CPUs. Everyone modelled a GPU shortage. Few modelled this one. Anthropic signed an $11.6B, seven-year compute deal with Akamai. The White House reportedly asked OpenAI and Anthropic not to share new models with UK testers before US assessments.
