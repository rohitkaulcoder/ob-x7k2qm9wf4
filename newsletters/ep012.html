Ep 12: Count Megawatts, Not Moratoriums

Overnight Brief, written edition · Wed, Sep 16, 2026 · audio: 17:38
🎧 Listen to the audio episode

The wrong count

More than 300 American towns and counties have paused or banned new data centres. The obvious headline says local politics is stopping the AI build-out. SemiAnalysis mapped those rules to parcels and found roughly 1.5 gigawatts actually delayed locally. The US is still forecast to deliver 38 gigawatts of data-centre IT capacity in 2027.

One statistic counts laws. The other counts machines.

A moratorium matters only when it blocks an approval a live project still needs. Most freeze new applications. They do not revoke old permits. County rules stop at city lines. A 2028 project can wait for a six-month pause to expire. Grid restrictions do not stop a campus with its own generation. Exposed capacity is not delayed capacity.

This is really a lesson about critical paths. A project can be inside a restricted boundary while financing, equipment or power was already the slower step. Policy becomes load-bearing only when it moves the delivery date.

Source: SemiAnalysis, “Everyone Says Datacenter Moratoriums Are Killing the US Buildout. We Disagree,” Sep 15, 2026 · read the original

Permitted power is the asset

About 20 gigawatts sit within restricted boundaries. Only 1,525 megawatts are directly delayed by local action, according to SemiAnalysis. Three projects account for nearly all of that. New York’s order touches 1.4 gigawatts, but only 0.8 gigawatts faces a meaningful delay caused by it. Meanwhile, 22 gigawatts of the 2027 forecast is already under vertical construction.

Politics still matters. Ohio residential power bills rose 25.7% year on year after data-centre demand lifted capacity costs. Forty local moratoriums followed. These pauses are bargaining instruments. Residents want protection from the bill. Developers want permission to energise.

Texas shows the market response. Grid delays push projects behind the meter, where generation sits on the customer side of the utility connection. SemiAnalysis counts 75 gigawatts of firm behind-the-meter equipment orders. Regulation does not merely reduce supply. It changes the architecture.

The winners are existing sites, turbine and fuel-cell makers, microgrid software and developers with permits in hand. The moat is not the building. It is the right to turn it on.

The software factory arrives

Inside OpenAI, Codex has gone from coding tool to operating layer. Usage across non-engineering teams rose from almost zero to 90% in four months. People use long-running threads to research, build documents and keep working toward a goal for days.

The software factory is a loop. A human defines an outcome. Codex gathers context, changes code, runs tests and opens a pull request. It watches continuous integration and fixes failures. Specialist agents review security, infrastructure and performance. Risk labels decide whether the change can be auto-approved or requires a person. Deployment agents watch production. Perf Factory turns regressions back into patches.

The unit of automation is no longer “write this function.” It is “own this outcome through feedback.” The model is the engine. The harness is the factory.

Factories create bottlenecks. Pull requests per engineer are rising like a hockey stick. Tests, review and deployment now absorb the load typing once did. Native mobile remains especially slow because store approval still takes hours or days. Code can be abundant while permission stays scarce.

Source: The Pragmatic Engineer, “Inside OpenAI’s Agentic Software Factory,” Sep 15, 2026 · read the original

Verification becomes the market

Giving every developer a coding agent is the entry ticket. The advantage is making software legible to agents: documentation near code, machine-readable tests, risk labels, observability and rollback. Poorly instrumented systems cannot become autonomous because the agent cannot tell whether it succeeded.

Engineering roles move up the stack. OpenAI engineers describe becoming more like product managers. Domain experts join engineering teams because taste defines the outcome. Specialisation does not disappear. It moves from producing every artefact to defining quality and designing checks.

The venture opportunity follows the bottleneck. Agent identity. Test generation. Policy enforcement. Code provenance. Risk scoring. Deployment control. Incident replay. The market does not need another system that writes a function. It needs the control plane that decides whether a million functions are safe to run.

When production becomes abundant, proof becomes scarce.

The contract beats the benchmark

Major Anthropic customers are still waiting for zero data retention on the newest Fable models. Palantir and Booz Allen restricted Fable use and expanded OpenAI’s Astra, where they could secure zero retention. A cybersecurity vendor serving government and finance moved customer-facing applications mainly to Astra for the same reason.

There is a real trade-off. Retaining interactions helps a lab investigate abuse. Deleting them limits customer exposure. The provider wants observability. The enterprise wants minimisation. Both call their preference security.

Anthropic plans Enterprise Frontier Safeguards later this fall, effectively restoring zero retention for eligible customers. The qualification rules remain unclear. That uncertainty is already a sales channel for OpenAI.

Benchmark leadership has a short half-life. A clear data contract can win deployment before a rival model is approved for testing. Enterprise AI is sold as capability plus terms.

Trust travels down the stack

Calls to pace frontier development are already making ordinary software buyers more cautious. One investor told The Information that a small-business AI application had encountered new customer concern after catastrophic-risk headlines. The labs intended to discuss frontier safety. The fear reached procurement.

The effect splits the startup market. New model labs face higher compliance costs. Security and infrastructure vendors gain demand. Applied-AI companies may gain time if frontier progress slows. Gimlet Labs routes work to the right chips. Neo protects devices from agents and raised $100 million. Both are toll booths on complexity.

For an Indian enterprise startup, the useful safety policy is five answers. What data reaches the model? How long is it stored? Can it train the model? What may the agent do without approval? Can an incident be reconstructed?

The smartest model does not automatically win. The acceptable system wins the purchase order.

Source: The Information, Dealmaker, Sep 15, 2026 · subscriber article · read the original

Capability asks for permission

Meta plans camera-free smart glasses called Luna. Six microphones connect to Meta AI and Muse. Speakers return audio. The arms are slimmer. Removing the camera sacrifices visual capability and reduces the social cost of wearing the device.

Existing Ray-Bans signal recording with an LED, but some owners have covered it. Meta now stops recording when the light is blocked. The technical fix cannot settle the social problem. A camera on someone else’s face creates a consent question before it creates a product feature.

Meta may also preview Phoenix AR glasses with a power puck and displays. Reality Labs lost $4.62 billion last quarter. The strategy is a ladder: audio glasses, camera glasses, then spatial displays. Every rung adds capability, battery, cost and social friction.

Meta also launched Meta One, bundling extra AI use with social tools. AI now needs permission from towns, reviewers, enterprise data owners, people near a wearable and consumers asked to subscribe. Constraints do not stop markets. They redirect margin. The opportunity lives at the boundary.
