Ep 26: The Backlog Is Not the Building

Overnight Brief, written edition · Wed, Sep 30, 2026 · audio: 22:31
🎧 Listen to the audio episode

Demand is not delivery

Nscale's $103 billion contracted backlog has the shape of a business and the timing of a construction project. The Information reports five operating data centers and another twelve contracted for development. Nearly all of the backlog depends on sites not yet built, financed, powered or equipped. Its first-half revenue was $140.6 million, up from $10.4 million a year earlier. The gulf between that revenue and the backlog is the story.

A compute contract is not cash in the bank. A developer must procure land, power, cooling, networking and chips before it can rent capacity. Some contracts allow customers to leave if capacity is late. Anthropic accounts for nearly half the backlog; Microsoft accounts for about 42%. Nscale has $24 billion in technology-equipment purchase commitments due this year and next, against $1.5 billion of cash at June 30. It has issued nearly $11 billion of debt since. That is progress in financing, not proof the buildout is funded.

Valuation turns on the delivery schedule. The Information's analysis divides two roughly $44 billion contracts over about six years, suggesting a combined $14.6 billion annual rate once fully operating. With other deals, an optimistic 2028 could approach $18 billion. But facilities scheduled for late 2027 do not generate a full year of revenue on January 1. A slower ramp yields a modeled $8 billion in 2028 and supports a valuation nearer the $15 billion private mark than the reportedly sought $35 billion IPO value, using the cited peer multiples. These are scenarios, not forecasts. The multiple cannot rescue revenue that has not yet been delivered.

For a venture investor, the repeatable test is simple: list the contractual revenue, the milestones needed to earn it, the spending due before those milestones, and the customer's right to walk. A backlog is evidence of demand. It is not a substitute for underwriting construction and capital.

Source: The Information, True Value, Sep 29, 2026 (subscriber)

Who finances the electricity?

Data-center power is becoming a separate investment business. Grid-connected generation and transmission can take five to ten years to develop, Fitch Ratings estimates, as cited by The Information. On-site power can shorten that wait, at a higher unit cost. Williams says its first 200 megawatts of on-site generation came online less than 18 months after commercialization.

A Blackstone-led consortium including KKR and Apollo is investing $5.3 billion for a 49% stake in five Williams gas-power projects. Some funds buy stakes in power developers; others finance specific assets. Bloom Energy's arrangement with Brookfield offers up to $25 billion of potential financing for future fuel-cell deployments, not $25 billion of completed projects. A $1.7 billion Bloom installation for Nebius has minority equity from Oaktree and senior debt led by MUFG. The power supplier, project owner, lender and cloud customer each have a different claim on the same future cash flow.

The mismatch is duration. A gas plant or fuel cell may work for decades; GPU generations turn over quickly and compute contracts tend to be shorter. Investors prefer ten- to fifteen-year power commitments to recover their costs. Compute customers want room to switch to grid power or more efficient chips. Selling surplus electricity into the grid later is a possible exit, subject to connection and price, not a guaranteed one.

Convertible bonds offer another route to lower cash interest today, at the cost of possible future dilution. Technology firms raised roughly $78 billion through convertibles through September 11, around 60% of US issuance, according to S&P Global Market Intelligence figures cited in the newsletter. Financing terms will decide who captures the value of AI demand. The chip is only one asset in the chain.

Source: The Information Special Report, Sep 29, 2026 (subscriber newsletter)

The IPO window has its own clock

Oura postponed its IPO, citing market uncertainty. Amaero and Holtec Nuclear had already delayed theirs. SB Energy filed publicly but had not begun marketing its offering when The Information's Briefing went out. These firms do not share a business model. They share a market asking tougher questions about valuation, rates, energy costs and the financing still needed.

Bloomberg's Asia briefing says OpenAI is seeking at least $30 billion in new funding at a valuation around $1.4 trillion. The Information's Briefing discusses Anthropic's potential IPO, but a delay is its columnist's judgment, not an announced plan. The Information's AI Agenda, citing Reuters on a confidential filing, says nearly a quarter of Anthropic's $4.6 billion revenue last year came from two customers. The concentration problem extends through customers, cloud suppliers and lenders. A postponed IPO is no verdict on AI's usefulness. It is a test of the price and risk allocation demanded by new capital.

Bloomberg's dated market snapshot put the US 30-year Treasury yield at 5.57% and Brent futures at $102.54. Those are not live quotes. For an India-focused investor, costly capital and oil affect follow-on financing, imports and margins. The useful discipline is to price milestones rather than extrapolate a total addressable market.

Sources: The Information, The Briefing and AI Agenda, Sep 29-30, 2026 (subscriber newsletters); Bloomberg Morning Briefing Asia, Sep 29, 2026

The shopping screen is the prize

Shopify welcomes Meta's Muse shopping agent. Amazon blocks it. Tanay Jaipuria and a16z describe the incentive behind each move. Shopify sells merchant software and earns from payments. Merchant solutions were 76% of its revenue, the Jaipuria newsletter says. If an agent brings an order to a merchant and Shopify still processes checkout, it can gain without owning the customer's search screen.

Amazon owns a marketplace and the sponsored placements on its screen. Jaipuria cites roughly $69 billion in 2025 advertising revenue. An agent can route a purchase through Amazon's warehouses while choosing the item elsewhere. Fulfillment survives; the sponsored placement may not. Discovery, payment and delivery are different layers. AI can unbundle them.

An outside agent brings two kinds of orders: genuinely new ones and orders the marketplace would have won anyway. The latter can leave the retailer with the same work, a smaller ad yield and perhaps a referral fee to pay. The agent has its own conflict. If fees influence recommendations, an apparently impartial answer becomes a new ad slot. The test for marketplaces and India-facing commerce startups is incremental demand, fulfillment economics, customer support and disclosure, not the mere availability of an API.

Sources: Tanay Jaipuria, Sep 29, 2026; a16z, Sep 29, 2026

The answer engine pays for its sources

Google is testing payments to about 100 digital publishers based on how much their content improves AI answers, The Information reports. Some smaller participants are receiving less than 0.1% of their advertising revenue. Others report $50,000 to $60,000 over a few months, or more than $1 million annualized. These are different publishers and time spans, not a common rate.

The old bargain was indexing in exchange for visits. AI Overviews can answer a question before a visit happens. A new payment system values a publisher's contribution to the answer, rather than just the click it receives. But publishers say the measurement is opaque and payments can move without clear explanation. Google runs both the search engine and the scorekeeper. Fresh independent material remains useful to the answers, yet a small check may not replace lost audience revenue. The business question is whether creators can negotiate a fair price when the buyer controls attribution.

The same measurement caution applies to model-training data. The Information's AI Agenda reports that Proximal, a coding-data startup backed by General Catalyst, claims over $200 million in annualized revenue by multiplying revenue generated in the current quarter so far by four. That is a founder-reported run rate, not a completed year. Its approach uses AI to create coding tasks and graders. A useful task must measure a real skill, not invite the model to game its own test. The metric to watch is durable demand and improvement on unseen work.
