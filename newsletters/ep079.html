The keystone falls

Hold two facts about the same company at once, because the whole strange week is inside them. On Thursday, Anthropic’s debut briefly made it the sixth-most valuable company on earth at the open, before it closed the day at $1.76 trillion, in seventh place, and started dragging the entire stock market down with it. On Friday, one of Anthropic’s own mathematicians posted a note on his personal website saying that, working with Claude, he had discovered a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, the most famous unsolved problem in all of mathematics. On Saturday, the mathematics community said: we checked. It’s real. The problem deserves the reverence. Primes are the atoms of arithmetic, and their distribution looks random - a gap, a cluster, a long desert. In 1859 Bernhard Riemann pointed at where the hidden order lives: his zeta function re-encodes all the primes at once as geometry, the way a spectrogram turns a song into every frequency laid out cleanly. The zeros of that function control how the primes are spaced, and Riemann’s guess was that every non-trivial one lies on a single straight line. If so, the primes obey a strict statistical law, and thousands of theorems proved by saying “assuming Riemann” get promoted from conditional to true. The Clay Institute put a million-dollar bounty on it, and for 167 years the best minds mostly didn’t even try, because there was no visible path in. Until October, when an OpenAI math result made the first chink in the armor. And then Friday.

The Guardian, Nov 21 (two independent groups verified). Scientific American, Nov 20, updated Nov 21 (proof confirmed; Lean/Mathlib joint statement). WSJ Today’s Paper, Nov 21, A6 (digest treatment).

Six weeks, then a compiler

The mechanism matters as much as the result. Levent Alpöge is not a hobbyist - a serious number theorist, Harvard and Princeton, a student of Manjul Bhargava, now on Anthropic’s technical staff. He says he spent about six weeks working with Claude on the proof: six weeks against a problem that outlasted Gauss, Hilbert and Turing. And he did the one thing that makes a claim like this checkable at all: he published the proof as code, written in Lean, a proof-checker - a compiler for mathematics. If the code compiles, every logical step is valid; no referees, no vibes, no trusting reputation. Mathlib, the community-built library behind it, already contains most of undergraduate and research mathematics in checked form, so a new proof stands on a million verified shoulders instead of starting from axioms. The questions mathematicians asked were exactly the right two, laid out by Kevin Buzzard at Imperial College: does the theorem the code proves actually say what the Riemann hypothesis says, word for word, and does the code compile cleanly on everyone else’s machine. On Saturday came the answer: the Lean and Mathlib communities issued a joint statement of full confidence in both the statement and the proof, with two independent groups verifying. The grown-ups are still being grown-ups - journal peer review takes months - but machine verification is not a weaker form of checking; it is the strongest form mathematics has. And be honest about what happened: this is not “AI did math.” An elite human spent six weeks directing a model that filled in steps which would have taken him a lifetime. Taste stayed human; the combinatorial grind went to the machine. The workflow lesson generalizes: Alpöge didn’t ask the model to be right, he asked it to produce something checkable - generate freely, verify mechanically. The timing irony is unignorable: in the same 48 hours, the market marked Anthropic down as a bubble while its model helped close a 167-year-old problem. Wall Street spent the week asking whether these models are worth anything. Mathematics spent the week answering.

The trillion-dollar thought experiment

Masayoshi Son apparently woke up and chose violence. The Information’s exclusive: the SoftBank chief recently approached Wall Street institutions about financing a possible buyout of SK Hynix - the memory-chip giant - at a total deal value, with a typical premium, close to a trillion dollars. SoftBank confirmed the exploration happened and says it is no longer under active consideration, which is corporate for: yes, we really did this. Walk the numbers. Hynix listed in the US this July and now carries a global market cap around $580 billion, and it was worth considerably more about a month ago; a standard 40% premium on the higher mark lands near the headline. Could SoftBank pay? Unclear - the reporting is blunt that there’s no sign Wall Street would have financed it, with the balance sheet already stretched over OpenAI commitments. Would Korea allow it? Almost certainly not: crown-jewel acquisitions need prior government approval, SK Group showed no interest, and Hynix says it has no knowledge of the matter. So this was never a deal. It was a thought experiment with bankers on the phone, and the thought experiment is the signal: Son looked at the AI supply chain, found the one layer he doesn’t own, and it happens to be the most profitable one. The bottleneck in modern AI chips is often not calculation but feeding the calculator - HBM, DRAM stacked in vertical layers parked next to the processor - and this year that power showed up in the P&L: margins close to 80% at Hynix, Micron and Samsung. Son already has the chip IP in Arm, the data centers in SB Energy, the design house in Ampere, the financing in DigitalBridge, stakes in Intel and OpenAI. Memory is the gap, and the gap prints money. The timing is darkly funny: memory is the most cyclical business in technology, 80% margins are exactly what attracts the capacity that kills 80% margins, and Son buying at the top of the cycle would be very on brand. Admire the leverage architecture while you’re at it: borrowed against Alibaba to buy Arm for $32 billion, borrowed against Arm to put $64.6 billion into OpenAI - a stack of loans balanced on previous bets. A trillion-dollar memory buyout on top wouldn’t be a deal. It would be a dare.

The restoration campaign

The saga that will not die went fully public this week. Jensen Huang said Tuesday he’d be open to returning; on Friday President Trump said Nvidia should give him the job back - “we’re a country of second chances.” The conference circuit turned into a tribute band: Musk at DealBook saying no one can really replace Huang, Travis Kalanick and Antonio Gracias wearing black leather jackets in solidarity, Altman calling him a personal hero, Bezos calling him remarkable. Then the actual scheme: Bill Ackman, amplified by Garry Tan, floating a plan to assemble 15% of Nvidia’s shares, call a special shareholder meeting, vote out the anti-Huang directors and swing the board - a hostile restoration by shareholder vote, engineered around a real lever, since Nvidia’s own bylaws let holders of 15% force a special meeting. Columbia law professor Jeffrey Gordon’s verdict was savage: somewhere between extremely unlikely and remote. The math is brutal: even rallying every active institution with a big stake, the BlackRocks of the world, leaves the enormous passive block, and passive funds vote with management or not at all - joining an insurrection is structurally the opposite of their job. The realistic path is the board changing its own mind, and that room is shrinking: SEC, Commerce, Justice, three congressional committees, possibly the FBI. Directors who rehire a CEO mid-investigation own whatever the investigation finds. The Information’s Weekend piece dismantles the Altman comparison cleanly: Altman’s 2023 return rode a genuine staff rebellion, 745 of 770 employees, over 95%, threatening to walk to Microsoft - the board wasn’t persuaded, it was outnumbered. The pro-Huang letter claims about 10,263 signatures; even if every one is real, that’s roughly a quarter of Nvidia’s workforce. A quarter is not a rebellion. It’s a fan club. The restoration story is irresistible to cover, but the numbers say it’s not a story yet.

A valuation story, not an economy story

The week deserves a frame more than a rerun. The Journal’s opinion pages have named the moment: the AI correction trade arrives. Their analysis desk went further, arguing American state capitalism helped produce the Nvidia scandal - government and corporate interests so intertwined under Trump that the backlash was structural, not personal. Under the hood the inputs are genuinely mixed: Fed governor Lisa Cook explained her October dissent as inflation urgency, which is hawkish, while consumer sentiment actually climbed in November, which is not what a crash tape usually produces. Households are fine; the discount rate is not. That can stay a valuation story as long as the jobs data holds. And the fragility thread we keep tracking had another week: flight delays continued after the FAA ground stop, and JPMorgan, Wells Fargo, Apple and Microsoft all reported service disruptions. The plumbing keeps rattling exactly when everyone is staring at the ceiling.

Cheese is about everything

To India, and a story loved because it’s about cheese, which means it’s actually about everything. The Core reports that artisanal cheese, sourdough and imported chocolate have quietly become quick-commerce categories. The vignette: a retired Delhi teacher who used to make a ritual of Modern Bazaar, the capital’s old-school gourmet grocer, now orders the same premium groceries on Blinkit. That anecdote is the whole premium-grocery model in miniature, and the numbers underneath move in two directions at once. Satish Meena at Datum Intelligence frames the disruption as two-way: existing premium-grocery customers move part of their basket online - the store keeps the discovery trip but loses the replenishment - and, more interestingly, a whole second group discovers premium products directly on the apps without ever setting foot in a gourmet store. It cuts both ways for brands: the founder of The Gourmet Jar says quick commerce took her clean-label sauces into smaller cities that have no premium grocery retail at all, customers a store-led strategy could never have reached. For a consumer investor this is the q-commerce thesis in miniature: the apps aren’t just stealing the gourmet store’s customers, they’re manufacturing new ones in markets the store could never reach. The channel became the market.

The Core, Nov 21 (Modern Bazaar/Blinkit vignette; Datum Intelligence’s Satish Meena; The Gourmet Jar).
