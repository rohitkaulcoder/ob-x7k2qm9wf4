The filing, in numbers

Saturday we said a warning is free and a filing is sworn. Monday delivered the sworn version. Anthropic publicly filed its S-1: roadshow in about two weeks, trading targeted for the week of November 16, Nasdaq, ticker ANT. The business is genuinely startling. $16.3 billion of revenue in the first half, $11.6 billion of it in Q2 alone - two and a half times all of last year, up over 1,300% year over year. Gross margins around 85%. An operating profit of $974 million in Q2, from a $1.7 billion operating loss in Q1. Operating cash flow positive $3.9 billion for the half against negative $2.6 billion for 2025. The $80.7 billion headline net loss is convertible-note accounting: every valuation jump books a paper charge; ex-charges the half-year loss is $8.4 billion. The model family is Mythos - Fable from April’s Project Glasswing, Opus 5.5, Sonnet, Haiku - behind a product portfolio that has quietly become a suite: Code, Cowork, Security, Tag, Design, Science, mostly sold as metered consumption. Pay for intelligence by the sip. The other half of the document is the obligation: roughly $657 billion of compute commitments over seven to ten years, $417 billion uncancellable, up to $806 billion with deals under negotiation - $114.5B Google Cloud, $110B AWS, $44.9B Microsoft, $161B for Google chips via Broadcom, $20B AMD, and $120.5B to SpaceX by 2029, about $3.1 billion a month at full delivery. Against that, $112 billion of cash and a $15 billion revolver. Compute was 48% of revenue by Q2, down from 251% a year earlier, but the filing itself warns that compute is now committed 12-18 months out and short-term costs are rising. Top five customers are 25% of revenue; 48% of revenue arrives via cloud resale, at a 17% distribution fee. The suppliers are the sales channel.

Five classes of stock

The governance is the strangest thing in the document, and the part that survives the roadshow. Anthropic is a public benefit corporation with five share classes. Class T, one share held by a Long-Term Benefit Trust - five trustees, $400,000 a year each, forbidden from owning stock - appoints four of seven directors. Class F, one share held by the founders’ LLC, carries a fixed 50.1% of all votes and appoints the other three; the seven co-founders, including Dario and Daniela Amodei at roughly 2% economic ownership apiece, keep control indefinitely. Employees hold the tiebreaker if the trust and the board’s nominating committee split on the fourth trust seat. Sunsets exist but require stacked supermajorities of founders, employees and public holders. Lock-ups are staggered SpaceX-style but more complicated: some insiders can sell 10% on day one, founders up to 29% after the earlier of 180 days or Q1 2027 results. Matt Levine’s Money Stuff read (free in the email) is the one to carry: the founders’ letter asks you to buy stock in exchange for miracles, and the 90 pages of risk factors write the existential risk in the passive voice - concerns have been raised, and those concerns could adversely affect results of operations. The risk factor is not that the product might harm humanity; it is that concern about it might harm the stock. Levine’s hierarchy: humanity, then founders, then employees, then shareholders, then Amazon and Google - shareholders, but not the miracle-working kind. Goldman leads, Morgan Stanley stabilizes, Reuters puts the raise at up to $100 billion against a $1.5 trillion-plus valuation. None of the pricing is in the filing; the blanks are all still blank.

A quarter-trillion Monday

Anthropic was not even Monday’s only cash grab. Meta filed to raise as much as $95 billion in stock offerings, its board committee stating that timing matters to secure capital before competitors saturate the funding options - the quiet part, in writing. SpaceX signed a $40 billion financing agreement with an Apollo-managed entity to lease GPUs, the largest GPU financing in history, structured through an SPV kept off SpaceX’s balance sheet to protect the credit rating. Three announcements, nearly a quarter of a trillion dollars, one day. Musk marked the occasion by predicting guppy-level death and consolidation among the 200-plus neoclouds, debt markets now closed to companies whose only revenue is AI data centers. Self-serving, and also the tape: CoreWeave fell about 20% in a week and its existing debt now yields above 15%. The day’s perfect detail is that Apollo’s own chief economist, Torsten Slok, published an essay over the weekend finding zero financial payoff from AI for corporate America outside tech - the firm arranging the largest GPU financing in history is simultaneously publishing that the end customers have not made money from the product yet. Both things can be true. The tension between them is the financing story of 2026. Next checkpoint is midweek: Google, Microsoft and Amazon all report.

The agent’s card

Stripe has taken the early lead in how AI agents pay, and the card networks are on edge, per an Information exclusive. The mechanism: when an agent like Meta’s Muse buys for you, Stripe’s Link wallet issues a single-use virtual card with a limit tied to the exact approved purchase; the agent never sees your real card, the merchant sees a normal payment, and Stripe charges your actual card separately. Two transactions instead of one, which severs the networks’ end-to-end visibility, weakens fraud models, muddies disputes, and could even route volume onto a different network than the underlying card. Visa and Mastercard’s counter is tokenization - Visa Intelligent Commerce, Mastercard Agent Pay - identifying the shopper to the agent without sharing credentials. The tiebreaker may be the issuing banks, who control authorization itself: Bank of America and five others just proposed industry guidelines calling for auditable methods to enter payment credentials, a shot at virtual cards without naming them. Why it matters now: Zuckerberg says over a quarter of Muse’s 10 million users already shop with it, the average ticket is over $200, and Stripe had to raise credit limits within a week of launch. A payments CEO in the piece names the stakes: the biggest risk to Visa and Mastercard is Stripe infrastructure becoming the default - more so in agentic commerce than it ever was at checkout.

DoorDash circles Instacart

Friday, Instacart was exploring a sale with preliminary talks at DoorDash and Uber. By Monday the Journal had DoorDash in advanced talks - exclusive, per Semafor - with a deal possible this week valuing Instacart around $17 billion against a $13 billion pre-report market cap; shares popped 5%. If it lands, America’s two biggest delivery brands merge just as both pivot to grocery-and-everything, and the antitrust defense writes itself: Amazon and Walmart are the real giants. Watch whether Uber, having lost the exclusive, comes back over the top.

India: the shelf war

The sharpest India read of the day is Daman Soni’s framing of quick commerce’s assortment split. Blinkit now stocks about 100,000 items in Delhi, up from 80,000, air conditioners included; Albinder Dhindsa wants most of what a household buys inside ten minutes. Swiggy spent the same quarter arguing the opposite - Sriharsha Majety calls assortment a curation game rather than a volume game, the aim being to let people buy better, not just offer more. One widens the shelf until it holds a split AC; the other narrows on purpose. The kicker is that both companies decide this year which brands sit on that shelf, which makes assortment philosophy the real battleground for consumer-brand distribution in urban India. Whoever wins sets the terms on which every brand in the country reaches its best customers - a bigger prize than the delivery-fee war ever was.

Daman Soni, Blinkit’s Flywheel, Swiggy’s Treadmill, Oct 26 (free, full read).

Brazil’s record crash, and quick hits

The macro story nobody in tech watched: Lula won Brazil’s presidency in a surprise after global investors spent weeks positioned for Flavio Bolsonaro, the first-round winner, to deliver deregulation. The unwind was violent - the real down more than 10% against the dollar, the Bovespa down nearly 17%, a record daily loss per the Journal. That is what a crowded political trade looks like when the crowd is wrong. Quick hits: Cathedral, a startup founded by former DOGE members, says its AI agents gained access to Anthropic’s internal systems - an awkward claim on S-1 day and another data point in the agentic-hacking wave. Microsoft is extending voluntary buyout offers to 60,000 employees, over a quarter of its workforce, with uptake expected well under 20,000. Block released Dessa, a voice model testers call the first to cross the uncanny line against GPT’s live voice. Burry has a free abridged post slicing 45 years of S&P 500 tops into the broken, the busted and the leaders - the pattern is the point. Stratechery’s The End of the Internet argues the critical PHP bug ends the era of anyone hosting a server; managed everything is simply the web now. France capped freshman high-school classes at 28 to answer the student protests, and Florida is leading a multi-front state fight against AI and big tech (WSJ, labeled). Two Bloomberg headlines flagged without detail while its site blocks our session: Musk’s SpaceX-Tesla merger dream rests on Tesla shareholders, and their markets columnist is surfing the AI-bond wave in increments of fear, with oil as the wipeout trigger.
