The states step in

Day three of the correction, and it stopped being a Nasdaq story. Overnight, China cut its margin requirement to 80%, mechanically adding borrowing power to a falling market; Taiwan banned short selling outright; Korea rolled out what it calls phased stabilization measures. Emerging-market stocks crossed into a bear market anyway. The final tally from Friday deserves precise numbers: Anthropic closed at $88.91, down 8.5% on the day, 7% above its $83 offer price after two days of trading, having traded as low as $83.05 - five cents above the offer. Thursday it had briefly touched the sixth-most-valuable-company mark at the open and closed at $1.76 trillion fully diluted; Friday’s slide took that to roughly $1.6 trillion. The complex went with it: AMD -13%, Broadcom -12%, Dell -11%, and Nvidia, after Thursday’s -3%, lost another 14% to close near $125. The Journal’s James Mackintosh has the cleanest frame: the mini-bubble - the speculative froth in data centers and their suppliers - has already popped. The open question is whether the big one follows. The anxiety is visible in corporate behavior: Oracle issued a statement late Friday insisting it had already raised all the money it needs for its OpenAI data-center deal. Companies do not issue statements like that in calm markets. And governments do not intervene over froth. They intervene when they fear the froth was load-bearing.

From boardroom to spy case

The Nvidia saga crossed a line this weekend. The Director of National Intelligence, Jay Clayton, confirmed on television that the US is actively investigating the alleged Chinese spy whose affair with Jensen Huang sits at the center of the scandal. Treasury Secretary Bessent said authorities need answers from Nvidia on compliance. Huang is formally set to appear before Congress on December 1. A week ago this was a governance story; it is now a counterintelligence investigation with a sworn-testimony date, and the restoration campaign we covered Saturday - the Ackman 15% bylaw plan, the tribute circuit - has run straight into that wall. Directors weighing Huang’s return are no longer pricing reputational risk; they are pricing a spy case. The Journal added a name: behind Alice Huang at Megaspeed, the Singapore firm suspected of funneling Nvidia compute to China, sat Li Ming, a 52-year-old Chinese national now facing fraud charges in Singapore, involved in Megaspeed’s decisions while playing a role in other companies trading Nvidia chips. Commerce Secretary Lutnick is taking fire from his own side - Bessent reportedly blames him for slow-walking chip restrictions, and Senator Cotton says he repeatedly ignored smuggling warnings. Two more threads worth holding: Tim Higgins went after Huang’s famous handshake deals, the no-contract agreements that read as governance red flags now; and the Journal checked the headline that Wall Street would mobilize $500 billion for Nvidia - not a dime has actually arrived. The gap between announced capital and arrived capital is becoming the theme of the entire AI build-out.

The Google tax

How one company’s AI pivot quietly taxes everyone else. Google’s search ad growth has decelerated for three straight quarters - 19%, then 17%, now 12% - because AI Overviews and AI Mode answer more queries directly and monetize worse than ten blue links, as Google’s own chief business officer admitted. The strange part: total query volume is growing at its fastest pace in five years. People search more than ever, in longer conversational phrasings that simply do not lead to purchases the way keyword searches did. The pain is exported. Apple takes a cut of the ad revenue Google earns on Safari - court documents put it at 36%, worth $20 billion in 2022 - and Apple’s services growth just slowed from 12% to 10%, with executives warning it cools further, partly for this reason. Reddit is the extreme case: less referral traffic, US daily users down from 53.2 million to 50.7 million in one quarter, stock down 34% since earnings, more than double the Nasdaq’s drop. Google’s search machine produced $187 billion of ad revenue in nine months; when a machine that size shifts gears, the whole supply chain of the attention economy feels it. Analysts peg AI Mode’s monetization parity with old search at late 2027 or early 2028. Until then, everyone else’s numbers leak.

The ground game

The week’s must-read reframes the data-center race: it is not a chip story, it is a permitting story. Amazon is converting Homer City, Pennsylvania - once a 2-GW coal plant that New York distressed-debt investors picked up in 2017 - into a 4.4-GW AI power park with thirty-six buildings, the last one expected in 2036. Count the stakeholders: three townships, two boroughs, a county commission, a planning commission, a state environmental gatekeeper, two regional grid authorities, a federal energy regulator. Eleven entities, each wanting something. Nine trade unions are on site; the boilermakers’ man put it plainly: invite us to Thanksgiving dinner and we all get a piece of turkey, or none of us stay. Environmental groups have appealed the air permit; the governor wants 32% clean-firm power by 2035 for fast-tracking, and the plant is designed as 100% gas. Amazon’s edge is not money but twenty-nine years of community relationships and utility credibility - in Indiana, the state utility agreed to connect Amazon’s sites sooner while giving ratepayers bill credits as usage ramps. Try that as a two-year-old neocloud. The piece also corrects the satellite-image crowd: the plan puts 100% of the power on the grid and wheels some to Amazon next door, easing a regional shortage and buying political cover. Cleanview’s tally of actual construction starts, steel going up, over twelve months: Amazon 4.6 GW, Google 4.1 GW, the Nvidia-chip ecosystem over 10. And the kicker: many of those Amazon and Google buildings will run Trainium and TPU silicon, not Nvidia GPUs. After the midterms sent more data-center skeptics into office, excellent economics at scale is a big-company game, and it will only get more so.

Paywalled for readers: The Information, “How Amazon Plays the Long, Expensive Data Center Game That Neoclouds Can’t,” Ann Davis Vaughan, Nov 22 (full text via subscriber newsletter).

Physical AI, Indian cheques

The Runway’s weekly deal sheet: fifteen deals, five cross-border, and the theme was physical AI - with Indian investors writing the cheques, mostly across borders. Genesis AI, building general-purpose human-dexterity robots, raised a $500 million Series A at a $3.5 billion valuation, co-led by Premji Invest with Khosla and Eclipse, with both Nvidia and AMD Ventures in the round. Same theme at Humyn Labs, capturing real-world human activity as robotics training data, where Accel led a $47 million round with two Bajaj vehicles participating. At home the standout is Bharat Semi: a $24 million seed, big for Indian deep tech, led by Info Edge-backed Redstart with South Park Commons, building vision sensors that analyze images on the chip itself instead of shipping raw footage to the cloud. Also notable: FMO led Rs 101 crore into InPrime Finserv, an NBFC lending to the informal sector with its own AI underwriting; SIDBI’s space fund put Rs 60 crore into SatSure, whose data product sits inside the RBI’s unified lending interface for farm loans; and PIDG made its first waste-management investment in Asia, Rs 110 crore into Ahmedabad’s Nepra. The ten domestic deals drew under $100 million combined. The signal is nuanced: Indian deep tech gets funded steadily, but the ambitious robotics money still goes to Paris and San Carlos.

The Runway, “Chips, Robots and Bubble Tea,” Nov 22 (India PE-VC weekly).

Three reports before Thanksgiving

A thin earnings week, but three reports carry the load. Zoom, Monday: the interesting line is not the 4-5% growth business but the Anthropic stake worth close to $5 billion at the IPO price, roughly a fifth of Zoom’s market cap - Friday’s 8.5% slide hits its books directly. Workday, Tuesday: a saaspocalypse candidate, growth slowing to about 10.6%, co-founder Aneel Bhusri back in the chair since February, no visible magic yet. Dell, Tuesday: the picks-and-shovels pure play. Its AI-optimized server line did $1.8 billion two years ago, grew another 222% in the first half of this fiscal year, and the stock was up 121% year-to-date before Friday took 11% off. The FT added a wrinkle, reporting on the aggressive pricing policies Dell and other server makers are using to keep the AI order book growing - hold that in mind when reading the margin lines. The backlog stands at $95 billion; that is the number that tells you whether the capex machine is still being fed. Also in deal news: Calcalist reports Amazon is close to buying Decart, the Israeli maker of hyper-efficient video models and an early Trainium3 customer, for $7.5 billion.

Quick hits

CrowdStrike is in talks to buy Zscaler, market cap $32 billion, in one of the largest cybersecurity deals ever - consolidation against AI-powered hacks. Nvidia is near an acquisition of a diffusion-model developer for more than $500 million, per The Information - buying through the drawdown. OpenAI’s chief scientist proposed industrywide development limits tied to safety milestones, and Altman endorsed it, which reads differently after the Democrats’ midterm sweep. The wealth effect rolls on: Bloomberg Businessweek tallied San Francisco’s AI jackpot, and the Journal notes more than 5,000 Anthropic employees became paper millionaires, with early backers like Lightspeed on stakes worth north of $25 billion at the offer price - Sapphire’s Jai Das had the honest quote about over-rotating and wasting capital. COP31 in Antalya collapsed, the first major COP breakdown in years, after dozens of nations rejected a deal they saw as pandering to fossil fuel producers; Panama’s minister alleged a petroleum mafia held the summit hostage, and the EU’s climate chief said alternatives outside the UN process are now needed. Marc Lore’s Wonder is trying to sell Grubhub, weeks after Amazon swapped it for Uber Eats on Prime. And the artifact of the moment: Kalshi offered streamer Cliff Savage over $100,000, plus DoorDash credits and pizza, to play GTA VI to completion; 35,000 people applied.
