Count, don’t call

America voted, and as we record - Tuesday evening on the US East Coast - almost nothing is decided. Georgia, Ohio and Florida have closed and are counting; Texas, Wisconsin and the last corners of Michigan close at nine PM Eastern, seven thirty this morning IST; California votes until nine thirty this morning IST and Alaska goes last. The Associated Press’s first Senate call of the night gives Democrats a win in Virginia. Everything else this newsletter covers politically - the data-center governor races, Proposition 40, control of Congress - is uncalled, and we will keep saying so until it isn’t. The texture that does exist: exit surveys put fuel and food prices at the top of voter concerns, with many blaming the president. The discipline is the story. The first full picture arrives over the coming hours; anything more specific tonight is a guess wearing a necktie.

What tonight can’t change

Whatever the count shows, one thing does not change this morning: the antitrust regime belongs to the administration, not Congress. Dealmakers have a two-year window regardless of who wins, and they are sprinting through it. Announced M&A is at $2.6T this year, up 66% - The Information’s Martin Peers calls it the Supermarket Sweep market, and the aisle runner of the season is Salesforce: Cresta for $2.7B on Monday, Braze for $5.7B last month, Fin for $3.6B in September - $25B across eight deals in twelve months, paid almost entirely in cash. That is the discipline detail. Benioff’s last spree depressed margins and invited the activists; this one is funded by the efficiency drive itself, with free cash flow margin up from 20% of revenue to 35% - over $14B a year of ammunition. Whether the deals earn their keep is a later question; the structural point is that relaxed review lasts through 2028 whoever holds the House, so the shelves get cleared either way. Good for founders seeking exits, less good for anyone hoping competition policy saves them.

The governor map is the AI map

The races that matter most to the buildout all sit in the uncalled column. In Texas, Gina Hinojosa wants a stricter data-center moratorium than the one Greg Abbott already signed - in the state holding roughly 30% of next year’s planned capacity; polls close at seven thirty IST this morning. In Georgia, Keisha Lance Bottoms wants a construction pause in what CBRE says is now the country’s biggest data-center construction market; polls closed, counting. In Florida, Byron Donalds - backed by more than $4M from PACs affiliated with Anthropic and OpenAI’s Greg Brockman - faces David Jolly, who wants a moratorium; counting. In Alaska, Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins, running on a pause plus AI accountability rules, is the favorite, but ranked-choice counting and a one AM Eastern close mean patience. Three of those four states have not elected a Democratic governor since the nineties. The stakes underneath: SemiAnalysis counts 62 GW of planned 2028 capacity in states that voted last night, and a moratorium is a precise weapon - it stops approvals, not construction, so paper projects carry the risk while steel in the ground is safe. That is why the industry answers with money and the candidates answer with conditions: ratepayer protections, tax-break repeal, labor rules.

The SpaceX factory

While states debate permits, SpaceX is building faster than the debate. SemiAnalysis reports the 2027 data-center target revised up to roughly 13 GW, with 3.6 GW under construction and over nine identified - about everything Google built between 1999 and today, in a year and a half. The mechanics are the point. Colossus 1 did 150 MW in 122 days; Colossus 2 did 210 MW in 91; Project SPAM, a building in the middle of Colossus 2, started October 5 and targets December. A Mississippi greenfield called the Gun Range is tracking four months, the fastest large build anyone has on record. The method: parallelize construction, bring your own power (truck-mounted gas turbines behind the meter, plus a 5.9 GWh battery install that is likely the world’s largest), and repurpose what you already own - Starbase is getting two data centers, possibly over a gigawatt, and the 100M-square-foot TeraFab project looks likely to host one third parties can rent. The Anthropic and Google contracts already make it, per SemiAnalysis, the most profitable neocloud on earth; their $300B revenue destiny is a claim, not a forecast, and their own power model shows secured gigawatts trailing the data centers coming online. But they called the 10 GW supply order six weeks early in June. When they say thirteen, listen.

How much should Anthropic pop?

Matt Levine asked the question of the week. On Hyperliquid, perpetual futures on Anthropic - bets on its post-IPO price with no actual shares - trade at a valuation around $1.8T, and pre-IPO perps have been eerily accurate, calling the opening prints of both Cerebras and SpaceX. Meanwhile the Journal reports institutional investors are pushing Anthropic to price at $1.5-1.6T. Put those together and you can see the game: price at one point five, open at one point eight, hand IPO investors a perfect 20% first-day pop. Investors say they trust CFO Krishna Rao to deliver it; nobody wants to be the bank that priced the dud after SpaceX popped 30% in June. Levine’s deeper point: if a prediction market already publishes the clearing price, the century-old ritual of roadshow, book-building and deliberate underpricing becomes theater - when Meta sold $25B of stock last week it priced at a 0.9% discount, not twenty. The CFTC just approved perps on US stocks; pre-IPO versions are the obvious next step. Private and public markets are merging into one continuous price, and for venture that means the last private round is no longer the last word. Context for the buyer’s market: OpenAI’s round came in at $11B committed against a $30B target, and lenders are getting choosier across the buildout.

Bloomberg Money Stuff, Nov 3 (newsletter text, labeled). WSJ via Money Stuff (investor push, Rao). Bloomberg Morning Briefing Asia, Nov 3 (newsletter text, labeled).

Claude designs the chips

The Information reports Anthropic’s new fifteen-person chip team is already using Claude to engineer the company’s custom silicon - faster, it tells IPO investors, than the traditional workflows at Nvidia and AMD. A benchmark from startup ChipAgents and Nvidia now measures how good models are at chip design; Claude Fable 5.1 tops it. OpenAI runs the same loop: its custom chip, Jalapeño, was designed with Broadcom in nine months using OpenAI’s own models, with volume use planned next year. Nine months, when chip projects take years. The plausibility check: a modern AI chip carries over a hundred billion transistors, and most of a project’s years go to simulating and verifying that logic before anyone commits to silicon - exactly the search-and-check grind a frontier model eats. The honest caveat, also from the benchmark: nobody is close to automating the whole workflow. The market this points at is Synopsys and Cadence, the design-software duopoly doing a combined $16B a year. And the tension: Anthropic’s biggest backers - Amazon, Google, Nvidia, AMD, roughly $90B invested - are also its chip suppliers. If your model becomes best in the world at designing chips, do you sell it, or keep the edge? Anthropic hasn’t said it will commercialize any of it. But “Claude designs the chips that train Claude” is quite a sentence for a roadshow deck.

Sell shovels, bring cash

The tape, because the financing weather turned again. Odd Lots flagged a Ben Emons chart: compute stocks down 60% from peak, memory down 40%, hyperscalers down 13% - landing exactly as the buildout pivots from debt to equity. Oracle paired its $10B bond sale Monday with $9.9B of new stock at an 11% discount to the market; Meta is planning an equity raise of up to $97.5B, one of the largest stock offerings in history. Selling equity into a drawdown is what you do when the bond market starts charging a boom premium; you would rather sell high, but here we are. The same tape had AMD up after hours - data-center chip revenue nearly doubled, total revenue up 45%, guidance for rapid acceleration - while Shopify fell 7.6% because AI spending ate its subscription margins. That is the whole market in three tickers: sell the shovels, watch your margins if you buy them, and bring cash.

A coding test

How did North Korea get into Binance? Not a smart-contract exploit. A job interview. Binance’s postmortem describes a multi-stage campaign running since June: attackers approached a staff engineer with a fake job opportunity, put him on a call with someone posing as the CEO of a fictitious company, and had him download an assignment containing a malicious code repository onto his work computer. That is the $6.3B door: a coding test. It is the oldest play in the state-sponsored hacking book - Lazarus has run fake recruiter lures for years - and it worked on the biggest exchange in crypto. Bitcoin ETFs lost over a billion dollars in the aftermath as the hack met election jitters. Two lessons: the perimeter is your people, always was; and the response still worked - $3B frozen at the address level, Binance covering the rest, customers whole. If you are hiring, somebody is interviewing your engineers right now, and not all of them plan to pay a salary.
