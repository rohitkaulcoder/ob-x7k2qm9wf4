Ep 28: The Bigger Mask

Everyone is waiting for someone else to buy the bigger mask

The most interesting chip story of the week has no new chip in it. TSMC and ASML say the foundry will use High-NA EUV for volume production in 2030, and that the industry will move to a larger photomask, six inches by twelve, with a pilot line in 2031 and volume in 2033. Samsung issued a similar release the same day. This looks like a lithography story. It is a coordination story.

Start with the mechanism. A photomask is the stencil for a chip layer. At EUV wavelength, 13.5 nanometers, almost everything absorbs light, so the mask is a mirror: roughly 40 pairs of molybdenum and silicon layers, each pair about 7 nanometers thick, topped with an absorber that is etched into the circuit pattern. Resolution depends on numerical aperture, the share of light the optics can collect. Today's machines run at 0.33. High-NA runs at 0.55, which brings half-pitch lines down to about 7.5 to 8 nanometers and reduces the need to print one layer several times.

The cost is field size. To get the higher aperture without the light cones overlapping, ASML stretched the image eight times in one direction. The area exposed in one shot halves, from 33 by 26 millimeters to about 16.5 by 26. A chip bigger than that must be printed in two halves and stitched, and a modern AI chip is exactly that kind of chip. Even chips that fit need two shots where one used to do. A scanner reportedly costing around $350 million is a tool whose price is spread over its throughput, so lost throughput is lost economics.

A larger mask removes the penalty. Because the image is stretched in one direction, the stencil can be larger in that direction and the full field returns. Intel is cited as estimating 23 to 50 percent more scanner productivity, which cuts the cost gap with low-NA by about half. The catch sits upstream. Blanks come from AGC and Hoya, pellicles from Mitsui or Canatu, pods from Entegris and Gudeng, writers from IMS and Nuflare, inspection from KLA and Lasertec. Mask-shop tools for the larger format are estimated at 20 to 100 percent more than today's, and many shops may need to run old and new lines side by side. Nearly every supplier is a single source.

The physics is settled. The open question is who pays, and when. Each supplier needs to see a return in three to five years, and the customers are AI chip makers in a boom that may look different by 2033. Watch standards bodies. Gudeng has already helped get its carrier into the SEMI specification, and a company that is in the spec is hard to remove.

Source: Asianometry, "EUV Photomasks Are Getting Bigger," Oct 1, 2026 (members; read in full from the email). Members-only for readers.

A chip is worth what the lender thinks it is worth

Michael Burry's latest piece starts from a slide Nvidia showed investors on September 27. It argues that A100, H100 and B200 chips retain enough value to beat accelerated depreciation schedules, and so buyers are, if anything, writing them off too quickly. Only the opening of the piece is public, and it is mostly history. Burry sets a 1968 scene from The Money Game, in which young traders buy computer-leasing stocks on three to five percent cash and say the need for computers is practically infinite, against the 2026 tape. We have not read the paid argument, so the claim here is narrower than his.

Depreciation is an accounting choice that works as a profit lever. A $40,000 GPU written off over five years costs $8,000 a year. Over three years it costs about $13,300. The cash is the same. The reported profit is not. The same number also acts as collateral. The Information's Briefing reports that Australian neocloud Sharon AI raised $356 million in its first loan backed by GPUs and customer contracts, at 9.95 percent before fees. A lender in that position is underwriting the resale value and the rental life of the chip, not only the customer.

The market is already discriminating. Data-center builder Accelevation priced its IPO at $18, below a $20 to $24 range, and closed Thursday at $16.59, with $647.8 million of debt against $28 million of cash. Meanwhile Bloomberg reports Anthropic targeting a mid-November offering, and Nvidia and SoftBank have each paid their final $10 billion into OpenAI's last round. The strong names raise easily. The levered builders pay for it. The practical test for any model in this stack: which number is a vendor's opinion, and what breaks if it is wrong by a year.

Sources: Michael Burry, "Don't Believe Your Lyin' Eyes, GPU Depreciation & Useful Lives," Oct 1, 2026 (public excerpt); The Information, The Briefing, Oct 1-2, 2026 (read in full from the email). Paywalled for readers.

Chips are back, and so is the copycat bet

Newcomer reports that Benchmark won a contested lead on a round for Tendrils Compute, a small Cambridge, UK team led by Nils Cremer, with a fast follow-up discussed at a valuation above $1 billion. Neither round was previously reported. The team is betting on a graph-based model of computing called interaction nets, in which work runs wherever its inputs are ready rather than in a fixed sequence. The idea is the thing to underwrite. The funding is the signal.

The signal is Cerebras. Benchmark led its Series A in 2016 and saw a 20.5x multiple when it listed this spring. Etched doubled to a $21 billion valuation on a $700 million raise in August. Coatue and MatX have discussed a joint venture of billions to finance memory and capacity. Then the caution: Cerebras listed at $185, reached $386, and sits near its offer price. One outcome has changed how a whole group of investors prices hardware risk. That is the usual pattern, and it is why the second wave carries more risk than the first.

Source: Newcomer, "SCOOP: Benchmark Backs Early-Stage Startup Tendrils Compute," Oct 1, 2026 (link).

Agents are a trust problem before they are a model problem

Bloomberg's Q&AI reports that OpenAI introduced Dots at DevDay, an agent that connects to thousands of apps and acts on a user's behalf, and that Meta's Muse has overtaken ChatGPT on mobile charts by letting people build custom agents for tasks like finding discounts and booking restaurants. Protesters stood outside OpenAI's event, and a poll this week found most Americans expect AI to do more harm than good. OpenAI says recent incidents involved unreleased models without the guardrails Dots has.

Everyone has the same plan. The differences are permissions and track record. An agent that can act is only as useful as the number of things a user will let it touch. Distribution will help the large players. Trust will decide who keeps the access.

Source: Bloomberg, Q&AI newsletter, Oct 1, 2026 (read from the email). Paywalled for readers.

Microsoft's veterans are leaving at a good price

The Information's Martin Peers notes departures of Ryan Roslansky, head of Office apps, who leaves at year end, and Peter Lee, head of Microsoft Science, after Rajesh Jha's earlier exit. These are mostly 15-year veterans, holding stock up 1,289 percent since 2014. The reading offered is not panic. AI should grow Microsoft's cloud, and may also undercut its software, so people who have already won may be choosing to leave while the going is good, while newer leaders such as Asha Sharma gain responsibility. It is a reasonable reading, and still a reading.

Source: The Information, The Briefing, Oct 1, 2026 (read in full from the email). Paywalled for readers.

India's banks have cheap-ish money and nowhere obvious to put it

The Core reports $127.2 billion in FCNR(B) deposits, about Rs 12.2 lakh crore. System liquidity rose from Rs 6.7 lakh crore in mid-August to Rs 7.8 lakh crore by September 1. The money costs banks roughly 6.5 to 7 percent against 4 to 6 percent for domestic deposits, so the pressure is to earn double digits. The obvious product is unsecured credit, where the RBI has already raised risk weights, and the SBI chairman said these inflows will not lead to abnormal lending.

The likelier first use, per economists quoted, is replacing costlier funding such as commercial paper and bulk deposits, and delaying domestic deposit-rate increases. For startups that is a modestly easier credit backdrop, not a consumer-lending surge. Mint's Friday front page adds a seven-month high in manufacturing PMI, with a caution on input costs, and a test for ICICI Lombard's technology push with a combined ratio above 100.

Sources: The Core, Oct 1, 2026; Mint ePaper, Oct 2, 2026 (headlines and summaries only).

Private markets may open to everyone, slowly

Matt Levine writes that the SEC has proposed rules for what it calls responsible retailization of private markets. SpaceX listed this June near a $1.8 trillion valuation, so the first $1.8 trillion of its growth went to private holders. Levine describes roughly three routes: make going public easier, widen who can invest in private companies, or a middle path that still protects disclosure. The SEC is pushing the first two. For a venture fund the long-run question is who ends up as the marginal limited partner.

Source: Matt Levine, Money Stuff, Oct 1, 2026.
