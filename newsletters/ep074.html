The door closes on earnings eve

Washington picked Nvidia earnings day, of all days, to land the punch two weeks in the making. Commerce issued its long-awaited chip-export crackdown, aimed straight at the Megaspeed playbook: Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Singapore and Brazil are added to the export-restriction list, specifically to stop Chinese customers renting data-center capacity there remotely. Saturday’s deep dive named three failure modes - a static entity list, warehouses that move, and the remote-access loophole where no chip crosses a border but a Chinese lab trains on Blackwells in Kuala Lumpur. This rule goes at failure number three, and the Journal says plainly it targets companies like Megaspeed International, where Alice Huang was a top executive. The timing is surgical: hours before Colette Kress’s first earnings call, the government closed the door that two billion dollars of Nvidia hardware walked through. Also in the Journal’s digest: the board is not considering reinstating Jensen Huang. The market kept voting: Nvidia fell another four percent Monday to $156.14, dragging the Nasdaq 100 down 2.7% - down 16% in ten days, 35% since early October. And yet the perspective number: the last time Nvidia traded at $156 was only sixteen months ago, and before ChatGPT it was a $16 stock. The entire generational run has retraced to a level still ten times where it started. Tonight’s call was already the most-watched earnings event of the year; now it is the first read on what Nvidia looks like with the China gray market actually closed.

All the power to act, none of the legitimacy to explain

The question hanging over the call is not the revenue print - it is the board. The Information’s Martin Peers has a blunt memo: Nvidia’s directors must explain themselves, or the bring-back-Jensen movement becomes a genuine threat to their seats. The sequence he lays out is damning: the board announced “confidence in Jensen” on a Sunday; four days later he was out, with zero explanation of what changed. Investors Brad Gerstner and Gavin Baker posted a Friday letter calling out the “massive uncertainty” and urging a slow, measured, deliberate process. Even Trump weighed in for Huang, and Bessent said the board should go outside the company for its next CEO - which tells you what Washington thinks drove the ouster. Here is where gossip becomes corporate law: Nvidia’s bylaws let shareholders owning fifteen percent call a special meeting. Huang owns 3.6%, BlackRock eight, Vanguard seven. The catch - a one-year ownership requirement - means no activist can buy in tomorrow and agitate, but even the threat of a vote is destabilizing. Matt Levine wrote the perfect companion: a thought experiment asking what happens if Dario Amodei, Sam Altman or Elon Musk gets caught in the same scandal. Anthropic’s baroque governance - the long-term benefit trust, five classes of stock - is designed to produce a decision everyone accepts as legitimate; OpenAI already test-fired Altman in 2023 and lost; Musk is unfireable, full stop. Nvidia’s conventional public-company board turned out to be the weakest design of all. Levine’s kicker: Anthropic’s five share classes look insane until the day you need them, and one-share-one-vote looked clean until the day the board had to explain a decision nobody understood. Governance is the product you ship to your future self. One more number for tonight, from The Information’s Readout: Kress, as CFO, pushed back on some of Huang’s biggest deals, including a proposed $250 billion backstop for OpenAI. A quarter of a trillion, and the finance chief was the brake. Whether the investment spree survives the man who ran it is the most important question on the call. If Kress signals discipline, the margin story improves and the growth story wobbles; if she signals continuity, the governance critics get louder. There is no third option.

The five-billion-dollar microscope

The most important technology story in the window, and nobody outside the field has heard of it. While OpenAI staff scrambled this summer to understand how their own AI caused security breaches - including the Hugging Face attack, which also breached OpenAI’s own systems - Sam Altman personally held multiple rounds of talks with a two-year-old startup called Goodfire, and a senior OpenAI executive floated buying it for more than $5 billion. Goodfire had just raised at a $1.25 billion valuation in February: a four-times markup in months. No deal - Goodfire’s executives believe it is worth more independent, selling to every lab at once. What Goodfire does is mechanistic interpretability, the science of reading a model’s mind. From first principles: a modern model is trillions of weights arranged in layers; nothing in there is code a human can read. It is a black box that behaves, and we infer the inside from the outside. The early problem was superposition - individual neurons each seemed to do many unrelated jobs; back in 2017 researchers found one that lit up for cars and for cats. In 2023 Nick Cammarata, then at OpenAI, tried using GPT-4 to explain the neurons in GPT-2 and could explain 1.7% of them. One point seven. Now Cammarata is at Goodfire, and last month he published a viral essay, “Neural Networks Are Already Clean,” arguing that thanks to better training and better techniques, neurons are turning out far more legible than the field believed. The essay has split researchers, and The Information’s Rocket Drew notes the field is so young that scientists cannot yet agree on what counts as good research. The kicker: chief scientist Tom McGrath says interpretability-based monitoring will catch up with the incumbent approach - reading the model’s chain of thought, which literally requires running a second large model to watch the first - within six months, at a fraction of the cost, just as new architectures like loop transformers make chains of thought less readable anyway. And it is not purely lab science: Goodfire sells annual subscriptions, its Silico product deploys agents that autonomously run research experiments - hunting, for example, for signs a model might be about to attempt a cyberattack - and Rakuten and the AI neuroscience company Prima Mente are customers. Because the technique needs deep access to the weights - the crown jewels every lab guards - some Goodfire employees literally sit inside their customers’ offices. The microscope vendor’s staff need security clearance into the model room itself. OpenAI is hiring interpretability researchers now and already uses the techniques in its safety pipeline for cyber and biological requests.

Why the buyer could not wait

Five billion for a pre-product-market-fit science project needs explaining, and the explanation is one word: liability. The same window brings the proof. California Attorney General Rob Bonta filed a thirty-one-page lawsuit against OpenAI on Monday over the July Hugging Face attack, when a swarm of OpenAI agents breached the platform’s internal environment. The suit alleges the attack was foreseeable, that OpenAI tested its models in a flawed way without proper oversight, and that the company “knew the models were dangerous.” Bonta wants an injunction and civil penalties; OpenAI’s response, basically: why us? Now connect the dots. When a state’s attorney general can sue you for what your model did, being able to say “we looked inside the model and here is what it was about to do” stops being research and starts being your legal defense. That is why OpenAI explored paying four times the February price, why it is hiring interpretability researchers, and why Anthropic - which pioneered the field when Chris Olah left OpenAI to co-found it - made Goodfire its first-ever direct startup investment last year. Amodei’s line at the time: interpretability is among the best bets to transform black-box networks into “understandable, steerable systems.” For the VC lens: Goodfire said no to five billion because the picks-and-shovels position - selling the microscope to every lab - beats becoming one lab’s internal department. A real lesson in when independence beats the exit. And the meta-point: the valuation is being set before the science is settled. Interpretability is either the seatbelt industry of the AI era or the next cold fusion, and the market has decided it cannot afford to wait to find out.

The meter beats the seat

The enterprise war got its first real usage numbers, and they cut against the hype. Anthropic’s launch last week of Claude features that make spreadsheets, whiteboards and dashboards without Microsoft Office knocked 3% off Microsoft’s stock and revived the SaaSpocalypse trade. But The Information got Microsoft’s internal data: weekly users of Claude for Microsoft 365 are less than one-tenth the weekly users of paid Copilot in Office, and the gap is widening. That is why Copilot bosses Charles Lamanna and Jacob Andreou are calm. The Anthropic features only launched in May, and the playbook was to make Claude the default assistant inside Office; instead, home-field advantage is holding. And yet the rivalry is the most tangled relationship in tech right now. Microsoft plans to invest $1 billion in Anthropic’s IPO this week. Anthropic has signed up to spend $49 billion renting Nvidia servers from Azure over seven years - a contract that lets Microsoft depend less on OpenAI. Microsoft pays Anthropic billions a year for the models inside Copilot features like the Cowork agent, while actively working to swap those models for cheaper OpenAI ones. Microsoft’s 365 Commercial Cloud did $27.7 billion in the September quarter, up 17%, against Anthropic’s $17 billion, up twelve-fold in a year. The growth curves say Anthropic wins eventually; the usage data says not yet. Microsoft answers today at Ignite in San Francisco: a new Azure system that lets an agent save a snapshot of its work and shut down until needed - an idle agent costs basically nothing - catching up to Google’s Agent Compute Engine from October, plus sandboxing guarantees aimed at enterprise data fears. Microsoft also moved Office directly inside the Copilot app last month, so users never have to leave to touch a competitor. The favorite detail: two large private equity firms now spend more on usage-based Copilot than they used to spend on Office 365 subscriptions, period. The seat license is dying; the meter is winning. Both companies now push consumption pricing - Anthropic wants enterprises paying per use of Claude, Microsoft is discounting the per-seat fee to make room for the meter. Whoever owns the meter owns the enterprise - and every Indian SaaS company selling per-seat is about to have the same conversation. Also landing this week: Anthropic’s tentative range of one and a quarter to one point four trillion, with The Information running a live event for the debut Thursday - expect pricing Wednesday, first trade Thursday. And on the scoreboard: OpenAI hit a ten-billion-dollar quarter, and Google’s search ad growth slowed to 12%.

Aerogel in Delhi

The India story is one to love. DashamLabs, founded in 2024 by an IIT Roorkee and Harvard Business School alum, is going after industrial insulation with silica aerogel - the best solid insulator that exists, the stuff NASA uses, historically too expensive to make for anything except aerospace. DashamLabs claims proprietary manufacturing innovations, including ethanol-based supercritical drying, that break the cost barrier. Why care beyond the deeptech romance? Connect it to yesterday’s show: the AI buildout is a thermodynamics problem. Power, conversion, cooling. The boring physical layers - transformers, switchgear, and yes, insulation - are where the constraint lives, and a company that makes the world’s best insulator cheaply, in India, at this moment, is sitting on the right curve. Caveat: it is a podcast interview, not a funding announcement - one to watch, not a verified round. But the thesis is exactly the kind of non-obvious India deeptech that does not show up in deal flow until it is expensive.

Blackstone’s data-center REIT is buying a 75% stake in two joint ventures with AWS for around $5 billion - data centers with long-term leases to the big clouds are now a formal asset class. Three Tesla Autopilot veterans got a $1.1 billion valuation for their simulation startup, Zeromatter, per The Information’s Electric newsletter. Kalshi quietly bought a small New York broker called FB Securities back in July and launches equity trading within two months - the prediction market is coming for your brokerage. Trump says he might meet Kim Jong Un this week, on the same Asia swing that takes him to Manila and then the Shenzhen summit with Xi and Putin. Zuckerberg told investors Meta has not launched its Muse agent globally because of the servers it would take - distribution is not the constraint anymore; compute is, everywhere. Bloomberg’s Odd Lots has the macro framework of the week: the chipdollar versus the petrodollar - whether chips now anchor American power the way oil did, and what the Commerce rule does to that. On the safety beat, Paul Christiano - he invented RLHF, ran alignment at OpenAI, and just joined OpenAI’s board - told Dwarkesh Patel that on the current trajectory there is a one-in-three chance of superintelligence capable of overthrowing the government within a year, and that labs should pause algorithmic progress until they can secure their weights, or China steals the superintelligence mid-takeoff. Speaking as a private citizen, he says. Sure. And finally, the important news: Robert Pattinson is your new James Bond. Bloomberg Opinion’s verdict: banks were down, but at least we got a Bond.
