The elevator files its paperwork

Anthropic’s prospectus is expected within days, the listing next month, and it will likely be the largest IPO ever. The interesting part is not the size; it is the gap the roadshow must close. The company’s own survey says 91% of employees believe AI will produce a Nobel-level breakthrough by 2030. The American public: 39%. Inside the building, agent-hours already outnumber human hours six to one. Outside it, JPMorgan’s finance chief says the benefits of AI are not observable in quarterly earnings, and some investors who sat through the pre-IPO meetings decided a valuation floated near $2 trillion was a valuation too far. Anthropic is going public at the exact moment Wall Street faith in AI is at its yearly low. This is not really an IPO. It is a referendum on whose timeline is right, held in public, with real money.

The cap table is the second referendum, and it is about venture capital itself. Anthropic capped every investor deliberately: no firm owns more than 2%. The industry that passed on the weird safety company for years showed up late with all the cash it could carry, for slivers. The best venture outcome of the generation, and venture barely got any. The lesson for every firm, including Indian ones: access to the consensus deal is not a strategy, it is a fee.

The bottleneck is the business model

The AI buildout’s financing mood shifted this week, and the tell was who sounded relaxed. CoreWeave’s stock fell another 10% on Thursday and its bonds are sliding; Firmus pulled its IPO and needed an Nvidia backstop; SB Energy is thinking about December or later. Meanwhile Blackstone’s executives told analysts that power constraints and local opposition make new capacity hard to build, which makes existing data centers more valuable, and that this is where they earn their highest returns. Scarcity is the moat - if you already own the moat. The honest read and the cynical read are the same read: the people saying the buildout is fine are the people collecting the tolls. Into this mood walked OpenAI’s numbers: a $50 billion annualized pace, a $1 billion ads business that tripled its advertisers since August, and a quiet decision to shift 10% of compute - the scarcest resource in the industry - toward safety. Every safety percentage point is now a revenue trade.

Votes, repriced to zero

Matt Levine’s full essay on Meta’s Class C is titled Zuckerberg Wants to Keep His Votes, and it is the cleanest framing of what changed this week. Dual-class stock was a compromise: give up economics, keep control. The nonvoting share is not a compromise; it is the end of the conversation. Zuckerberg already has the votes. The Class C exists so Meta can raise new money forever without the control question being asked again - at exactly the moment it needs the most outside capital in its history. The market has repriced founder votes to zero, and it has done so willingly. That is not a loophole. That is the new listing standard.

Bloomberg, Money Stuff, Oct 22 (full text read).

The agent tollbooth

The most important enterprise story of the week got almost no headlines: from around November, Salesforce will require customers to register external AI agents and treat every agent action as a billable interaction. Seat pricing assumed headcount growth equals revenue growth; agents invert it - the customer gets more productive and the vendor gets poorer. So Salesforce, with Atlassian, Workday, ServiceNow and Datadog behind it, is building the tollgate before the traffic arrives. Whether the agent tax sticks decides what SaaS margins look like for a decade, and every Indian SaaS company selling per-seat into the US should read it as a preview of its next pricing negotiation. Adjacent: Datadog offered nearly $4 billion for Upwind, an Israeli startup watching cloud apps and agents, at roughly 80x ARR - richer than Google-Wiz - a year after trying at $1 billion. The fear premium is compounding faster than the revenue, and the buyers are paying for the guard dog before the gold rush is proven.
