The filing is the answer to Saturday

Anthropic’s S-1 could go public as early as today, and the timing could hardly be worse scripted: it lands the same week OpenAI is publicly waging a price war against the margins the filing must defend. The leaks have mined the headline numbers, so the document’s real content is what leaks cannot carry. The Information’s shopping list is the right one: the profitability trajectory in audited form; the partner economics - what Anthropic pays Google and Amazon, what they own, what those stakes are now worth; concentration, with two customers supplying a fifth of first-half revenue and API sales rising and falling on developer fashion; governance, which is genuinely strange - a Long-Term Benefit Trust of non-shareholders appoints a board majority, seven co-founders hold a planned 50.1% of votes, and employees get a tiebreaker share class; and lockups, possibly extended past 180 days with a secondary sale at listing that neither SpaceX nor Cerebras permitted. The week around it is loaded: Google and Microsoft report Wednesday, Amazon Thursday, Jassy is already saying AWS is largely sold out through next year, Apollo’s Slok says no non-tech company has seen any AI payoff, and Moonshot’s Kimi K3.1 Pro is the second Chinese frontier-grade model in ten days. Bloomberg Economics’ frame from the weekend is the one to carry into the filing: at $2 trillion, Anthropic’s warnings are the pitch. A warning is free. A filing is sworn.

The boom meets its insurers

Every layer of the AI buildout turns out to need a new financial instrument, and this week’s is insurance. The mechanism is the financing: when every party builds with borrowed money, a delay is not an inconvenience but a cascade, and tenants have started pricing that into contracts - late-delivery penalties quintupled in months, from $1 million to $5 million a day. Two months of slip is $300 million in penalties plus forgone rent, against debt that still needs servicing. KKR’s digital infrastructure head calls it: late penalties will bankrupt more developers than interest rates ever will. The market being born to absorb this is already twice the size of global aviation insurance - $10 billion of premiums this year, Swiss Re says $24 billion by 2030 - against $2 trillion of insurable value growing $300 billion a year, with single campuses at $20-30 billion and $50-60 billion per gigawatt all-in. No single insurer can hold one campus, so risk gets pooled, and S&P expects insurance-linked securities next. The telling gaps are where the assumptions are softest: nobody can yet price GPU residual-value coverage (the CoreWeave assumption), a 45-minute outage can void 40% of a year’s cash flow under five-nines terms, vermin coverage exists because rats eat fiber, and the one risk Marsh calls uninsurable is labor. The boom’s financing requires the insurance to exist; the insurance requires the penalties to be priced; the penalties are priced on AI revenue assumptions. Every number in the chain wears a hard hat.

India: the sellers and the exits

Two tapes ran in opposite directions this weekend. The foreign bid keeps leaving: Ajit Dayal tells The Core that stretched valuations, capital-gains taxation, rupee depreciation and governance - he calls 20-30% of the major indices uninvestable - outweigh the growth story, and the selling streak reached ten straight weeks. Growth alone, his line, cannot attract global capital. Pair it with Jitendra Gohil’s argument that a stronger economy does not guarantee stronger earnings and the picture is an expensive market held up by domestic SIPs. Meanwhile the exit window works anyway: Zetwerk listed Friday, popped 5.5%, and faded below its offer price by the close - retail subscribed just 0.73x - but Kae Capital banked a 38.3x return and its first-ever IPO, with Peak XV, Accel and Lightspeed taking partials between 11x and 38x. Secondaries, OFS-heavy IPOs, co-invest pools: everywhere, liquidity is being engineered rather than awaited. For Indian venture, Zetwerk says distributions are becoming a flow, not an event. The close below offer says who the market thinks buys the next one.

The Hackening

The AI-attack wave the Journal flagged Friday has a name now - Zvi Mowshowitz calls it the Hackening - and Noah Smith’s weekend essay (free, full read) lays out the mechanics. The internet runs on decades of human code full of old vulnerabilities; models are now good enough to find them and cheap enough to find all of them. The flashpoint: AI-discovered critical bugs in PHP, with Amazon saying attackers are actively exploiting flaws that could expose up to 70% of the world’s websites. Noahpinion quotes what it describes as an NSA advisory urging PHP users to patch or go offline - we have not seen the primary source and attribute it as newsletter-quoted. Azeem Azhar calls it the internet’s Wuhan moment. The durable point is the asymmetry: offense is now cheap, infinite and patient, and open-weight models can have their guardrails stripped. There is no vermin clause for a model that never sleeps.

Meta’s number, and quick hits

$825 billion. That is Meta’s disclosed pile of future leases and noncancelable contracts, revealed the morning after the Class C announcement; the stock fell 8%, free cash flow is negative in a record-revenue quarter, and the ad business carries the whole buildout (WSJ tech newsletter summary, labeled). Every hyperscaler call this week gets graded against that number: funded like Google, rented like CoreWeave, or mortgaged like Meta. Also on the tape: Apple’s first touchscreen MacBook expected at Wednesday’s event; Instacart closed up 9% Friday on the sale report, earnings Nov 5; Burry teased a price hike Saturday and reversed overnight - “come November 1, nothing at all will change”; the credit-card points wars escalate with lifetime bonus limits at Chase and Citi (WSJ, labeled); Havells and SRF stay under pressure at home; and the Economist scores Trumponomics’ winners and losers (not subscribed; headline only) as Trump tells Iran it is time to strike or deal.
