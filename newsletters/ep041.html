Scale is a strategy until the cost of money moves

US consumer prices rose 3.7% in September from a year earlier, above August's 3.4% and the 3.6% economists expected, the WSJ reports. The S&P 500 fell 1.1% and chip stocks extended a rout. The worst hit was CoreWeave, a neocloud: a young company that builds AI data centers and rents out computing power. It fell about 15% to roughly $65.

The cause is how it is funded. The Information says CoreWeave issued $4.2 billion of convertible notes last month and said it may sell up to 35 million shares over time. A convertible note is a loan that can turn into shares at a set conversion price, so the coupon is low. Illustrative arithmetic, not the real terms: a 2% coupon on $4.2 billion costs $84 million a year, against $336 million at 8%. The saving is large while the stock rises. If the conversion price were $120 and the shares trade at $65, the conversion option is worth little and the holder owns a low-rate loan to a company whose bonds are wobbling.

CEO Michael Intrator called the market irrational. Filings show he sold stock every week from April to the end of September, at $128 in May and $86 last month, against about $65 now. The company says co-founders are making ordinary financial planning decisions. Co-founder Brannin McBee told the FT the firm hopes to borrow from insurers and that "you have to be huge."

This is not really about one stock. It is about the difference between demand and financing. Anthropic reportedly told investors its Q3 revenue rose 46% to $16.8 billion from $11.5 billion (FT, via The Information, not audited). Demand is real. A landlord with fixed debt and rented capacity is still exposed to rates first. Bending Spoons shows the other side: it rose 41% to $53.46 after Berkshire Hathaway agreed to a $330 million placement at $33. Trust moves prices more than analysis does.

India's next 300 million were too expensive to serve

A newsletter from what reads as Lightspeed's India platform (LSIP; it names Bachatt as a Lightspeed portfolio company) argues that India's next 300 million users are not too poor. They are too expensive to serve. A bank helpline with a bench of agents per language does not work for most languages.

Voice AI changes the arithmetic. The author says a lost-card call cost a bank about 50 a year ago, about 20 by July, and about 11 per figures shown at Global Fintech Fest in September. The author guesses close to 2 a year from now. The email text has no currency symbol, so rupees are assumed. Illustrative: ten million such calls cost 50 crore at 50, 11 crore at 11 and 2 crore at 2. The two is a forecast, not a measurement.

The piece says platforms built for India's top 10 percent assume English first, text first and high order value first. IAMAI and Kantar data cited say 98% of internet users go online in an Indian language at least sometimes. Only about one in four workers draws a regular salary. The five principles: start from the constraint, solve for relevance not frequency, fit the cash flow, do it for them, and keep the question honest. Bachatt, which lets users save from about 50 a day, is the cash-flow example.

The implication is that cheaper models do not by themselves create a market. The moat moves to understanding the customer's constraint, trust and distribution.

Funds are getting bigger, and one partner is leaving to get smaller

Bloomberg's Tech In Depth reports that Lightspeed's Bucky Moore, who joined last year after seven years at Kleiner Perkins, is leaving to start his own fund, per people familiar. That runs against the trend. As round sizes grow, funds need to be bigger to own a meaningful share. Lightspeed manages more than $50 billion, Union Square Ventures doubled a fund from $275 million to $500 million, and Benchmark closed its first growth fund this year. An investor quoted calls himself a small fund believer and says the biggest funds now offer a different product from traditional venture capital.

Illustrative maths. A 5% stake at a $1 billion valuation costs $50 million. At $7.5 billion it costs $375 million. A $1.5 billion fund wanting three times its money needs $4.5 billion back, which needs giant outcomes. A $50 million fund needs $150 million. These are different businesses.

Newcomer separately reports that Indian VC firms are following founders to Silicon Valley. A Palo Alto lawyer says a dozen Indian founders a week now ask about moving to the US, more than triple two years ago, and Indian VCs have made over a hundred US investments in two years. Only the opening of that piece was available (paywalled for readers), so nothing beyond it is claimed.

Uber shows what an AI-ready company looks like

The Pragmatic Engineer's episode with Uber distinguished engineer Uday Kiran Medisetty is a tour of how a large company absorbed coding agents. Uber had about two services in 2015, thousands in 2019 and about the same number in 2026, though the business is roughly ten times larger. It built M3DB, a metrics store handling about 500 million metrics a second, and Cadence, the workflow engine behind Temporal.

In 2023 it made everything built with AI model-agnostic. A single Model Gateway switches between Anthropic, OpenAI, other vendors and open models, and carries a security check, per-user spend tracking and audit logs. An internal wrapper lets engineers switch between Claude Code, Codex and OpenCode without losing settings. Engineers get spend tiers, a plugin gives cost tips, and token costs are down about 50% in recent months.

The harder problems are downstream. Code review is a bottleneck. CI jobs more than doubled in twelve months, forcing a code red. Uber stopped measuring pull-request speed, because agents can produce hundreds, and now tags changes as feature, bug fix, maintenance, testing or docs. Medisetty's view is that engineering is now about building context for agents. The AI tooling looks new, but it sits on a decade of developer infrastructure.
