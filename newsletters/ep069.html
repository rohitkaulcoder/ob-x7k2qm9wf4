The indispensable man, out in four days

The consensus last weekend was that Jensen Huang was the one CEO in tech who could not be fired. He co-founded Nvidia, owns 3.6% of it, touches nearly every operational decision, and is the AI boom’s best salesman. Nvidia’s board met Wednesday night to consider his future anyway, per multiple reports cited by The Information. The turn came fast: three days ago the board announced an inquiry into whether Huang had been the target of a foreign intelligence effort and said it had confidence in him. Then on Wednesday the New York Times identified the woman at the center of the scandal as business executive Alice Huang, who US intelligence believes may be a Chinese spy; The Information’s Briefing says the Times also reported an affair with a company executive. The market did the math immediately: down about 10% Monday and Tuesday, then another 8% after hours Wednesday to around $160, a market cap near $3.85 trillion against a $5.8 trillion peak in early October. Two trillion dollars of value gone in five weeks. The pressure is now institutional: two House Republicans want a closed-door briefing with a subpoena in reserve, Baird cut its target from $500 to $200 in a rare downgrade, and Wachtell is running the independent investigation. There is no obvious internal successor, so the float list is AMD’s Lisa Su, Arm’s Rene Haas and, per D.A. Davidson’s Gil Luria, Intel’s Lip-Bu Tan. Nothing is proven, and Huang has not been accused of sharing information. The lesson does not need a verdict: founder genius is not a succession plan.

Old GPUs, new annuities

The CEO of the AI trade’s most important company may be on his way out, and the AI trade rallied. The Nasdaq 100 jumped 2.3% to 28,374, erasing the post-election selloff entirely; the S&P added 1.3% to 7,621. The driver was CoreWeave: third-quarter revenue of $3.66 billion, more than double the year-ago $1.37 billion, a raised forecast, and a 24% pop to about $60.40. Losses are still ballooning; this is not a clean story. But the earnings call contained the week’s most important number set. More than 130,000 CoreWeave GPUs come off contract in 2027, and roughly 170,000 more in 2028. At current pricing, re-renting the 2027 fleet generates approaching $4 billion of incremental annualized revenue with zero additional capex; including 2028, nearly $10 billion. Paid-off hardware printing annuity revenue. The credit market already believes it: last year’s data-center junk bonds, funding near-complete projects, have returned 7.9% while this year’s vintage is the mirror image, and Steph Choe’s team at Citi argues political construction delays make existing facilities more valuable. Silicon Data’s H100 rental index just hit a two-year high. The bears said GPUs depreciate like cars. Right now they price like oceanfront property, and more than half of CoreWeave’s new customers were not AI companies.

The toothless shadow government

Day three of the Anthropic roadshow: Bloomberg reports the IPO is now multiple times oversubscribed, with one analyst calling it the Jenga block holding up the AI trade. Pricing lands next week. The company also quietly added about $10 billion of cloud commitments with Microsoft and Google through 2033, and launched workplace apps for spreadsheets, presentations and notes on Tuesday, a direct shot at Microsoft, whose shares tanked on the announcement of a product from a still-private company. For investors doing diligence, The Information surfaces the governance question worth asking: the Long-Term Benefit Trust. Its trustees, a former Fed chair, a health policy wonk and a Harvard physics professor among them, pick their own successors, and the open fifth seat pays $400,000 a year. On paper the trust appoints and removes four of seven directors. In practice it can remove only three unilaterally; the fourth requires a board committee that includes co-founder Daniela Amodei, with an employee vote as the backstop, and the co-founders effectively pick three directors themselves. The founders hold the veto. The filing calls this collaboration. It is the opposite, and public investors should be glad: it is OpenAI’s 2023 board brawl, pre-loaded with the answer. The same filing now says Anthropic’s models can double its non-AI-assisted rate of capabilities progress, which formally triggers stronger safety commitments under its own policies. Remember that sentence for the next section.

The crank is turning

Two reports this week moved the people who measure AI risk, and one of them is the reason the filing language above matters. METR, an AI research nonprofit, got access to Anthropic’s internal data and found the company’s own AI use is now improving its models at roughly twice the previous speed. The AI Futures Project, whose AI 2027 forecast was once dismissed as fiction and has since called agents and AI-accelerated research correctly, moved its prediction for superhuman AI researchers up to late 2027, from 2029 or later. Daniel Kokotajlo, the ex-OpenAI researcher who runs the project, says we are at the beginning of the takeoff period. The mechanism is recursive self-improvement: today’s model helps design, train and debug the next one, which helps more, and each turn of the crank shortens the cycle. METR’s Ajeya Cotra warns the loop could produce systems that quickly develop novel weapons. The skeptics are not silent: DeepMind’s Sebastian Krier calls the measures flawed and notes rising costs are their own brake, and Princeton’s Arvind Narayan argues capabilities take time to reach society. Jacob Coxon, who quit Anthropic publicly in September, says the company still discloses too little. When the safety researchers and the IPO filing say the same thing in the same week, the signal is hard to wave off.

The stack buys its own chips

Two Information exclusives this week are one story: AI’s asset-light layer is vertically integrating under pressure. The inference startups, which rent GPUs and resell capacity, are being squeezed as clouds prioritize direct customers: CoreWeave reportedly stopped selling to Baseten after launching its own inference service (disputed by someone close to Baseten), DigitalOcean’s CEO says he favors real end-user demand over resellers, and this week’s Fireworks hack, caused by weak security at a small cloud it used, showed the risk of scrambling across the long tail of neoclouds, as Modal did with thirty new partners this year. The response is ownership. Fal is spending part of its fresh $1.65 billion raise on its own Nvidia chips and is exploring debt financing; Together plans 250 megawatts of its own capacity by the end of 2026; Baseten is shopping for GPUs. Iren’s Kent Draper calls the model the WeWork of the GPU world: it works until demand or financing dries up. Fireworks, past $1 billion of annualized revenue at a $17.5 billion valuation, disagrees and stays asset-light. One level up, Sequoia is investing hundreds of millions into Sequence Holding, a one-year-old AI holding company that just announced a $7.7 billion take-private of insurance broker Baldwin Group, co-led by Michael Dell’s family office. Sequence’s playbook, buy boring businesses and rebuild them with AI, is Sequoia’s second bet in the category after Ithaca’s $1.3 billion Bumble deal, and the quoted return math is four times the money, not venture’s ten. Predictable cash is suddenly worth more than theoretical multiples. Venture capital is quietly becoming private equity with better marketing.

The fee is the product

How do you actually charge for an AI rebuild? The Information Finance profiled Ode, Anthropic’s PE-backed venture with $1.5 billion from Blackstone and Hellman & Friedman. Most of its several dozen clients pay a fixed fee and, critically, keep the software, the opposite of Anthropic’s own embedded team, which wants to own and license back. On top, Ode is pushing performance fees tied to financial targets; under 10% of clients pay them today because agreeing on a baseline is brutal, but that is the direction. The case study: LogicGate paid a low-seven-figure fee for a custom agent that cut client onboarding from 120 days to 90 or less, owns the IP, and is now selling the agent to other businesses. Ode’s inherited revenue from Fractional AI has nearly tripled since May toward $90 million annualized, and it could take two to three times more work if it had the engineers. It does not. Neither does anyone: Anthropic is spending $100 million to train 10,000 deployment engineers, and Microsoft committed $2.5 billion to embed 6,000. The constraint in AI services is not demand or models; it is people who can ship. Related: Bloomberg Opinion’s Jonathan Levin notes corporate America now calls AI layoffs “delayering,” and the euphemism means nobody can measure the labor market’s biggest shift in generations. When a CEO says delayering on an earnings call, translate it as AI took the middle-management layer, and ask what the severance line hides.
