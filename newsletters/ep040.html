The model is the easy part

Tanay Jaipuria of Wing writes that Anthropic and OpenAI have both started deployment companies, DeployCos: separately funded firms that embed engineers inside customers to get AI agents working in real workflows. Anthropic's is a $1.5 billion services firm set up with Blackstone, Hellman and Friedman, Goldman Sachs and other private equity firms. It launched in July as Ode with Anthropic, built on the acquisition of Fractional AI, and starts with the sponsors' portfolio companies. OpenAI's raised over $4 billion, led by TPG, is majority-owned by OpenAI, and began with the acquisition of Tomoro and more than 100 forward-deployed engineers. AWS committed $1 billion to a similar unit in June.

Two reasons explain the move. The first is the diffusion gap: models can do more valuable work than companies use them for. The newsletter cites OpenAI's GDPval, where models beat experts on over half the tasks graded, while most organisations are nowhere near that level of use. The second is organisation. Access to systems, redesigned workflows and buy-in cut across teams, and are nobody's job. Accenture has booked about $11.5 billion of generative AI work in three years. Palantir has accelerated revenue growth for 12 quarters.

The labs have private reasons too. Deployment generates traces of how real work gets done. Model performance is close enough that being embedded creates lock-in. And running the agent lets you charge for outcomes, not tokens.

This is not really about consulting. It is about where margin lives once models converge. Illustrative arithmetic: 100 engineers at $300,000 a year cost $30 million. A flat $40 million fee leaves $10 million, a 25% margin, and revenue grows with headcount. A software product costing $10 million to run, with ten customers at $4 million, earns $40 million at a 75% margin, and each added customer is nearly free. Services and software get different multiples. Many vertical AI companies selling to big enterprises are arguably DeployCos, as the newsletter says.

Chips are now improved by how they are joined

The WSJ's AI and Business newsletter says better AI chips increasingly depend on how silicon is laid out and stitched together, not only on cramming in transistors. Moore's Law, the old doubling of transistors every two years, has weakened. TSMC kept advancing but with smaller jumps in density, and the newest manufacturing is expensive.

A chip is a die cut from a wafer. Advanced packaging joins several smaller pieces, chiplets, into one package. Illustrative yield maths: put twenty random defects on a wafer. Cut it into 60 large dies and about 17 are hit, leaving roughly 43 good. Cut it into 240 dies a quarter the size and about 19 are hit, leaving about 221 good, or about 55 large-chip equivalents. That is around 28% more usable silicon from the same wafer, and small pieces can be tested before they are joined. The WSJ says AMD and Intel moved this way first, joining pieces made with older, cheaper technology.

AI raised the stakes. Nvidia's chips combine heavy computation with high-bandwidth memory in one package, and the WSJ says they would not have been possible without advanced packaging. Packaging capacity has been a bottleneck for Nvidia's sales. TSMC has converted old factories and raised capacity several times. Future designs stack silicon vertically. Memory sits close because moving data costs time and energy, and the stacking idea is a general explanation, not a claim in the newsletter.

The business point is simple. A bottleneck gives pricing power. Packaging was a low-margin, low-innovation step. Now TSMC is doing more of it in-house, and Applied Materials' CEO said in August that packaging-related revenue would grow over 70% this year. TSMC reports on Thursday. Nothing in the overnight sources shows an Indian company in this layer.

Agents are breaking things, and exits are changing shape

The Information reports that the Justice Department says an OpenAI engineer, now arrested, planted a recording device behind a TV in a conference room used for confidential discussions. The DOJ identified him as a Saudi national. These are allegations. The same newsletter lists an exclusive on OpenAI trying to fix safety issues before its next flagship model, which was seen only as a headline.

On agents, the WSJ quotes startup CEO Shane Mac saying his AI agent leaked his personal bank balances into a company chat. JPMorgan's Jamie Dimon said the bank will block outside AI agents, including Meta's Muse and Instinct, from client accounts, calling it too risky for clients and the bank.

Character.AI filed for bankruptcy on Monday. Its investors had cashed out in 2024 when Google paid $2.7 billion to license its models and rehire its co-founders. Disney hired its CEO as CTO and bid $20 million for its IP in the bankruptcy. Microsoft with Inflection and Amazon with Adept did similar deals, structured as licences to avoid antitrust scrutiny. The lesson is that the investor return can be real when the company is not. Adept's website has not been updated since mid-2024.

Cheap intelligence, built fast

Bloomberg's Odd Lots newsletter revives Tracy Alloway's coffee pod theory: many buyers will take a cheap pod machine, open models, over a $1,000 espresso machine, closed frontier models. A $25,000 BYD against a $50,000 Tesla is another version. China seems to have picked the cheap side. Closed-source barely appears in its 2025 AI Plus Action Plan.

Barclays analysts add that China's data centers are industrialised: prefabricated parts, factory-built modules and standard designs. Alibaba's 100-Day Datacenter model is 30 days of prefabrication and site prep, 50 days of installation and 20 days of commissioning. Alloway reads this as a bet on the whole economic model: manufacture and deliver commoditised intelligence at lower cost.

The WSJ's OpenRouter chart, covering about 120,000 companies, largely AI-native startups, shows Anthropic and OpenAI spending allocations close to even over the past month, after OpenAI released cheaper models in June. Cheaper has beaten better again. The same newsletter notes that Firmus, an Australian cloud company with two operating data centers, scrapped its IPO after seeking a $30 billion valuation.

Bloomberg Odd Lots newsletter, Oct 13. WSJ AI & Business, Oct 13.

Bumble closes, and the rollup thesis shows up

Yesterday's talks became a deal. Ithaca Holdings said it will buy Bumble at a $1.3 billion valuation, $6.05 a share, one-seventh of the $43 IPO price. Ithaca co-CEO Ravi Gupta said Bumble stood out from hundreds of companies reviewed as one that would become more valuable as AI advances. The Information is sceptical. Bumble could adopt AI without a sale. Its revenue fell 10% last year and 15% in the first half, and at June 30 it had $450 million of debt against $154 million of cash.

Tanay's DeployCos piece ends on AI-native rollups, which buy a business and infuse AI from within. Long Lake closed a $6.3 billion take-private of Amex GBT. Sequence took Baldwin private for $7.7 billion. Owning the asset lets you redesign processes and keep the upside, at the cost of capital and scale. Bumble is the same thesis at a smaller price. Whether the AI explains the deal, or the debt does, is the question.
