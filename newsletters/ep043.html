This is a sorting, not a pop

The AI trade had its first honest repricing this week. Firmus Grid, an Australian former bitcoin miner backed by Coatue, Jane Street and Blackstone, pulled its IPO after investors balked. The numbers explain why. It sought a valuation around $30 billion on $59 million of fiscal 2026 revenue. It has delivered 46 megawatts of a 912 megawatt pipeline. Its CEO once served time for insider trading. Blackstone-backed AirTrunk postponed a roughly $1.5 billion data centre REIT in Singapore. CoreWeave, the category's archetype, is wobbling as rates rise, and Anthropic's newly released financials did not settle anyone's nerves.

Yet into the same week walks DayOne Data Centers, a Singapore operator seeking up to $5 billion in a New York IPO. DayOne is, for now, a ByteDance wrapper with a power bill: 69.2% of first-half revenue from one customer. But read the prospectus the other way. None of that ByteDance revenue is AI. It is video serving and e-commerce, contracted for years, with 838 megawatts committed across Malaysia, Thailand and Finland. Oracle is customer two at 15% and growing. In 2027 forward contracts, ByteDance falls below half.

The juxtaposition is the story. The market is not rejecting data centres. It is rejecting the financing structures wrapped around them: thirty billion dollars of hope on fifty-nine million of revenue fails, while boring contracted cash flow with a concentration problem gets a hearing. Carnage for the structures. Not for the demand.

The best latency is none

Neil Movva of Sail Research tells Substrate that 95% of inference could eventually be asynchronous. The claim sounds like hype until you walk the mechanics. Inference has two phases. Prefill processes your whole prompt in parallel and is compute-bound: the GPU loads its weights once and spreads that cost across thousands of tokens. Decode generates the answer one token at a time, and every token requires reading all of the model's weights from memory again. Decode is memory-bound. The chip waits on memory; the maths is nearly free. Compute has improved faster than memory for decades because logic and DRAM are different industries with different fabs. That gap is the tax on everything.

The escape is batching. If the weights must be loaded anyway, let hundreds of requests share one load. Illustratively: if one weight-read costs one unit of time, one interactive user gets one token per unit; 256 batched requests get roughly 256. Cost per token collapses. But batching's enemy is latency, and a human watching a chat window will not wait for 255 companions. Interactive traffic forces small batches and idle chips.

Hence the thesis. The coming workload is background agents running for hours with no human in the loop. No latency requirement means aggressive batching, off-peak scheduling, and cheaper hardware: Sail builds on Nvidia's consumer-derived RTX Pro Blackwell with ordinary GDDR, sidestepping the HBM shortage entirely. It also means different real estate: sub-megawatt modular sites wherever power is cheap, instead of 20-megawatt monoliths. SemiAnalysis estimates 30% of US capacity could be modular by end-2028. If even half of this lands, inference splits into two assets with different economics, and the market is currently pricing them as one.

The optimist has penciled in the ending

Bloomberg frames the week as a debate between two chip bosses. Broadcom's Hock Tan, whose $1.6 trillion market value rests on the AI spurt he says is a unique event in a mature industry, told a Singapore audience that many AI investors have no clue what they own and are effectively in a casino. He noted, dryly, that his customer OpenAI loses money because of what it spends.

Nvidia's Jensen Huang, asked in Stockholm about CoreWeave, said no bailout is needed. Then he added the sentence worth keeping: a bubble happens when supply exceeds demand, supply probably will exceed demand at some point, but not today. Both men now agree on the shape of the ending. They disagree on the date. Position accordingly: when the person selling the picks starts discussing the end of the gold rush, the interesting money moves to what gets built with the picks already sold.

Apple is pricing the cost of not opening

The Justice Department is negotiating a settlement of its 2024 antitrust suit against Apple, and developers fear a soft deal. PayPal and Epic Games have both taken their concerns to the department. The talks centre on interoperability: smartwatches that pair as smoothly as Apple's, tap-to-pay that works as cleanly with rivals. Officials have asked developers for lists of improvements.

The politics point one way. Aggressive antitrust chief Gail Slater clashed with the administration's dealmaking instincts and left in February. Her interim successor prefers settlements as faster and cheaper than trial. The permanent nominee awaits confirmation, which may be all that slows the deal. Apple, meanwhile, has form: it offered interoperability promises to avoid the suit in 2024, and it was found last year to have willfully violated the Epic injunction. Watch what the settlement excludes. The App Store toll booth is not on the table. Interoperability concessions cost Apple little; that is precisely why they are the currency of the deal.

The internet is saying no to agents

Cloudflare data cited by a16z shows the share of 403 Forbidden responses served to agents and bots has roughly doubled since summer. Amazon and fellow etailers are blocking agent shoppers outright. The reasons are not mysterious. Returns have doubled and return costs tripled since 2019, dispute fraud is the top complaint, and agentic shopping threatens both the customer relationship and the advertising eyeballs that subsidise the platforms. The agentic commerce wave is real. So is the wall. Expect the first durable wins where the merchant gains more than it loses, not where the consumer's agent is just another scraper with a wallet.
