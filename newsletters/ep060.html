The product expires like milk

Anthropic’s amended S-1 is really two documents. The first describes the best-executed scale-up on record: revenue from $376M a quarter to $17.0B in under two years, up 47% in Q3 alone, net dollar retention around 900%, GAAP profitable in Q2, and under $10B of lifetime burn to reach a $65B run-rate with $131.7B still in the bank. The second document is the obligation that pays for the encore: roughly $657B of signed compute commitments, $417B of them uncancellable, with total planned compute spending near $900B. The mechanism worth understanding is the shelf life. Models released in the last six months produced over 80% of Q3 revenue. A frontier model is not a factory; it is fresh produce. That makes training spend less like R&D and more like cost of goods sold, and it makes the forward commitments a bet that the harvest keeps selling at today’s pace through 2028 and beyond. Reported gross margin is 85%; adjusted for free-tier inference and the roughly 35% toll on the near-half of revenue sold through AWS, Google and Microsoft’s stores, the honest range is 45-64%. Either way the machine works today. The question the IPO prices is whether it works at nine hundred billion.

Someone’s contract is someone else’s trap

Notice who is being asked to hold the other side of that bet. Nvidia is in talks to backstop a cloud provider’s $45B Anthropic project. Blackstone anchored a $63B chip financing and is already telling its own investors to expect a high junk rating on Anthropic’s debt after the listing, expected the week of November 16. Meanwhile the buyers of the equity are talking the price down: institutional investors are pushing for $1.5-1.6T against the $2T figures floated this fall, citing choppy markets and AI overexposure. The asymmetry is the story. Anthropic’s compute is locked in for years; its revenue is not. Contracted backlog is about $14B against a $65B run-rate, 80% of revenue is pay-per-token API usage, and Replit switched model providers in an afternoon. Enterprise customers, per The Information, are resisting larger annual commits this renewal season precisely because models keep leapfrogging each other - and the vendors’ own cheaper tiers (Haiku, OpenAI’s Terra and Luna) keep cannibalizing the premium ones. Anthropic wants non-cancellable multi-year commits from customers at exactly the moment customers want none. One side of this negotiation will end up trapped in the other’s contract. OpenAI’s trimmed raise ($30B at $1.4T cut to $20B at $1.25T, only ~$11B committed) says the market has started guessing which. For everyone building on these APIs, including India’s app-layer startups, the subsidy continues: a price war between richly funded suppliers is the best input-cost curve in software.

The Ternus discount

John Ternus’s first earnings call as Apple CEO was engineered to be boring, and the numbers obliged: September-quarter revenue up 12% to $114.5B, gross margin 47.9%, both ahead. Then the CFO guided December growth to 7-10% and warned margins will fall on memory costs, and the stock dropped 4%. Three tells sit under the print. iPhone’s 17% surge in a dead quarter was pull-forward - buyers chose the cheaper 2025 model over the barely-better 18 Pro. China missed. And Apple can no longer dictate chip volumes to its suppliers, because Nvidia is taking the capacity: the AI buildout is now large enough to push around the largest company on earth. Meanwhile Aaron Tilley’s account of the J595 home robot reads as the era’s parable: nine years, hardware finished for two, perhaps ten true roboticists, in-house AI so thin the team was cleared to use open-source and Google models, a launch that could slip to 2028 at about $1,000 - while OpenAI’s Jony Ive device team of 400 ex-Apple engineers and Meta’s December-shipping Charm move. Apple is a finely tuned machine for iteration. The question the Ternus era opens with is whether it can still invent.

Election day, nothing called

America votes today, and the discipline matters more than the prediction: as of recording, no race is called. Polls open this evening IST, the first closes land early Wednesday morning our time, California closes after tomorrow’s episode, and close races may take days or weeks. The stakes for tech are the ones mapped yesterday: 36 governor races with data-center construction live, pause candidates competitive in up to 15, 62 GW of planned 2028 capacity in voting states, and California’s Proposition 40 billionaire tax. Bloomberg’s framing: the midterms could upend the political climate that has underwritten the AI buildout - a roadshow risk for Anthropic priced in real time. The texture: a Michigan Democrat endorsing the Republican Senate nominee, a 30-year statewide drought on the line in Texas, South Carolina suddenly competitive, and the administration asking the Supreme Court to set aside 20,000 California mail ballots. Democrats need four Senate seats and three House seats. Everything you read tomorrow morning, including from us, is partial.

The bill reprices

The financing layer of the AI trade had its worst day in weeks. Oracle fell 9% kicking off a $20B debt-and-equity raise. Meta is lining up at least $20B of bonds. Alphabet sold €6B at meaningfully wider yields than May - the credit market has started pricing the boom like a boom. And Marvell’s all-stock bid for Lumentum became the cautionary exhibit: management claimed an “implied” $115B value using its own pre-leak share price; at Monday’s close the offer is worth $771 a share, under $80B, and Marvell fell 12.7% on its own announcement while Lumentum closed below the offer. The acquirer dropped nearly thirteen percent on its own deal announcement. Worth remembering: Lumentum went from under $200 to $1,100 to $840 in a year. That round trip is the AI trade’s heart rate monitor, and the market is checking the patient’s pulse more often now.

Binance becomes a bank

The post-mortem on the $6.3B Binance heist is more interesting than the heist. Tether and Circle froze about $3B of the stolen stablecoins, $1.8B of it within forty minutes; Binance covers the remaining $3.3B from its SAFU fund and reserves, and is buying back $1.3B of the stolen coins in the open market. Customers whole. Matt Levine’s point lands: the freeze mechanism is a central bank reversing a wire with extra steps - blacklist the thief’s addresses, void the frozen tokens as claims, mint fresh ones against the freed cash. Crypto spent fifteen years designing against reversible transactions, then rebuilt reversal the first time the amounts got serious. The thieves, meanwhile, were so rushed to dump stablecoins before the freezes that they paid an average $2,657 for Ether trading at $2,300 elsewhere - quant firms on the other side skimmed about $119M. The largest heist in crypto history, and its clearest lesson is that the system now works like the one it was built to replace.

Compute gets a ticker, plus quick hits

The logical endpoint of nine hundred billion in fixed-price compute contracts is a futures market, and it is being built: Ornn is raising at up to $1B (Thrive, General Catalyst) to broker GPU capacity, Silicon Data publishes an hourly rental index from a hundred sources, and CME and ICE both have compute futures designed - stuck at the CFTC on the honest question of whether a depreciating chip can underwrite a commodity contract. The index, not the hardware, is the answer, which is why the data company may matter more than the brokers. Quick hits: Bengaluru’s Cent AI raised $15M from Blume Ventures and South Park Commons, part of a five-deal healthcare AI Series A cluster this week (ClarityCare, Vera Health, Kivo, and Pune’s Clinics On Cloud). OpenAI signed Magnite and PubMatic to fill unused ChatGPT ad space. Goldman is restricting bankers’ AI tools while BofA and Morgan Stanley DMCA-strike FinTwit. Brent fell 8% as Iran’s president got authority to pursue peace talks.
