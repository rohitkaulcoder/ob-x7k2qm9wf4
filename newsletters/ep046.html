The reserves are flat because the gold is up

India’s September CPI printed 1.5%, an eight-year low, with food and beverage prices actually falling. Exports grew 6.8% in the first full month of US tariffs: America down 12%, UAE up 25%, China up 30%, electronics up 50%. The rerouting is no longer a forecast. But the same data has the tell. Imports jumped 17%, the trade deficit hit $32 billion, and the FX reserves only look stable because of accounting. Since June, the RBI’s foreign currency assets fell $23 billion, the quiet cost of defending the rupee, while the value of its gold holdings rose $18 billion on price alone. The war chest looks full because the gold bars got marked up.

This is not really about India. Gold at $4,347 now rises on risk-on days and risk-off days alike; its forty-year relationship with real rates broke in 2022 and never came back. Raghuram Rajan’s explanation on Odd Lots is the cleanest: when the financial system can be weaponized, everyone from Moscow to Ottawa wants an asset with no counterparty. India is running that hedge by accident, selling dollars to hold the rupee while its gold pays the bill. A central bank thinking like an Indian household. One caveat from the same source: low inflation alone will not cut rates. The RBI just raised its growth estimate, so unless growth weakens, 1.5% CPI is a curiosity, not a catalyst.

Margins are beating demand

The first week of earnings season, 217 companies: revenue up 9%, EBITDA up 12%, profit up 11%, and 14% excluding financials. The composition is the story. Dixon’s profit rose 80% on electronics demand; Vikram Solar’s rose sixteen-fold on the solar manufacturing push; commodities posted 77% profit growth on pure operating leverage; Infosys grew 13% on AI and cloud deals. Meanwhile FMCG profits fell 12% on input costs despite double-digit revenue growth. Growth is broadening, but margins, not demand, are deciding winners. That is what an economy moving up the manufacturing stack looks like from inside an income statement.

India Data Hub, This Week in Earnings, Oct 19.

Australia bulldozed the ratchet

Asianometry’s history of AWA is the week’s essential read. For most of a century, AWA was Australia’s largest electronics company: Marconi’s local arm, half of the country’s international telegraph by 1929, a million vacuum tubes a year through the Depression, the only Australian-owned transistor maker, a lab doing early solar and optical fiber work, its first IC in 1967. Then the 1972 Whitlam tariff cut killed the labor-intensive lines overnight. AWA retreated up the stack and, in 1988, opened a 6-inch, 1.2-micron fab, ahead of TSMC at the time, that made the chip for the cochlear implant. Then a 23-year-old FX trader lost $50 million, and in 1996 the weakened company sold the profitable fab for $11 million to fund a buyback. The fab ended with Peregrine’s Mars-rover chips, then a small LED firm, until the NSW government took the land for a metro line in 2019. It closed in 2021, the year of the global chip shortage.

On Monday, the US and Australia signed an $8.5 billion rare earths pact, and Australia returned to the strategic table as the quarry. National tech capability is a ratchet: policy cranks it up slowly, tariffs, patient ownership, one lab at a time, and it spins back to zero in a decade of small bad decisions. Delhi, mid-semiconductor-mission, should read this as the ghost of Christmas future.

Print the arrogance score on the PPM

Josh Kopelman’s venture arrogance score, surfaced by Sajith Pai: a $7 billion fund owning 10% at exit needs $70 billion of value created per turn, $280 billion for a 4x gross, and raised every three years that is $90 billion of exits a year, against $180 billion a year of total US venture exits in the best decade ever. The model requires capturing half of all value created in America. No fund has ever repeatedly captured ten percent. The useful part is not the snark; it is that two numbers, fund size and terminal ownership, fully determine how much of the world a fund has to eat. For India, where annual exits are a fraction of America’s, the same arithmetic argues for small funds, high ownership, or an explicit bet that exits grow an order of magnitude. Choose deliberately.

Sajith Pai’s Newsletter, Oct 19, quoting First Round’s Josh Kopelman on the Uncapped podcast.

Adoption is plateauing where it matters

The Ramp AI Index, built from actual business spend, shows paid AI adoption declined in September, the second decline of the year. Tech is at 73% and plateauing; retail is at 34%, construction 28%, food service 22%. The silver lining is retention, up from about 60% annualized in 2023 to over 80% now: those who adopt, stay. Spend is deepening narrowly, Anthropic toward a $9 billion run rate, OpenAI past $20 billion, with a freshly published top-30 customer list of trillion-token accounts. Combined with Friday’s finding that the ten smartest models carry 14% of developer tokens, down from 55%, the shape is clear: the boom needs production workloads, not more pilots, and OpenAI reaching for a TikTok-style app is what a plateau smells like from inside.

Kyle Poyar, Growth Unhinged, Oct 19.
