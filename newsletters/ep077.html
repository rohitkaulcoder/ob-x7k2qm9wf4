The opening was designed, and it worked

Anthropic’s first trade printed at $111.50 at 11:48 AM Eastern - 108 million shares in the opening auction alone, a 34% pop off the $83 price. It briefly touched up 39%, a two-trillion-dollar company for a few minutes, then faded to close at $97.17, up 17%: a $1.76 trillion fully diluted market cap for a company founded in 2021. Textbook, almost to the decimal - the banks target a twenty percent pop and got nearly that. The mechanics deserve attention because they explain the afternoon fade. Roughly 1,200 institutions bid; more than half got zero shares, allocations skewed to US funds, and about 15% of the deal went to retail. So hundreds of the world’s biggest money managers spent the morning holding empty allocations and watching the stock open up a third - then did exactly what the machine is designed to make them do: buy in the open market. The banks’ cut: $550 million in fees. The seven co-founders, who personally sold 23.6 million shares at $83, are worth more than $140 billion on paper. The IPO itself raised about $81 billion on 956.4 million new shares; the final tally with the simultaneous private placement cleared $107 billion, topping SpaceX’s five-month-old record.

The must-own problem

Levine framed the real significance cleanly: Anthropic is only about 2% of the US stock market, but it is the first pure-play frontier lab you can buy, and the market is now basically the AI trade - so every professional investor on earth needed an opinion by Thursday morning. Craig Coben at the FT heard the same phrase over and over from investors with reservations about the valuation: it is a must-own. Not from conviction - from symmetry. Being underweight the defining company of the era is the riskiest position of all. Nir Kaissar at Bloomberg Opinion took the other side: it is fine to pass; you already own Anthropic through Amazon and Alphabet. Both are right, which is the tell. And the VCs behaved like the dip buyers of their own exit: Sequoia and Iconiq planned to buy more at the IPO, Lightspeed and Accel too, and Altimeter’s Brad Gerstner told CNBC he aimed for $5 billion - he got a partial fill and kept buying in the aftermarket. Iconiq and Lightspeed each held stakes worth over $27 billion before trading started. The entry math explains it: Lightspeed led at $61.5 billion last year, Altimeter and Iconiq came in at $183 billion in the fall, Sequoia only got in this February at $380 billion. When your cost basis triples inside a year and it still prints $1.76 trillion on day one, the IPO price reads as a discount. IPO advisor Lise Buyer raised the right eyebrow: venture was supposed to be the unproven asset class. Inside the building, Hill - a marketplace for private-company shares - estimates 2,800 employees hold stakes worth at least $10 million and 600 hold more than $100 million. They could sell 10% of their shares on day one; the rest is locked until full-year earnings early next year, and the VCs mostly wait until mid-next-year. The S-1 says regrettable attrition ran at 1%. The retention question of the decade starts now.

Valuation, checked two ways

The bull comp: fully diluted, Anthropic is roughly Broadcom-sized. Analysts expect Broadcom to generate $175 billion of revenue in its fiscal year ending October 2027, up 65%. Anthropic is projected at $127.7 billion for calendar 2027, more than double this year. On growth-adjusted revenue, defensible. The catch is the shape of the cash: Broadcom is a net producer; Anthropic has committed to spend most of its cash on compute for years. One is a bond with a growth story, the other is a promise. The market handed out two discount coupons on Thursday and ignored both: Zoom’s Anthropic stake was worth about $4.9 billion at the IPO price, roughly 20% of Zoom’s own market cap, and Amazon’s stake is worth around $338 billion, about 12% of Amazon. Both stocks fell on the day. The tape’s mood matched: the S&P 500 fell 2% on Anthropic’s coronation, and Tracy Alloway flags that tech and consumer staples have run neck-and-neck all year under a calm index - the same rotation pattern as late 2021, the Tarantino market, right before the 2022 bear. The IPO popped; the market sulked. SB Energy and Nscale both refreshed stalled IPO filings this week, but Class V’s Lise Buyer’s line holds: this doesn’t open the floodgates unless you look like Anthropic.

TSMC’s memory gambit

The night’s most under-discussed story: The Information reports TSMC is drawing up plans to make 3D DRAM - at Nvidia’s direct request. TSMC does not make memory. It tried in the nineties - fabbing DRAM for Fujitsu in 1996, and incubating Vanguard as a DRAM maker - and the memory bust nearly killed Vanguard and wiped out Taiwan’s whole DRAM industry; Inotera and Rexchip ended up inside Micron, ProMOS sold for parts. By 2005 memory was under 1% of revenue, and the culture settled: logic means steady profits, memory is a commodity casino. For twenty years Samsung and SK Hynix have owned that casino. So why now? The effort is led by VP Hsu Kuo-chin, formerly COO of Micron’s Taichung fabs, and Nvidia has been partnering since September with d-Matrix, a startup selling stacked compute-on-DRAM for cheaper inference. The conservative reading is packaging - stacking memory wafers with logic dies, which TSMC already does better than anyone. The aggressive reading, which the report explicitly floats - buying an existing fab or building new ones - is a bet-the-fab decision, and the physics explains the stakes. A DRAM cell is one transistor plus a capacitor, and the capacitor is a hole one to three micrometers deep but twenty to thirty nanometers wide - high-aspect-ratio etching, among the hardest tricks in the industry, and not something TSMC’s fabs do. The elegant escape route: chip making’s front end bakes silicon transistors at up to 1,000 degrees Celsius, but the back end’s copper wiring can only tolerate 400 - so nothing silicon can be built after the wiring. Oxide semiconductors like IGZO deposit below 400 degrees, which means memory cells can be grown on top of finished logic, layer after layer. Three oxide-memory families are in play: a straight oxide DRAM cell (TSMC’s Katherine Chiang demoed one for cache in June 2025), a capacitor-less gain cell (IMEC’s long-running program - and TSMC listens to IMEC), and the exotic ferroelectric FET, a single transistor that stores state in its gate polarization and survives power-off. If any of these industrialize, the story is not TSMC becoming Hynix. It is TSMC inventing a memory that grows out of its own process, on top of logic, for AI workloads - and the memory wall starts to crack.

Nvidia buys speed

Even mid-leadership-crisis, Nvidia’s deal machine keeps humming: it is in advanced talks to acquire Inception, a two-year-old Bay Area startup, for at least $500 million plus earnouts - a deal that began before Jensen Huang departed, after Microsoft had expressed interest in something similar. Inception builds diffusion language models. Standard models like ChatGPT and Claude are autoregressive - they write left to right, one token at a time. Diffusion models generate the entire answer at once as noise, then refine it in passes, the way image generators work - and the payoff is speed, especially on long responses. The founders are serious: Stefano Ermon (Stanford), Aditya Grover (UCLA) and Volodymyr Kuleshov (Cornell), all expected to join Nvidia full-time; Inception had raised $50 million from Menlo, M12, Mayfield and Nvidia itself. The strategic read is Nemotron, Nvidia’s open model family - the one Booz Allen, Palantir and US agencies use precisely because it is neither Chinese nor closed. Nvidia is spending billions assembling model talent exactly as Anthropic and OpenAI design their own chips to escape Nvidia’s GPUs. Everyone is climbing into everyone else’s lane at the same time.

The discount war

Now Anthropic has to earn the valuation, and the under-the-hood reporting shows both the ramp and the strain. Revenue hit $4.7 billion in Q1 - more than all of last year - then grew 144% in Q2, then 47% in Q3 to $17 billion. Concentration is the problem: the top 5% of enterprise customers on Claude Code spend about twenty times more per month than customers without it. So the post-IPO story is diversification: Claude Cowork for business users, Claude Managed Agents in public beta, tailored tools for seven sectors, and a cybersecurity push where one partner says clients moved 10-20% of their Anthropic budget from coding to vulnerability scanning. And they are buying the land-grab: credits up to $100,000 for expanding customers, $2,000-per-user trial credits, discounts above 30% for big commitments - and share-of-wallet clauses requiring customers to earmark a percentage of total AI spend for Anthropic, with invoices produced to prove compliance. The six-week-old public company is auditing your AI budget. The reason: OpenAI’s Greg Brockman says cybersecurity will be bigger than coding for them too, OpenAI has offered 40% discounts to flip coding customers to Codex, and FedEx, Robinhood, Coinbase and Shopify have built internal harnesses to swap between models at will. Meanwhile the incumbents weaponize the same moment: ServiceNow’s new AI tool cuts migrating off Salesforce’s CRM from six months to six weeks, and Oracle, SAP and Workday are shipping their own switching tools. AI is making every enterprise software contract negotiable.
