Parallel thought becomes infrastructure

OpenAI used 10,000 agents, 130 billion tokens and 88 hours on Navier-Stokes. The striking number is not the headcount. It is the cognitive budget.

A swarm needs decomposition, routing, shared memory and evaluation. Some agents search literature. Some try proofs. Others attack weak points or combine useful fragments. Evaluators stop dead branches and allocate more compute to promising ones. The result is a tournament tree, not a committee vote.

Noam Brown says multi-agent organisation deserves less than 10% of the credit. The larger factor is a powerful model operating over long horizons and in parallel. The swarm multiplies capability. It does not create capability from weak parts.

Mathematics is friendly to this method because candidate arguments can be checked. AI research needs experiments that consume chips and time. Ten thousand agents can propose ten thousand runs before the lab can execute ten. The bottleneck moves from thought to testing.

The recursion has a cloud bill

Brown’s historical ladder moved from seconds of human-equivalent mathematical work to Olympiad problems lasting roughly 100 minutes. He expected a Millennium problem only around 2028. It arrived earlier.

That makes recursive self-improvement more plausible. Better agents generate research ideas, code experiments and analyse results, producing better agents that repeat the loop. The loop accelerates only if chips, experiments, data and evaluation move too.

OpenAI’s top 1% of internal Codex users were already spending $7,000 to $8,000 a day in early August. Cognitive labour now has a cloud bill. The moat is not merely the best model. It is the budget and orchestration layer able to keep thousands of copies working on one objective.

A swarm without a scheduler is a thousand consultants billing simultaneously. Impressive activity. Unclear outcome.

Four agents, one broken lock

Security startup Air found the same flaw in Claude Code, Codex, Gemini CLI and GitHub Copilot. The weakness lived in skills, downloadable instruction and file bundles that teach agents to perform tasks.

All four products scanned the first version. A malicious publisher could later upload different code under the same skill name. The agent treated the update as the trusted thing already approved. Verification checked identity by label, not content by digest and publisher authority.

Secure distribution needs integrity, authenticity and continuity. Did the bytes change? Who signed them? Is that signer still authorised? A name answers none of these. It is the label on the medicine bottle, not the tamper seal.

Anthropic, OpenAI and Google patched the issue. Microsoft had not confirmed a Copilot fix to Air at publication. No exploitation was found. The shared mistake still matters. Four teams imported the extension-update model into agents that can read files, run commands and touch production.

The supply chain for delegated action

The model can be perfectly obedient and still run poisoned instructions. The attack changes what “obedient” means.

Enterprises need approved registries, signed skills, version pinning, permission manifests, update review, revocation and logs connecting each action to the exact model and skill version. These are old controls with a larger blast radius.

The business model is endpoint security rebuilt around delegated action. Scan once. Set policy. Block unsafe updates across every agent.

The winning pitch is not “we stop superintelligence.” It is “no downloaded skill reaches production unless its publisher, permissions and exact bytes pass policy.” Procurement understands that sentence.

Compute as a systemic institution

Bridgewater’s Greg Jensen proposes regulating holders of more than roughly 5% of US or global compute like systemically important banks. His concern is concentration plus externality. A large provider can take risks whose failure harms customers and society beyond its shareholders.

Jensen expects OpenAI and Anthropic could control 35% to 50% of global compute in two years. Bridgewater is not watching from the sidelines. More than 80 employees work in AIA Labs, and a dedicated fund uses AI to manage $4.5B. Machines can make investment decisions while data acquisition, risk controls and execution remain human-driven algorithms.

The firm saw a model cheat on an internal test by finding the answer key. That is specification gaming. In finance, a strategy does not control its own risk limit because the backtest looks good.

Stress tests are the useful part of the bank analogy. What happens if a provider loses power, financing fails, an update is poisoned or a lab pauses deployment? Which customers are stranded? Which contracts carry the shock?

The publisher doom loop

A Microsoft document cited in publisher litigation says AI content strategy has started a doom loop that could damage models and the web. OpenAI’s Nick Turley reportedly said that after ChatGPT answers, there is no good reason to click the news link.

Publishers fund reporting with visits, ads and subscriptions. AI consumes the facts and satisfies the reader without sending traffic. Less traffic means less reporting. Less reporting means worse future data for retrieval and training.

This is a supply-chain problem dressed as copyright litigation. The model’s output competes with the supplier creating its input.

Licensing can move money back, but attribution is hard. Flat fees favour large outlets. Per-answer revenue needs a way to decide which source caused which answer. Capital can still find data centres more easily than local journalism. The imbalance is the product.

OpenAI tests the outer valuation

OpenAI is discussing a private round at $1.2T or more after raising or securing $182B. It projects roughly $170B of burn through 2028. Annualised revenue crossed $40B in July, behind Anthropic’s $65B.

The obvious trade is a 25% markup if a $1.2T round precedes a $1.5T IPO. The harder question is who anchors tens of billions. SoftBank is borrowing to fund old promises. Amazon projects $200B of capex. NVIDIA supports up to $105B of an Ohio data centre. The suppliers financing OpenAI carry their own infrastructure loads.

This is a consortium maintaining demand for the assets it sells. Chips and clouds invest in the customer buying chips and cloud. OpenAI uses the money to buy more supply. Revenue grows. Circularity grows too.

AI’s scarce resource is no longer intelligence alone. It is trustworthy coordination. The swarm needs evaluation. The skill needs a signature. The lab needs a stress test. The model economy needs suppliers that survive the answer.
