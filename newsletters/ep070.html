Ousted in four days

The board met Wednesday night, and by Thursday morning Jensen Huang was gone from all three of his roles: president, CEO, board member. Nvidia calls it a resignation, accepted Wednesday night per the securities filing; Bloomberg’s headline says ousted. Both are true the way these things are always true. The speed is the story. Sunday he apologized to employees for being unfaithful. Monday the board announced an independent investigation, run by Wachtell Lipton. Tuesday he ran a normal meeting of his fifty or so top executives and prepped next week’s earnings call. Wednesday afternoon the New York Times reported that video evidence had surfaced internally of a second affair, with Kimberly Powell, a Nvidia healthcare vice president who reported directly to him. Wednesday night the board met. The replacement architecture tells you how unplanned this was: finance chief Colette Kress as interim CEO, and Harvey Jones, seventy-three, as the company’s first-ever chair of the board. In thirty-three years Nvidia never needed one. The market’s verdict was a morning crash to $155.13, down 11%, roughly $450 billion gone, then a bounce to close near $166, down just under 5%, still a quarter below where the stock traded two weeks ago. The number that lingers: 13.7 times forward earnings, per Koyfin, against AMD’s 38.9. The market is pricing the era’s dominant chip company like a value stock because nobody knows what it is without the man. Matt Levine’s frame is the right one: governance exists to keep bandits honest, but the returns came from the bandit. Nvidia’s board chose governance over short-term returns, knowingly. Blankfein’s epitaph, the graveyards are filled with indispensable men, and Costolo’s prediction, that Nvidia will bring Jensen back within four years, bracket the range of outcomes. Meanwhile the President says the FBI is investigating, and a House subcommittee wants Huang behind closed doors next week.

The caretaker and the missing bench

Colette Kress is fifty-nine, joined in 2013 from a Cisco division CFO seat after a decade at Microsoft, and is the only voice besides Huang’s investors hear on earnings calls. The next one is Tuesday. Her paper trail says risk officer: The Information reports she pushed back on Huang’s proposed $250 billion credit backstop for OpenAI’s data centers, and in August Nvidia split the deal and has guaranteed only about $100 billion of it. People who know her say Huang steamrolled her, and that she clashed with his daughter Madison and son-in-law Nico Caprez, both rising inside the company. She holds the pen now, but the deeper exposure is structural. Huang ran Nvidia with roughly sixty direct reports and no number two, told colleagues he’d run it until he was 120; he is 63. SemiAnalysis calls it a kingdom with one king: 42,000 employees, tiny for its size, product designs like the GB200 landing at the last minute so the company can change its mind. The floated successors are Ian Buck, who created CUDA and joined in 2004, and GPU engineering chief Jonah Alben, there since 1997. One executive’s assessment earlier this year: Ian would rather die than succeed Jensen. Founder bets do not survive professional managers, SemiAnalysis argues, because no hired CEO bets the company on a money-losing software moat the way Huang did with CUDA. The search could take six months.

The DeepSeek allocation

The Information’s exclusive reframes the whole scandal, and the mechanism is worth walking slowly. During DeepSeek’s second funding round, the one that closed in October at 105.5 billion yuan, about $15.8 billion, valuing it around $92 billion in the biggest Chinese AI raise to date, DeepSeek employees personally introduced companies tied to Alice Huang to investors: Century Litong and its Shanghai-listed parent Jiangsu Lettall, known together as Litong. The employees said explicitly why Litong deserved an allocation in a heavily oversubscribed round: it is a substantial supplier of black-market Nvidia chips. The money went in through special purpose vehicles, so no Litong entity sits on DeepSeek’s shareholder list; one vehicle was run by Monolith Management, the Beijing firm founded by former Sequoia China partner Cao Xi, now DeepSeek’s third-largest investor, with Lettall putting 95 million yuan into it. Why did DeepSeek want Litong close? Century Litong became a preferred Nvidia cloud partner four months after its 2023 founding and has imported 240,000 Nvidia chips worth at least $7.5 billion into China since, per The Information’s earlier reporting; two sources say Blackwells are still getting in despite the crackdown. DeepSeek, adapting to Huawei silicon, wanted Blackwells as research insurance. At CES in January, Jensen Huang called DeepSeek’s R1 “really, really exciting work.” Draw your own org chart.

The policy floor shifts

SemiAnalysis asks the question out loud: was this really about the affairs? Their argument, clearly labeled as their analysis, is that a resignation four days into an investigation is tied to GPU smuggling, that their Accelerator Model counts more than 1.5 gigawatts of restricted Nvidia chips that still ended in China, and that the US intelligence community was a large part of the push to remove him. Whatever the trigger, the ground is moving. The Information reports the Commerce Department is finalizing the long-awaited rule closing the remote-access loophole that let Chinese labs rent advanced chips through cloud services; a tougher export-control bill has passed the House and could ride the defense authorization bill in coming weeks. SemiAnalysis’s dissent is worth hearing: remote access sends profits to American firms, keeps Chinese labs observable, and preserves a kill switch; cut it off and Huawei’s domestic stack wins faster. Their sharpest point is collateral: open source suffers, because Nvidia quietly works with Chinese labs on the optimizations the whole startup ecosystem runs on. Meanwhile Singapore has barred Alice Huang after concluding she gave inaccurate information in its Megaspeed investigation.

The Nvidia put, repriced

$140 billion. That is The Information’s tally of Nvidia’s dealmaking, including credit guarantees, in two months. The inventory: $10 billion into Anthropic’s IPO next week; $30 billion-plus into OpenAI over time plus the $105 billion Ohio backstop; $5 billion into Safe Superintelligence and roughly $2.5 billion into Thinking Machines, both signed and safe; $2 billion into CoreWeave’s private placement plus an unsold-capacity purchase; a $3.4 billion contract with Iren; $1.5 billion to rent its own chips back from Lambda on top of nearly $20 billion in lease guarantees; another $1 billion discussed for Figure AI; over $5 billion in guarantees under discussion for Nscale’s 460-megawatt Anthropic site; more than $1 billion into Reflection AI after Huang personally talked them into open models; and the $12.9 billion Hugging Face acquisition he drove. The Nvidia put, the assumption Nvidia steps in when a chip buyer wobbles, has evaporated, one finance executive says. Neocloud shares fell about 7%. The most exposed are companies mid-round with an expected Nvidia check. And the sentence of the day, from a cloud provider that buys Nvidia chips: “we would be an idiot not to” consider Google or AMD now; they had wanted to diversify before, and were afraid of offending Jensen. Fear was a moat. It just retired.

The trillion-dollar hedge

Anthropic prices its IPO into this storm next week at a tentative $1.25 to $1.4 trillion, and several investors expect it to price above the range; Bloomberg says the roadshow covered the 825 million shares multiple times within a day or two. The model buyers actually use is simple: $81 billion annualized in October, call it $100 billion by year end, double it two years to $200 billion in 2028, apply a 25% adjusted operating margin, and you get roughly $50 billion of operating profit, meaning the range charges over 25 times 2028 earnings that do not yet exist. Gabelli’s John Belton calls that cheap versus software and rich versus megacap; Morningstar’s Ahmed Khan has $1.7 trillion fair value at a 47% growth rate, with a $3.4 trillion bull case if models start training themselves. Two Nvidia wrinkles: Nvidia plans $10 billion into this IPO plus West Virginia data-center guarantees, and nobody knows if post-Jensen Nvidia shows up the same way; and a large UK asset manager on the roadshow argues Anthropic is the lab least dependent on any single chip roadmap, training and serving across Nvidia, Google and Amazon silicon. If Nvidia’s innovation pace is the week’s real casualty, the chip-agnostic lab is the hedge, and the rotation trade is literal: managers say they will sell AI infrastructure to fund the allocation.

The threat model flips

Yesterday’s segment was the doom case: the research nonprofit METR and AI Futures raising their probabilities. Today’s counterweight is the man whose firm benchmarks the models: Artificial Analysis CEO Micah Hill-Smith told Information subscribers a fast takeoff is “pretty unlikely,” the frontier lead is months, “arguably Anthropic this week,” capability per dollar is shrinking, spend ten times more for a model twice as good, while the cost of any fixed task falls five to ten times a year. The immediate risk is elsewhere: Tenzai CTO Ariel Zeitlin says free downloadable Chinese AI is now good enough to break into corporate networks, and “no one is restricting access to it.” He pointed to ARTEX, an open-source AI hacking framework posted to GitHub in July; CrowdStrike says a China-linked group used it against more than 200 organizations in 11 days. Zeitlin expects months, maybe years, of daily breach disclosures: fixing is harder than finding. The 2026 threat model, a week out from the doom papers: not a runaway god, but a thousand-dollar offense against a million-dollar defense.

Quick hits

ByteDance is buying back up to $6 billion of stock at about a $400 billion valuation, up 20% on last year’s buyback, $241.35 a share versus about $200 last November; the January TikTok deal, 80% of the US data-security operation sold to an American joint venture with the revenue engine kept, cleared the IPO path save Beijing’s approval, the put-right deadline slides to 2030, first-half profit fell single digits to $20 billion on AI spend, and AI staff get 300 million tracking shares against the poaching war. HubSpot’s Brian Halligan took the CEO job back after five years: Yamini Rangan grew revenue from $1.3 to $3.7 billion but the stock fell 74% over five years and popped 6.4% on the news; Jobs, Bhusri, Halligan, and keep the pattern in mind for Costolo’s Jensen prediction. Bloomberg says SpaceX and Tesla could unveil a merger as early as next month, share ratio and governance still negotiated, possible slip to early 2027. incident.io’s numbers via the Pragmatic Engineer: nearly 80% of production pull requests include agent-generated code, no-human-review PRs went from 12% to 43% in a year, PR size up almost fivefold, and engineering headcount rose 2.7 times, not down. Sequen, a $1.4 billion New York startup from Etsy search alum Zoe Weil, sells the “ranking intelligence layer” that reorders what you see on sites like Wayfair, no cookies; she says fewer than a hundred people can do this work and she has hired nine, one poached from a nine-figure job. Meta swaps infrastructure chiefs, AWS veteran Dave Brown in for Santosh Janardhan. And Bill Simmons canceled his show’s ChatGPT partnership after listeners revolted: even AI’s ad money now carries an audience tax. Earnings Tuesday.
