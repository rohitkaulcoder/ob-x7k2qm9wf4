A price is a public good

CME wanted to list futures on the hourly rent of Nvidia H100 and B200 GPUs on October 5. The CFTC has extended its review by 45 days, to November 9, citing "novel or complex" issues.

The idea is sound. A future is a promise about a price. An AI company that needs a thousand GPUs for a year can lock its bill. A lender holding GPUs as collateral can hedge the risk that rental values fall. That is how compute becomes an asset class rather than a bet.

The problem is not the contract. It is the price underneath it. GPU rent is set in private, bespoke deals, and a few players control most of the supply. A futures market on a thin, opaque index invites the largest supplier to move the index and collect on its position. Wheat works because nobody can push wheat around.

So the delay is not about whether compute gets financialised. It is about whether anyone can see the price first. Build the benchmark and the market follows. Skip it and you have built a manipulation machine with a CME logo.

The seat was a subsidy

Procurement firm Zip says AI providers such as OpenAI, Anthropic, Cursor and Sierra took 8% of its customers' software spending in the year to August, up from 1.4%. Total software budgets rose a median 13%. The labs are no longer a pilot line. They are a budget line.

The incumbents are answering with discounts. Workday is giving about 20 top customers a free year of its AI platform. Amazon offers four months of Kiro. Figma cut AI credit costs by up to half. Thoughtworks says typical discounts are now 30-35%, from under 20%.

The discounts treat a symptom. Per-seat pricing worked because more usage cost the vendor almost nothing. AI breaks that: every query burns tokens, often bought from OpenAI or Anthropic. So vendors move to usage pricing, and Pegasystems' GitHub Copilot bill went from $20,000 a month to $260,000.

This is not really about discounts. It is about the middleman position. An incumbent that rents intelligence from a lab, marks it up and bills by the query is teaching its customer to go straight to the lab. FICO's CIO says he will no longer sign a contract that does not bundle AI into the core licence. Workday says its focus is "adoption, adoption, adoption" and that the revenue question is "difficult to answer". That is an honest answer, and not a good one.

For Indian SaaS, the diligence question changes. Not "how many seats", but "what is gross margin when the customer uses AI a lot".

Tata is choosing whether to have a price

On September 17 the Tata Sons board extended N. Chandrasekaran's term by five years and voted to comply with a regulatory order to list, overruling Noel Tata on both. Tata Trusts, which Noel chairs, controls about two-thirds of Tata Sons. It lost anyway.

The reason is arithmetic. The Trusts have two nominees on the board. One of them, Venu Srinivasan, voted the other way, so the majority shareholder spoke with two voices. Srinivasan also complained to Maharashtra's charity commissioner, whose curbs on the Sir Ratan Tata Trust, holder of 23.6% of Tata Sons, deferred the group's AGM.

Follow the money and the fight is simpler. Shapoorji Pallonji owns 18.4% of Tata Sons. It is SP Group's most valuable asset and its least liquid, and SP carries costly debt. A listing turns an unsellable stake into a quoted one. The RBI declined this month to exempt Tata Sons from mandatory listing.

This is not really about personalities. It is about whether India's most important holding company becomes a listed company with a daily price, or stays a trust-controlled private structure. The next moves belong to the charity commissioner, a shareholder vote and possibly the courts, which is to say they will be slow.

The long end is a Fed forecast

The US ten-year yield was about 3.94% at the start of March and is now just under 5%. The popular explanation is the deficit. But the fiscal picture did not change in six months, and bond traders have known about it for twenty years.

A new paper by Beaudry, Cavallino and Willems finds that big long-rate moves cluster around jobs reports and Fed officials' speeches. The simplest reading: a ten-year rate is roughly the expected average of short rates over ten years. A one-point rise mostly means the market expects short rates about one point higher for the decade.

The distinction matters. Rates that are high because of Fed expectations can fall on one jobs report. Rates that are high because of a debt problem stay high. For anyone raising money from global LPs, whose hurdle is the Treasury yield, that is the difference between a bad quarter and a bad decade.

Data is now a border

China's internet regulator is investigating DeepSeek and Moonshot AI after Anthropic accused Chinese firms of using Claude illicitly, in some cases routing sensitive Chinese data through it. It lands in the week Xi Jinping visits Washington and lab chiefs address the UN Security Council. President Trump, meanwhile, rejected an international agreement on AI guardrails as a "globalist scheme".

Beijing punishing its own champions for sending data to an American model says data is treated as a security asset first. Washington refusing treaties says the rules will come from companies and bilateral deals. AI governance is becoming trade policy by another name.

Briefly

OpenAI announced GPT-6 Sol and GPT-6 Luna, cheaper and faster models in the Astra family. Cheaper models are how the software pricing squeeze eventually eases. Alibaba unveiled a new chip for training and inference.

Meta's Muse agent had more than 500,000 users try it in about a week and more than 250,000 daily users, per The Information. Citrini calls it agents' "Studio Ghibli moment". Reach is the moat. The cost of a 100-million-token weekly free tier is the question.

Avathon, which predicts industrial equipment failures and monitors mine safety, signed a five-year deal with Barrick Gold's North American unit worth $10-20M a year, paid as a base fee plus fees tied to results. Its ARR reportedly rose to more than $50M from about $15M. Outcome pricing in heavy industry is worth watching. Ande, an entertainment-booking startup, raised $52M from Lightspeed and Redpoint.
