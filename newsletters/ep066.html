The week the AI trade gets audited

Every cycle has a week where the questions stop being rhetorical. This is that week for AI. Anthropic starts its IPO roadshow in the coming days and lists the week of November 16. CoreWeave reports tomorrow with the stock fifty percent off in a month and an expected ten point seven billion dollars of quarterly cash burn. OpenAI holds an investor call after closing roughly eleven billion dollars of new funding at a one point two five trillion dollar valuation, in a round that could reach twenty billion. And Nvidia’s board is running an independent investigation into whether its own CEO was the target of a foreign intelligence effort. The thread connecting all four is a single question the market has avoided for two years: how much more can it absorb? Not demand for compute. Demand for AI paper. More than three hundred billion dollars of new AI-linked stock and mandatory convertibles has already been sold or priced this year, with roughly one hundred billion more on tap, mostly at-the-market programs. All US IPOs and follow-ons in 2025, every sector combined, totaled one hundred ninety-four billion. The AI complex has lapped the entire previous year’s equity supply by itself, and the biggest listing is still ahead. When debt got expensive, the buildout switched to equity; when equity gets crowded, something else has to give. Watch the bid, not the news flow.

Jensen Huang, investigated

The facts, kept carefully separate from the speculation. Nvidia says its board is “initiating an independent investigation” into reports that Huang “has been the target of a foreign intelligence effort.” Bloomberg reports the probe follows Huang’s admission to employees that he was unfaithful to his wife; he told staff he was “deeply sorry.” Nvidia’s statement adds the board has “no reason to believe that either national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised.” The New York Times reports Secretary of State Marco Rubio warned the White House about the relationship earlier this year. A congressional inquiry is underway, and Huang was already asked to attend a closed-door House briefing on November 18. An affair admission is a private matter; it becomes market news only through the counterintelligence question, and that question matters because Nvidia is the load-bearing company of the cycle and its CEO is its single point of failure. Both halves of Nvidia’s sentence can be true at once: no current evidence, and an investigation to test that. The November 18 briefing is the next hard date.

How Cisco got bullwhipped

The best thing published this weekend was a twenty-five-year-old inventory story. Asianometry reconstructs the 2000-01 Cisco collapse, and its value is the mechanism. Cisco owned no factories: 70-80% of production sat with Jabil and Solectron, wired into a “virtual supply chain” that gave suppliers a direct view of the order book and gave management a daily global sales report and a quarterly close measured in hours. Total demand visibility was the boast. Then lead times stretched to four to six months, and customers did what customers do in a shortage: double and triple orders, padded by salespeople’s buffers, sometimes placed with multiple resellers at once. Every layer of the chain amplified the signal, which is the textbook bullwhip effect, and Cisco’s beautiful dashboard faithfully recorded the phantom demand. Forecasting sixty percent growth, Cisco locked in component commitments while its own suppliers objected: Solectron warned the orders exceeded necessity, Xilinx cut its internal growth forecast. Cisco overrode them. On December 4, 2000, Chambers had “never been more optimistic.” A week later the daily report cracked. Telecom funding had collapsed from 5.6 billion dollars a month to seven hundred million by November; the 3.8 billion dollar backlog evaporated; April 2001 brought a thirty percent sequential sales decline, 8,500 layoffs and a 2.2 billion dollar inventory charge, much of it custom components destroyed because nobody else could use them. The lesson is not that Cisco was stupid. It is that the bullwhip requires no fraud, only a shortage, a layered chain, and a leader with a great dashboard who overrides the people closest to the metal. Hold that frame for tomorrow’s CoreWeave call: how much of any order book is double-ordering against scarcity, how much is vendor-financed circularity (Lucent did eight billion dollars of it; Cisco was comparatively clean at 2.4 committed), and who is quietly revising while the CEO talks. The hyperscalers can absorb their partners’ pain the way Cisco did. The neoclouds cannot, which is precisely why they trade the way they do.

The anti-AI trade points at India

Bloomberg’s Asia desk names the rotation: the anti-AI trade. With the Nikkei and Taiex in technical bear markets, money is fleeing the hardware complex, and the Nifty 50 just had its best week since 2021 on 1.9 billion dollars of net foreign buying through Wednesday. China and Hong Kong get the same flow into Alibaba and Tencent. One week of flows is not a regime change, but the logic is the first genuinely new idea in the India story this year. For eighteen months India’s weakness was its lack of AI exposure. That property is now the pitch: the large, liquid growth market with the least AI hardware beta in Asia. India wins if the AI unwind continues, and still has its domestic compounding story if it does not. A market that wins both branches of the scenario tree is rare. An India-focused fund did not have to manufacture this tailwind; it only has to not waste it.

Unity’s price problem

A consortium including PIF and Silver Lake has approached Unity, which closed Friday at $52.60, a $23B market cap, after rallying from $17 in February on its AI ad business. The strategic logic is the August EA deal: PIF, Silver Lake and Affinity combine Unity’s engine and ad network with EA’s content. The arithmetic is less cooperative. Unity already trades at a profit-multiple premium to AppLovin, which runs much higher cash flow margins and deserves the premium, so justifying a higher bid requires synergy magic the buyers have not yet described. And Silver Lake, Unity’s largest shareholder at 8%, sits on both sides of its own negotiation.

Quick hits

The OCC published a “super intelligence” supervision framework for bank examiners; beneath the branding it grades AI by use case, not sophistication - a simple agent that can move money outranks a sophisticated model that drafts memos - flags external customer agents as channel risk (JPMorgan and PNC say they will block them), and warns of correlated, cascading agent activity at machine speed. Expect the RBI to read it. Gurman: new Apple CEO John Ternus is privately pressing Federighi to rebuild Siri as an autonomous agent while publicly calling the current one “the kind of Siri that we always wanted.” Microsoft declined to renew its Oracle server-rental deal. CME’s GPU-rental futures slipped another 60 days into January. Cybertruck production ends around mid-next-year for Cybercab. Meta CSO Dave Wehner joins inference startup Fireworks as CFO. SpaceX director Donald Harrison resigned as Musk pushes the Tesla merger. Binance CFO Kaiser Ng is out after the six billion dollar hack, replaced by Justin Yek.
