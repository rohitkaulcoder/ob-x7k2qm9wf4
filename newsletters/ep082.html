The presidential pop lasted exactly one day

Monday was politics; Tuesday was arithmetic. Twenty-four hours after the reinstatement rally added nearly a trillion dollars back to Nvidia, the market gave it back: Nvidia fell 5.9% to $155.38, Anthropic closed at $81.91 - below its $83 IPO price for the first time, after trading as low as $75.61 - and the Nasdaq 100 dropped 2%. CoreWeave and Nebius each fell about 11%, and Dell, hours before printing a blowout quarter, fell 11.8% in the regular session. The Information’s Martin Peers had the right diagnosis: the market has gone manic-depressive. A 48% crash from the October peak, a spy probe, a presidential intervention, a 32% relief rally, then a broad give-back led by the exact stocks the rally was supposed to save. When price action gets that chaotic, the only discipline is to ask what actually changed in the world. What changed Tuesday is that the market got new information about supply and demand - the two things no phone call from the White House can fix.

The glut mechanism, priced

The arithmetic, step by step. SemiAnalysis, the chip industry’s most-watched research shop, published a report Tuesday saying it is “starting to see signs of a broader pricing cooldown in the market for Nvidia’s AI chips,” citing price cuts by none other than SpaceX. Ask the dumb smart question: if compute were still scarce, why would a seller cut prices? Then the supply math. JPMorgan estimates total new AI-chip shipments jumped from 10.1 million units last year to 16.3 million this year to 23.3 million in 2027 - supply more than doubling in two years. On top of that, the new Commerce rules blocking Chinese firms from renting advanced chips through Southeast Asian data centers will redirect those chips back into the American market, adding domestic availability. Now hold that against the bull case: CoreWeave’s CEO told investors 300,000 of its chips come off contract in 2027 and 2028, and that re-renting them “at current pricing” means nearly $10 billion of extra annual revenue - almost as much as the entire company makes this year. Every word of that sentence leans on “at current pricing.” The bond market is not waiting around: as Odd Lots pointed out, credit-default-swap premiums on the hyperscalers kept rising straight through Monday’s relief rally. Stocks took the phone call; credit did not. And the neoclouds are the fault line: borrow billions against the chips, rent them out, and pray rental rates hold long enough to cover the debt. The chips are both the collateral and the cash machine - which is why some CoreWeave bond yields had spiked before any of this.

Chips vs intelligence, and the IPO countdown

Matt Levine’s frame for the week: the market is sorting two different monopolies - Nvidia’s on chips, which supply growth can erode, and the model labs’ on intelligence, which compounds. Tuesday priced the first half of that sentence. Anthropic at $81.91 is now worth just under $1.5 trillion fully diluted, on the same basis we have used all week - and below the price its own underwriters set six days ago. Meanwhile the Journal’s countdown clock on the OpenAI IPO keeps ticking: third-quarter revenue of about $10 billion against Anthropic’s $17 billion, with a smaller operating loss, and roughly $750 billion of compute spending planned through 2030. IPO historian Jay Ritter’s quote of the day: “I don’t see a compelling reason to wait more than a couple of months.” The market is starting to price these two as rivals, not as one trade.

Dell closes the loop

The number that answered the question we had been asking since the weekend: Dell reported after the close, and the backlog is converting. Revenue up 103%, shipments of AI servers up 291% year over year, net income up 266% to $5.7 billion; shares rose about 5% after hours. We flagged the $95 billion AI backlog on Monday, told you Monday night was the test, and the test came back positive. Savor the absurdity: Dell fell almost 12% in the regular session, hours before printing those numbers, because the market spent Tuesday selling the picks-and-shovels makers on exactly the glut fears above. One nuance worth holding: a distributor’s blowout quarter does not settle the glut debate by itself - Dell sits between the chip makers and the buyers, and a quarter like this can mean either insatiable end demand or a channel stuffing itself ahead of a slowdown. The after-hours market chose the first reading; we will keep score. Also after the close: Workday’s growth slowed to 11.2% and its shares fell 7%. The saaspocalypse watch continues.

An apology, a therapy bill, and a harder job

The Journal’s exclusive on the inside of the restoration: at an all-hands after his reinstatement, Jensen Huang apologized to Nvidia staff, told them he is in therapy, and thanked the Trump administration for giving the company quick access to intelligence during the crisis. The Journal also surfaced a new class of loser: investors who sold when the board said Huang would not return, burned by the U-turn and reportedly furious. Megaspeed was added to the Commerce Department’s entity list on Monday, and the December 3 Senate Banking testimony is now confirmed on the committee’s own website. And The Information’s Phoebe Liu and Leo Schwartz make the case that Huang’s job may now be harder than before the ouster: he had lied to some senior Nvidia executives about the infidelities and has to rebuild trust inside his own leadership team; Commerce and DOJ are probing Megaspeed and Supermicro while the SEC probes Nvidia itself; customers are diversifying to their own chips; longtime lieutenants Colette Kress, Tim Teter and others may not stay; and Elizabeth Warren, who called the reinstatement reckless, chairs Senate Banking come January. Yale’s Sonnenfeld and C.J. Muse’s independent-chairman idea are both still on the table. Hakeem Jeffries called it indefensible; Tom Cotton called it the tip of the iceberg.

The oxygen problem

Goldman’s Jane Dunlevie, at the bank’s Las Vegas private-tech conference: the ten most valuable private companies are now worth $2.6 trillion, up from $1.1 trillion a year ago. That concentration is sucking the oxygen out of the rest of the market - the hottest startups at the conference (Legora, Unconventional AI, Abnormal AI) were swamped with meeting requests while older software companies tried to remind investors they still exist. The status barometer was David Solomon’s private dinner; the founders of Legora and Fireworks got the seats. Meanwhile 2026 is on track to be the fifth straight disappointing IPO year, with every 2026 listing below its offer price; attendees were betting the Vercels, Ramps and Plaids finally get out next year. The counter-example: Harvey, whose gross margins went from negative 55% to roughly breakeven in three months while ARR went from $195 million to $675 million - Winston Weinberg’s point being that the model costs are coming down faster than the doomers think. The read-through for an India-focused fund: the mega-round gravity well is pulling LP attention upstream into the same ten names everywhere, and the best counter-position is the one Goldman itself was pitching - the companies everyone forgot still exist.

Quick hits and what to watch

Done deal: CrowdStrike is acquiring Zscaler for $43.9 billion in an all-stock deal at a 34% premium - we told you Sunday about talks at a $32 billion market cap; it closed a third higher. New tariffs: Jamieson Greer announced replacement levies on China, the EU and 14 other economies after the Supreme Court struck down the originals; China extended its critical-minerals export pause. Huawei was convicted on 10 of 12 counts in Brooklyn. OpenAI launched Athena, a semantic layer for business data - unglamorous plumbing that is exactly the kind that is hard to switch away from; consultants say the single biggest agent failure mode is the model guessing what each number means. Sierra is raising $1.75 billion at a $30 billion valuation. Marlowe, which runs AI on chips that are not Nvidia’s, raised a $277 million seed - which tells you where the smart money thinks the glut argument lands. Amazon confirmed the Decart deal, key technology and staff for roughly $7.5 billion, to make its Trainium compute more efficient. Exa is in talks to raise at around $7 billion, led by Menlo. Anthropic launched Claude Ship for automated code reviews. Z.ai released GLM-5.4, its first model on the looped-transformer architecture. And the Saudi engineer accused of bugging OpenAI’s offices was released on a $1 million bond. Into the weekend: whether Dell’s pop holds, whether the SemiAnalysis cooldown shows up in actual contract repricing, and the December 1 and 3 testimony dates, now eight days away. The scandal, the glut and the IPO window all land in the same two weeks.
