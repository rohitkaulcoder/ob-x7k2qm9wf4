What SpaceX is actually building in Texas

The Information’s investigation, built from public records, utility filings, land deeds and job listings, shows the Musk buildout is far larger than understood. At Starbase, rising steel with gas turbines beside it plus a SpaceX job post point to a multigigawatt “Starbase supercomputer”; Occam Edge estimates one 444-acre parcel could support 1.5 to 2.6 GW of on-site gas. Musk has made previously unreported down payments to Karpowership - the Turkish company that sells power plants on ships to countries with broken grids - for as much as a gigawatt of floating capacity at the Port of Brownsville. Utility filings show 700-800 MW of grid draw being prepared at Bastrop; a county hearing revealed data centers inside the Terafab foundry; a turbine reseller SpaceX acquired had already parked mobile turbines in the Panhandle. This is the 10 GW by 2027 that Musk promised shareholders on October 20, backed by an estimated $224M in donations to Texas Republicans who lost. The business mechanics explain both the rush and the fragility. SpaceX’s compute unit, SI, did $7.56B in Q3, nearly triple the prior quarter, renting immediately available capacity at a premium - and premiums do not attach to capacity that arrives late. Worse, SpaceX has leased chips from Apollo with deliveries starting in H1 2027: chips without a powered building are a cost, not a product. Governor Abbott paused grid connections and air permits before the election, with audits running past December; the Starbase air permit has been stuck since September, and air permitting is, in a quoted lawyer’s words, the chokepoint. The honest counterweight: environmental commissioners are Abbott appointees with years left, Starbase controls its own zoning, and Musk’s crews raced to annex land and route underwater gas pipelines days before Brownsville’s moratorium hearings. Hinojosa’s own line to CNN - “it is not that I hate data centers, I hate the way they are taking advantage of Texas” - reads as negotiation, not prohibition. The hedge is already visible: the day after the election, SpaceX was in Cochise County, Arizona, pitching 6 GW on 747 acres. And a serious SI disruption complicates the SpaceX-Tesla merger both boards are considering. A great deal rides on an air permit.

Inside China’s Starbase

SemiAnalysis flew to Ulanqab and walked Galaxy, the closest thing on earth to Stargate Abilene: 800 MW of planned IT capacity against Abilene’s 820. The first 120 MW phase was delivered in under twelve months at roughly a fifth of US construction cost, with nearly 30,000 construction workers across 22 projects in the surrounding zone and 25 GW of announced projects regionally. The owner is not a tech company but Envision, a renewable group with 100+ GW of turbines installed, and the campus is vertical integration as strategy: own wind farms, own line, own batteries, own power modules, co-designed prefab substation (three months on site versus six). The power model - source-grid-load-storage - uses a dedicated line so the public grid becomes a supplement, not the foundation: 420 MW of wind across two farms 80 km apart, oversized 1.75x against the first two phases’ 240 MW load, plus a four-hour battery, yields over 60% of those phases’ power from owned wind. The arithmetic is the game: capacity factor (average output over rated maximum) runs 35-40% at good Ulanqab sites, so a farm rated equal to the load covers only ~40% of annual consumption; oversizing pushes wind’s share toward 55% at the cost of throwing away power in the windiest hours; the four-hour battery crosses 60%. The detail that matters most is a live 5 MW hall running solid-state transformers - 10 kV AC to 800 V DC in one integrated conversion chain, skipping the external transformer, UPS and rectifier stages - the architecture SemiAnalysis says is still on the US roadmap for Nvidia’s ~600 kW Kyber racks. A quarter of Chinese data centers built in the past decade run DC; 97% of American ones still use AC UPS. Cooling drops the central chiller plant entirely. Context: rents have halved in five years to ~$40/kW/month, capex is under $3M/MW, power runs about $0.055/kWh (SemiAnalysis calls it among the world’s lowest), and returns are thin enough that the real reward is selling your own turbines and batteries into the build - the BYD playbook applied to compute. CATL, meanwhile, has become VNET’s largest shareholder, partnered with the top data-center power-supply maker, and is one of DeepSeek’s biggest outside investors. The stack is integrating from electron to model.

And yet: the China bear case

On the same weekend, the internet’s most prominent China bull turned bear, and not for the reasons the Galaxy tour suggests. Noah Smith’s case is macro: the make-everything-country strategy is hitting its limits. China spends ~4% of GDP subsidizing manufacturing; the famous UNIDO-style forecast of a 45% global share by 2030 has been revised by cooler heads to 35-36%, and in dollar terms the share peaked during the pandemic. The deeper constraint is the gravity model: China is too big to be Giant Korea, export markets saturate and tariff backlashes grow (see the EU auto deal), so production must sell into a domestic market that is itself saturated - profits are collapsing across autos, steel and solar simultaneously. The infrastructure splurge is topping out too (the main transport network is “91% complete”, per a senior official), and depreciation means a large slice of GDP now maintains underused roads and trains. His conclusion: growth slows just as AI upends everything. Honesty corner, as on air: the section arguing China will flub the age of AI sits behind his subscriber gate; we read the economics in full and the AI argument not at all. The tension is the point: you can build the world’s best AI plumbing and still lose the software race. Hold both.

The weekend mood

The Information’s weekend column reads as quiet hedging. A Dutch bank is trying to offload a half-billion-dollar chunk of data center loans, including Stargate-linked paper - the reported fact is the sale; one plausible interpretation is de-risking while the market will still take it, another is ordinary balance-sheet management, but the financing side of the buildout is being examined in a way it was not two weeks ago. Anthropic investors are gloomy about holding $1.5T at IPO. Google said it would spend $15B expanding in Malaysia, days after the election - the buildout scouting friendlier jurisdictions. Ron Baron talked up space data centers by 2028. The grace notes matter more: Senator-elect Talarico, who won against Musk’s spending, called him “a great business leader and entrepreneur and innovator” and vowed to work with him (“Nice of him to say that,” Musk replied), and Gavin Baker read Hinojosa’s actual plans and pronounced her “reasonable”. After a week of Nuremberg-trial rhetoric from X’s former product chief, the adults are starting to talk. Bloomberg’s weekend essay adds the political demand curve: Tuesday did not kill populism, it diversified it - urban left, prairie faith, post-partisan. Price that into every permit.
