Groundhog Thanksgiving

America signed off for Thanksgiving the way it has signed off all week: a morning selloff, an afternoon rescue. Three selling waves in four sessions, each bought back almost entirely before the close - John Authers at Bloomberg calls it Groundhog Thanksgiving, and the label fits. The twist this time is that the data turned hostile. Third-quarter GDP came in above 4% annualized, one of the best readings in three decades, and in a market whose whole problem is liquidity, strength is the enemy: an economy running that hot gives the Federal Reserve every excuse to keep money tight. Worse, the same release confirmed the earlier CPI signal - supercore inflation, services excluding housing, is above the Fed’s zone of tolerance, and oil has almost no direct effect on it. The “it’s just energy” excuse is dead. Fed funds futures now treat a hike at the December 9 meeting as a virtual certainty; right after the midterms they priced one hike for all of next year, and today they price more than two. And the blackout period starts Saturday - whatever the Fed wants the market to know, it has already said. The chorus begging for a repeat of the Powell Pivot of eight years ago misses the difference: back then inflation was not above target. A dovish rescue would mean easing into a hot print, and that window is probably closed.

Meltdown or mandate

The two-week debate is now framed. Carl Weinberg of High Frequency Economics offers the shortest argument in economics this week: if you were wondering whether the economy is strong enough to withstand one or more hikes, it can, and this report confirms it. Robin Brooks at Brookings reaches for the word meltdown: the Fed is hiking into falling oil prices, and because falling energy prices mechanically lift real rates, you get a super-charged tightening nobody explicitly chose. His warning is blunt - go into the blackout without a dovish pivot and the wheels may start coming off, and the meltdown itself could end up being what stops the December hike. The market texture backs the fragility case: gold and Bitcoin, both liquidity-sensitive, staged dives Wednesday and still finished with gains, and stocks recovered nearly all their losses. Overnight, with the U.S. closed, European stocks extended the AI comeback, oil rose after an attack on a Saudi refinery, and Bitcoin pushed back above $60,000. One more thread: S&P Global Ratings is now publicly musing about cutting Nvidia’s credit rating, citing the governance whiplash of Jensen Huang’s departure and return. The rating agencies are circling the AI trade at the exact moment the liquidity story turns hostile.

Physics, not promises

What actually happens to the prompts your employees type into an AI tool? The question went public in September, when OpenAI said its agents had solved a version of the Navier-Stokes problem and NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster published a statement saying OpenAI may have drawn on its own tool while he worked on a related proof inside it. OpenAI denied that - but every general counsel in America got the point. Bloomberg’s Davey Alba profiled TrustedRouter, the startup built to make the question structurally impossible rather than contractually discouraged. The fine print it attacks is real: Amazon’s guidelines say Bedrock keeps all traffic up to 30 days with the newest Claude versions for abuse detection, and staff can review flagged prompts; Google keeps prompts and responses to OpenAI’s flagship models 30 days by default. Both offer “zero data retention” to qualifying customers - and for the most advanced models, the lab that built them has a say in who qualifies. Founder Joseph Perla’s line: zero data retention is whatever they want it to be. TrustedRouter’s answer is physics: open-weight models - DeepSeek, Moonshot, Google’s Gemma 4 - running inside confidential-computing enclaves where the prompt stays encrypted while processed. As of mid-November, 52% of customers ran fully confidential models. For closed models the prompt passes through encrypted but arrives in plain text, so the company strips personal information first. Traction: thousands of customers, a billion tokens in a day in August, six billion in a day this month, and a $6 million round led by Sierra Ventures with current and former OpenAI and Anthropic employees on the cap table.

The token exchange

Perla’s larger plan is the New York Token Exchange: businesses buying AI processing the way they buy oil or electricity. The premise is scarcity - the big three clouds are short of capacity, buyers overpay, and hundreds of smaller neoclouds have supply they cannot sell because they cannot write the contracts a bank’s lawyers require. TrustedRouter already sells capacity on demand; a spot market and futures contracts are planned for early next year. Sierra’s Ashu Guleri frames the endgame as inevitability: once a model’s weights are public, its tokens become a commodity, and commodities always end up in exchanges. Frontier labs will keep selling what he calls premium tokens; the rest of the market flattens into a spread business. The benchmark is OpenRouter, which Stripe agreed to buy in August in a deal reported at $7.5 billion - and Perla says enterprises avoid it precisely because it lacks end-to-end encryption. Two open questions: tokens from different models are not obviously fungible, and standardization is the boring work that decides whether this becomes a market or stays a brokerage; and the big clouds can bolt confidential computing onto their own stacks faster than a $6 million startup can build liquidity. The direction - privacy as infrastructure rather than promise - still feels right.

Tata goes to court

The biggest boardroom story in India is now a judicial question. A majority of Tata Trusts trustees have backed a lawsuit challenging Natarajan Chandrasekaran’s reappointment as chairman of Tata Sons; the trusts, associated with Noel Tata, moved the Bombay High Court, with the hearing today - ahead of a Tata Sons board meeting on November 30 where the third term was expected to be formalized. The trusts control roughly two-thirds of the holding company of India’s most storied conglomerate, so this is the single most consequential governance fight in the country, and a split among the trustees resolved in open court is without modern precedent. The immediate tells: what the court does with any interim asks today, and whether the November 30 board meeting proceeds on schedule.

Deepening markets, and quick hits

Tobacco consolidation: Japan Tobacco and Altria are exploring a joint takeover of Imperial Brands, the British maker of Kool menthols and Rizla papers; Imperial says it would consider a proposal but has received no approach - exploration, not a bid. Financial deepening on two rungs of one ladder: two Adani companies are preparing to tap India’s public bond market for at least $220 million - small money aimed at a market that remains tiny versus private placements, after Adani Enterprises’ January sale was snapped up within an hour - while the Pakistan Stock Exchange, where stocks have tripled in three years, prepares monthly single-stock options by June, borrowing expertise from its Chinese owners and lessons from India. India’s deep-tech day: GalaxEye and Bellatrix Aerospace signed a multi-year deal for Arka Hall-effect thrusters across 30 satellites of the Drishti Gen-II constellation, billed as India’s largest private-to-private space order by satellite count; Cosine Robots raised ₹22.36 crore (~$2.4 million) led by Avaana Capital to build high-precision actuators with a 25 kHz control loop; ePlane takes an air-ambulance eVTOL to European Rotors in Lyon with Apollo Hospitals backing and 2027 certification tests; QNu Labs signed an MoU with NIELIT on quantum-safe skills. The government’s scorecard: the bio-economy at $195 billion, from $10 billion a decade ago - a PIB claim, so the state’s accounting, not an audit. Tariffs: Indian goods at 17%, rising to 25% without a deal; officials say the deal is nearly done. Calendar: the Fed blackout starts Saturday; Jensen Huang testifies behind closed doors before three Republican-led House committees on Monday, December 1 (and remember, the Journal’s denial on the spy story was specific to coordination); the rate decision lands December 9. Also on watch: Huawei teasing a next-gen high-bandwidth memory chip, and Xpeng promising a humanoid retail robot in Q2 at roughly the price of a car.
