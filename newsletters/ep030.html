Liquidity is a market you have to build

An interval fund holds assets that are hard to sell, such as real estate or private loans, and still sells shares to ordinary investors. To square that, it promises to buy back a slice of its shares at set intervals at net asset value, the manager's own estimate of what the holdings are worth. Only a slice. If everyone asks at once, each gets a fraction and waits for the next window. Per Morningstar, cited by Bloomberg's Going Private, semi-liquid funds held about $600 billion at the end of March, up more than 120 percent from the end of 2022.

This is a bank's trick with the safety on. The fund limits the run in advance so it never has to sell buildings at a fire-sale price. That protects the fund. It does little for the investor who needs cash. Partners Group, the same newsletter says, is revamping a key European evergreen private equity fund it gated earlier this year. The SEC wants to let more retail money into private markets. That only works if there is a credible way out.

A first-of-its-kind auction may point to one. Run by LODAS Markets with Nasdaq Fund Secondaries and Harrison Street Private Wealth, it let holders of the $1.4 billion Harrison Street Real Estate Fund sell outside the scheduled repurchase windows. Sellers and buyers each chose from preset discounts to NAV, and every trade struck at a single clearing price. Take a $10 NAV. A seller willing to take $8 and a buyer willing to pay $9.50 both trade at the one price where supply meets demand, say $8.80. Nobody gains by guessing what others will do, so the best move is to state the real price. Those numbers are illustrative, not from the auction.

The reported results: 91 percent of the stock offered was sold, about two-fifths of the units in the fund sold above asking prices, and some sellers who offered at a 20 percent discount to NAV cleared at 15. All buyers were non-affiliated secondary buyers. The fund did not have to sell property or draw on its cash, and holders who did not take part were unaffected. A second auction, for the Harrison Street Real Assets Fund, launched Friday. LODAS already runs secondary markets for BREIT and SREIT.

This is not really a story about a discount. It is about what the discount says. NAV in a private fund is an appraisal. A clearing price is a market test of that appraisal. Some managers will welcome the test. Others will be quiet. LODAS's chief executive calls it game-changing for the industry, which is a founder's claim. Morningstar's Max Curtin is more measured: putting the decision in shareholders' hands seems more investor-friendly than waiting for an unsolicited offer. For an India-focused fund, it matters at the limited partner level. If wealth platforms start buying alternatives, they will ask what the exit looks like.

The bigger the market number, the worse the sign

Spencer Jakab's Markets A.M. column takes aim at the total addressable market. Casper's 2020 prospectus cited a $432 billion global sleep economy. It sought $17 to $19 a share, priced at $12, and was taken private a year later with the stock at $3.55. WeWork claimed a $3 trillion TAM based on nearly every office worker on Earth, reached a $47 billion private valuation, pulled its 2019 IPO and filed for bankruptcy in 2023. Uber cited $5.7 trillion for personal mobility, Airbnb $3.4 trillion. SpaceX's June IPO documents cited the largest actionable TAM in human history at $28.5 trillion.

There are two ways to size a market. Top-down starts with a giant industry and takes a slice: 1 percent of $432 billion is $4.3 billion. It takes five minutes. Bottom-up counts customers you can reach, multiplies by what each pays, and makes you name the buyers. It is slower and harder to defend, which is the point. Jakab adds that these figures are oddly specific, a habit science writer Charles Seife calls disestimation: precision that hides uncertainty. He also cites Jay Ritter's finding that nearly 4,000 loss-making companies returned on average negative 20.5 percent over three years relative to the market after listing. The first-day pop goes to insiders and favored buyers. Individual investors rarely get it.

Source: Wall Street Journal, Markets A.M. (Spencer Jakab), Oct 2, 2026 (read in full from the email). Paywalled for readers.

Capacity follows committed orders, not maybes

a16z's post on the American manufacturing asset class argues that defense tech has had its funding boom, with money flowing to companies such as Anduril, Castelion, Saronic and K2 Space, but that production at scale depends on thousands of small suppliers that venture has not funded. It cites the 2022 Economic Census: 16,876 machine shops, of which 83 percent of those operating all year employed fewer than 20 people. About three-quarters of the country's 240,644 manufacturing employers are that small. It also reports that 61 percent of tier-two-and-below defense manufacturers put tooling, automation or production-line limits in their top three barriers to expansion.

The mechanism is demand uncertainty. A prototype can use an absurdly priced one-off part. A line that makes thousands needs machines, trained people, inventory and process qualification, all before a buyer commits. The post says Anduril cannot begin production of its FQ-44 Fury aircraft without the Air Force's $1.1 billion FY27 procurement request being funded. Its phrase: sellers need buyer dollars. This is an investor's thesis, not a finding. But it rhymes with India's production-linked incentive, where building the plant does not guarantee a payment. In both cases, capacity follows committed orders.

Source: a16z, "The Case for the American Manufacturing Asset Class," Oct 2, 2026 (read from the email). Public newsletter.

Airfare caps would trade price for capacity

The Core's Signal Brief starts from a personal anecdote: a New Delhi to Srinagar round trip for the October 2 weekend that cost about Rs 15,000 when first checked, and a fear that waiting would cost more. Airlines blame fuel costs from the conflict in West Asia and a weaker rupee. Aviation consultant Mark Martin blames behaviour. A public interest petition in the Supreme Court argues air travel is an essential service and seeks fare caps and limits on charges like excess baggage. The piece flags the trade-off. If prices cannot rise when demand surges, airlines have less reason to add capacity, and last-minute seats get harder to find.

Source: The Core, The Signal Brief (Kudrat Wadhwa), "Why Are Airlines Charging You So Much?," Oct 3, 2026 (read in full from the email). Public newsletter.

Quick hits: an avatar that interviews, and a mirror cell

Synthesia's chief executive had his AI avatar survey more than 700 employees about how they use AI. He set the questions. The avatar followed each person's lead with follow-ups. He says answers were strong and drew no pushback, and argues the format is more convenient and deeper than a static form. Company figures: 78 percent of learners tried more than one roleplay and 83 percent improved their score. He draws a line at anyone using his avatar without his knowledge and says new norms are needed, comparing it to having your camera off on a call: fine in a car, rude in a boardroom.

The Economist's daily email carries a teaser on mirror life. Many molecules in living things come in two mirror-image forms, like left and right hands. The Economist argues regulators must act before a cell with reversed handedness becomes real, and calls the prospect potentially terrifying. We have only the teaser. The explanation of handedness is general background, not from the article.
