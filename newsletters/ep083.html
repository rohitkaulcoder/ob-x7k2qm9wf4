The spy case that wasn’t

Three days is all it took for the restoration to become the reckoning. The Journal reports that U.S. officials told Congress there is no evidence China coordinated Jensen Huang’s affair: the relationship, people familiar with the briefing said, “may have been amorous, not a coordinated play” by Beijing in the race for advanced chips. Read the sequence out loud, because it is absurd. The board fired its CEO citing a national security risk. The president reinstated him after extracting assurances about national security. And now the intelligence community itself tells Congress the coordinated-spy case was never really there. The market’s verdict on day three: Nvidia fell 3.9% to $149.30, Anthropic rose 1.1% to $82.79 - still below its $83 IPO price - and Dell held its post-earnings level at $297.06. Monday was politics, Tuesday was arithmetic; Wednesday was governance.

S&P discovers governance

The bill arrived from the most boring possible sender. S&P warned Wednesday it may downgrade Nvidia’s credit rating, saying the drama “exposed vulnerabilities in Nvidia’s management and governance framework.” Martin Peers’ response is the right one: congratulations on noticing what was hiding in plain sight. The board had no chairperson until two weeks ago, a fact disclosed in filings for years; there is no succession plan because Huang does not believe in them; the board tenure S&P suddenly finds excessive was always public. Obvious does not mean wrong - a downgrade may still be warranted - but the timing says everything about how ratings agencies work: they move after the bond market does. Recall Tuesday’s tell: credit default swaps on the hyperscalers kept rising straight through the reinstatement rally. The CDS market cleared this path; S&P is walking down it. Newcomer’s Rule of Law column frames the institutional cost: the board had a clean alternative - a leave of absence while its outside lawyers finished - and instead reversed under political pressure before its own probe or the federal investigations concluded. Process is now optional if your CEO is rich enough. That is not a scandal anymore; it is a precedent.

The two-jaw vice on Chinese AI

The Information’s exclusive on the new Commerce rule is the most consequential story of the week, and it has two jaws. Jaw one is chips: with smuggling largely shut down, Chinese labs had shifted to renting Blackwells in data centers outside China - Moonshot got the vast majority of its Nvidia compute that way, and it built Kimi K3 on it. The new rule gives every non-U.S. data center holding Nvidia chips 90 days to get a U.S. license if it serves Chinese end-users; Moonshot has already scrapped a planned $150 million deal in Indonesia. Jaw two is distillation - training a student model on a teacher’s outputs - which Anthropic and OpenAI have been policing aggressively since September with fingerprinted outputs (statistically uncommon word choices), degraded answers to suspicious patterns, and rate-limit tripwires. Inside DeepSeek, researchers now estimate the capability gap could widen to nine months from three to six. The response is a forced march to domestic silicon: MiniMax and Mihoyo to Huawei chips, Alibaba to its own T-Head - “domestic chips will be underpinning all of that training,” Eddie Wu said - if SMIC’s equipment-starved capacity allows it. And note the glut connection from Tuesday: one rule, two effects. Squeeze China, flood America.

The safety movement turns on Anthropic

Anthropic built its brand as the safety company, and the safety community is now publicly reconsidering. Ronny Fernandez of Lightcone Infrastructure says the company seems “more like a cult devoted to creating the machine gods as soon as possible.” Max Tegmark, whose institute gave Anthropic its best grade in the summer - a C+ - now calls it “in many ways the AI company doing the most harm.” Over a thousand researchers signed a statement arguing Anthropic uses safety branding while quietly weakening its pledges. The specific grievance is the METR report: the pace of development inside Anthropic has doubled via AI automation, but Anthropic redacted “a majority of the evidence we found most informative.” Meanwhile the transparency lead has flipped: OpenAI published its acceleration data and Jakub Pachocki’s internal progress graph with a call for industry standards; Microsoft followed; Anthropic has not. Fernandez supplies the cynical and probably correct reading: transparency is cheap for the runner-up and expensive for the leader. The business question is what the safety brand is worth to a $1.5 trillion fully diluted valuation when its most loyal constituency is writing open letters - in the same week the stock trades below its IPO price.

The IPO that worked, and volatility as a product

Quiz: which of this year’s IPOs has stayed consistently above its offer price? Not SpaceX, not Anthropic, not SK Hynix. The answer is Bending Spoons, the Italian buyer of the internet’s broken toys - AOL, Vimeo, Evernote, Eventbrite, Airtable - public since July at $29, closed Wednesday at $46.29. The tell is the Mailchimp deal: $3.68 billion, or 2.9 times trailing revenue, for the same asset Intuit bought in 2021 at $12 billion and 15 times trailing. The whole market pays up for narrative; the one stock that never broke issue pays three times sales for boring cash flows. Meanwhile bankers are turning the volatility itself into a product: companies whose charts look like an EKG are selling stock bundled with warrants at a premium instead of a discount. IonQ raised $2 billion in mid-October at a 15% premium - $5 billion across three such offerings - while the stock went from $82 last October to about $23.50 now; what investors really bought was the option on the rebound. And one hopeful note for the holiday: stocks have closed below their open nine straight days, October core PCE came in hot at 3.3% versus 3.2% expected, and yet Treasuries rallied, the 10-year yield down from above 5.15% last week. The old inverse correlation is showing its face again. Your hedges are hedging again.

Quick hits and what to watch

Tariffs: the new U.S. levies on China, the EU and 14 others would raise rates to 20% and potentially double to 40% if talks fail, per a senior U.S. official; China’s foreign ministry called it political maneuvering. Benchmarks: SemiAnalysis released GDPx, which scores frontier models only on the domains where they matter economically - most of the economy cannot justify paying for Fable 5.2, let alone Fable 7. India: Bain Capital’s Ivy Entertainment has deployed about $150 million into Indian regional-language music and film royalties - Bhojpuri and Telugu, not Bollywood Hindi - against 6 trillion streams in 2025, paid subscriptions that could double to 30 million by 2028, Hindi’s market share down to 59%, and royalties to Indian artists up 29% last year per Spotify. Streaming turned Indian music into a yield asset; the pattern is Bending Spoons logic applied to catalogues. Into the long weekend: House testimony December 1 and Senate Banking December 3, now five and seven days out; whether S&P actually downgrades; and the 90-day clock on every non-U.S. data center serving Chinese customers.
