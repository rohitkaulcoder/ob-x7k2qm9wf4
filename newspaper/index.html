Dario Amodei Enters the Tabloid

The New York Post has turned Anthropic’s chief and effective altruism into daily villains. The contradiction is revealing: Americans fear AI catastrophe and resent the people who ask them to fear it.

More than a dozen Post stories in a week framed Dario Amodei, effective altruists and AI-safety evaluators as a “cult,” a “sick conspiracy” and “super-woke globalists.” This is not a policy argument about embedded evaluators. It is the translation of a technical dispute into populist identity: liberal elites seeking power over technology.

The public attitude underneath it is unstable. A Politico survey found 63 per cent of US adults believe AI could eventually destroy the world. Yet apocalypse advocates attract ridicule and suspicion. People can fear the outcome while distrusting the institutions that claim expertise over it - especially when those institutions are wealthy, unelected and culturally distant.

The alleged cabal is smaller than the coverage suggests. The Information’s influence list contains Dario and Daniela Amodei, but no broad effective-altruist bloc. That does not mean their ideas lack reach; Anthropic is a major lab. It means the press can inflate a network into an all-purpose enemy, replacing institutional mapping with familiar conspiratorial shapes.

The political risk is bigger than reputational pain for one executive. Safety policy needs legitimacy. If evaluation becomes identified with one ideology, rules about network isolation or incident reporting can be rejected as factional control. The response cannot be better branding alone. Oversight must be plural, transparent and tied to mechanisms the public can inspect.

Dario Amodei’s Tabloid Era
By Abram Brown

Honestly, I almost feel a little sorry for Dario Amodei. That essay-writing, sweater-loving nerd is getting the treatment normally meted out to grim-eyed murderers and sex-romping celebrities.

Put another way: The New York Post has discovered him in earnest. Well, him and effective altruism. By my count, the outlet has published more than a dozen stories mentioning Amodei or effective altruism in the past week. One had a headline describing Anthropic’s staff as “cult-like,” the same label it had applied a while back to effective altruists in general. (C’mon, fellas! Invest in a thesaurus.)

A second Post headline gave prominent attention to President Donald Trump’s recent criticism of Amodei’s suggestion for an AI industry slowdown: a “SICK conspiracy,” Trump called it, the capital letters all his. A third headline described METR, the AI research outfit Amodei says he’ll use to independently assess his company’s safeguards, as a group of “super-woke globalists.” And a fourth headline on an editorial board piece thundered out: “Beware the ‘effective altruists.’”

Eeesh. I wonder what would excite the mouth breathers at the Post more: another Amodei doom dump—or JonBenét Ramsey’s parents finally saying they did it?

Sure, Amodei has steadily attracted more and more—and more—media attention over the past five months, and so has effective altruism, which obviously got a lot of attention in the Sam Bankman-Fried days, too. But there’s something about watching Amodei and the EA set get turned into daily tabloid fodder right now that strikes me as an important marker of where the broader world’s conversation about AI stands. It’s loud, wacky as heck—and candidly, I worry it’s a precursor to something darker.

I sat here trying to wrap my mind around it, and I started to get a headache like the one that sits at the bottom of a bourbon bottle. The Post is channeling a populist antipathy for Amodei and effective altruists that’s rooted in a dislike for their AI apocalypse talk. Yet at the same time there’s populist concern that AI may very well invite Armageddon: A recent survey by Politico found that 63% of U.S. adults believe AI could eventually destroy the world. So in sum, there’s widespread concern about destructive AI—and scorn for the people who want everyone to focus on it. As I said, it’s wacky.

Put aside AI catastrophe for a second. The bigger reason people dislike Amodei and the effective altruists is because they perceive them as the influential, liberal elite. It’s this potent derision that really animates the Post, and I find it unsettling. It’s the same type of addled, conspiracy-minded resentment that makes people still believe in Pizzagate and rigged voting machines.

What I find especially facile is the assumption that effective altruists and those who share their beliefs actually do wield gigantic influence over AI. I’d argue that they actually don’t. Articles by the Post and other outlets portray effective altruists as a wide cabal, but they only ever really mention the same three people over and over: Amodei; his sister, Daniela; and Facebook billionaire Dustin Moskovitz.

Look, three people simply do not make an all-powerful cabal! I keep thinking about that disconnect between assumption and reality—probably because I’ve devoted enormous thought lately to considering who really does have clout in AI as we put together our debut list of Tech’s 50 Most Influential People. Yes, the Amodei siblings are on the list, and they’re the No. 1 and No. 2 figures at a gigantic company. But that’s…it. Not a single other person on our list belongs to the effective altruism crowd.

I don’t write this as a passionate Amodei fanboy, nor as someone who finds effective altruism especially appealing: It’s an ideology that can be channeled into single-minded glumness when someone swallows the whole pill bottle and commits fully to it. A conversation with an effective altruist about the future is about as much fun as discussing foie gras with a vegan. It’s just that the concerns about Amodei and the effective altruists are beginning to strongly remind me of previous worries about lizard people taking over Congress.

Perhaps I’m making too much of those Post stories I mentioned. Still, with nearly 100 million readers a month, the Post is big enough to inspire other outlets to follow its lead, covering Amodei and AI with a sensationalism rooted in gassed-up anxiety and quarter-truths. Amodei’s the guy getting tarred and feathered today. It’d be a mistake to assume he’ll be the last.—Abram Brown (abe@theinformation.com)

Listening: “Embedded: Where Is Austin Tice?”

In 2012, Austin Tice, a former U.S. Marine who’d decided to try his hand at freelance foreign correspondence, went MIA in Syria. And he’s still missing 14 years later. His mother clings to hope that he’s alive. Every president since Barack Obama has vowed to bring him back. But if he has somehow survived, why hasn’t he surfaced since the Assad regime fell in 2024? Josh Baker and Sara Obeidat, a pair of veteran Middle East reporters, set out to try to answer that question in “Embedded: Where Is Austin Tice?,” an NPR-BBC joint production.

They’re well sourced and determined, and by episode 2, they’re in an infamous prison retrieving never-before-seen files that the Syrian government kept on Tice. This involves a little bit of Bond-esque derring-do from Baker, who risks a five-story fall from a window ledge to get from one barricaded room to another. Additionally, I appreciated that they could display obvious empathy for Tice’s loved ones and stop short of lionizing him. He was clearly ill prepared to be in Syria. It was his very first attempt at freelance reporting. His military experience had emboldened him to go and made him a popular talking head for TV networks looking for on-the-ground analysis, but it also made many Syrians think he was in actuality an American spy posing as a journo.—Abram Brown

Reading: “Valley of Death” by Sharon Weinberger

A year or so before Donald Trump’s reelection, Sharon Weinberger, then national security editor for The Wall Street Journal, visited the offices of an unnamed but prominent venture capital firm. Yes, the tech world invasion of Washington that has happened in the second Trump administration was still far in the future, but even then, Weinberger had begun noticing how Silicon Valley and Washington were intersecting more than they had in the past—particularly when she looked at the venture capital going to defense tech startups.

At the VC firm, Weinberger asked the investor why the private sector would invest in technology like hypersonic weapons given the astronomical costs and uncertain business models. “I honestly don’t care about the product,” replied the investor. “It has nothing to do with the tech.” Instead, he argued, the success of the products—and his own returns—depended more on government relationships and lobbying, which was increasingly a strength for Silicon Valley firms.

By Weinberger’s own account, she found herself thinking often of that investor’s sentiment as she wrote “Valley of Death,” an account of how Silicon Valley strayed from its Cold War national security roots before returning to the corridors of the Pentagon in recent years.

Weinberger offers an engaging history, with details of untold episodes in the intertwined relationships between Silicon Valley and Washington, like Steve Jobs’ brush with the CIA. As she goes along, she illustrates how the tech titans of defense contracting such as Elon Musk, Palantir’s Alex Karp and Anduril’s Palmer Luckey have strong-armed their way into the staid field. And she details how they’ve been adept at talking up their companies, though their products haven’t always lived up to the hype—especially in Ukraine.

Weinberger sees shortcomings in their “techno-optimism,” as she puts it. She thinks it could lead to ruinous scenarios not only for the firms’ financial backers but also for the U.S. armed forces at a time of heightened conflicts and supply chain constraints. Weinberger is a measured and well-traveled chronicler of the Silicon Valley arms race, raising the same concerns I’ve heard some investors share out loud.

By the time she ends with President Dwight Eisenhower’s timeless warning of the dangers of the military-industrial complex, or “subordinating national security to private interests,” it’s clear why the current moment of defense tech feels deeply familiar.—Leo Schwartz

Watching: “Hope”

Up near the 38th parallel, the fictional South Korean hamlet of Hope Harbor—the setting of director Na Hong-jin’s latest movie, “Hope”—exists in a state of permanent paranoia, with North Korea not very far away. Signs implore residents to “REPORT SPIES” and “PROTECT THE NATION AGAINST INFILTRATION.” And everyone seems to have a stash of automatic weapons hidden away at home, much to the irritation of the town’s chief constable, Bum-seok (Hwang Jung-min). But it doesn’t seem likely that communist commandos are responsible for the body of a mutilated steer just outside of town. A bear, maybe—or a tiger? No, something much, much bigger.

Na, clearly a devotee of the Spielbergian school of patient dread, waits almost an hour before getting that giant alien beastie on screen. Kudos to him. As is often the case, the buildup makes the payoff sweeter. Once the creature finally does smash its way fully into frame, “Hope” shifts into overdrive, with a number of genuinely exciting set pieces and chase sequences: I emphasize the plural there because, unfortunately for Bum-seok and his more able deputy, Sung-ae (Hoyeon), the town is threatened by an entire pack of these lanky-limbed E.T.s. They’re darn difficult to take down, and they play possum pretty well. And since “Hope” takes place sometime in the pre-cellphone past, backup is not a text message away.

The set decoration of ruined Hope Harbor looks fantastic. And the mayhem is captured well through the camerawork of Hong Kyung-pyo, who also did “Parasite” as well as “Snowpiercer.” His efforts mitigate the letdown of the CGI effects: It’s a puzzler that the movie can rank as the most expensive Korean film ever and not have managed better. Honestly, though, the lackluster CGI was far from bad enough to dim my enjoyment. I trust the aliens will get jazzed up when Na returns for his already greenlit sequel.—A.B.

Editorial · Legitimacy Is a Control

Technical systems fail when authority is ambiguous. Political systems do too. Amodei’s evaluators may have good methods and still lack public legitimacy. Accenture may have deep access and still lack independence. Influence agents may produce crude posts and still exploit platforms whose identity controls are weak.

Safety cannot be separated from who is allowed to judge, sell and act. A control that nobody trusts will be evaded. An auditor paid by the vendor will be doubted. An account system that treats every new identity as real will be industrialized by agents.

Legitimacy is not public relations. It is an architecture of conflicts, evidence and appeal.

Oversight · Bloomberg

The Auditor Also Sells the System

Anthropic will give Accenture evaluators employee-like access and the two companies will invest at least $1bn in safety capacity. Independence is complicated by 30,000 consultants trained to sell Claude.

Embedded evaluation answers a real weakness of model testing. A benchmark sees one finished release. An assessor inside the lab can follow how capabilities emerge during training, inspect decisions, red-team safeguards and compare practice with public commitments. Anthropic says Accenture’s people will have access similar to employees.

The conflict is structural. Accenture already partners with Anthropic to deploy Claude, has organized 30,000 people to scale AI systems for clients and sells a security product powered by Claude. Its revenue grows when enterprise adoption grows. The same firm will now assess whether the supplier behind that growth is safe.

Professional services has long combined implementation and assurance, but credible independence requires firewalls: separate leadership and compensation, publication rights, rotation, audit trails and a duty to report incidents even when the commercial partner objects. A billion-dollar joint investment increases capacity while deepening mutual dependence.

The better question is not whether Accenture is “third party.” It is who controls scope, evidence and disclosure. If Anthropic chooses the tests, owns the report and can remove the evaluator, embedded access becomes sophisticated internal consulting. If the evaluator can inspect uninvited, publish exceptions and trigger external review, it begins to resemble supervision.

Anthropic to Embed Accenture Evaluators to Test AI Safety
By Samantha Oltman and Lynn Doan

Anthropic PBC is partnering with Accenture Plc to test the safety of its advanced artificial intelligence models by embedding evaluators from the consulting firm directly within its operations.

Accenture's evaluators will red-team the AI lab's newest models and test their safeguards, as well as conduct assessments to ensure its technology is aligned with human objectives.

Anthropic and Accenture expect to invest at least $1 billion in building capacity in this area, and the partnership will involve Accenture's embedded assessors closely tracking how AI models evolve in training.

Anthropic PBC is partnering with Accenture Plc, a management and technology consulting company, to test the safety of its advanced artificial intelligence models by embedding evaluators from the consulting firm directly within its operations.

Accenture’s evaluators will red-team the AI lab’s newest models and test their safeguards, as well as conduct assessments to ensure its technology is aligned, or acting in line with human objectives, Anthropic said in a Friday blog post. The evaluators will have similar levels of access inside Anthropic as the startup’s own employees, the company said.

Both Anthropic and Accenture, which have an existing relationship, expect to invest at least $1 billion in building capacity in this area, Anthropic said.

On Saturday, Anthropic chief Amodei addressed growing concerns about the security risks of cutting-edge AI models in a 3,800-word essay, calling for a slowdown in development and saying he would implement new safety steps such as third-party evaluators.

Accenture’s embedded assessors will closely track how AI models evolve in training and observe the behind-the-scenes decision-making on how Anthropic is developing its technology, the company said.

“From this vantage point, embedded evaluators can assess how a company operates, verify that it is keeping its safety commitments, and identify blind spots. They can also report incidents and give the public a more informed account of benefits and risks,” Anthropic said.

Last year, Accenture and Anthropic struck a multiyear partnership to deploy AI at companies, forming a group of 30,000 people to be trained on Claude so they can help scale AI-powered systems for clients. And this March, Accenture announced Cyber.AI, a security solution powered by Anthropic’s Claude AI model.

The “third-party” nature of the partnership stands to draw skepticism given the close ties between Accenture and the large AI startups. Earlier this year, the consulting giant projected that its work with key AI partners such as Anthropic, OpenAI and Palantir would more than double in the coming year.

Anxiety about AI’s existential risks gained momentum last week, driven by the high-profile departure of Anthropic employee Jacob Coxon, who accused AI companies of “gambling with our lives” in a resignation post he shared on social media.

In the past several days, numerous AI company leaders, including Amodei and OpenAI Chief Executive Officer Sam Altman, have urged slowing the pace of the development of the technology to address its increasingly unpredictable risks — though they diverge on exactly how it should be handled. Amodei had suggested that companies open themselves to embedded independent evaluators to oversee the development of the technology.

Anthropic on Thursday issued a report detailing that 26% of its AI research and development work was driven by its Claude chatbot, providing an indication of how AI can help speed up development of future models.

Information Warfare
Information Warfare · The New York Times

The Influence Campaign Without Operators

Iran, China and Israeli firms used open models and agents to create accounts, coordinate messages and publish false claims with little human intervention. The campaigns were crude. Their production function was new.

Earlier influence operations used AI to write posts or make profile images while humans created accounts, scheduled content and adjusted the narrative. The new campaigns linked those steps. Hundreds of agents opened fake accounts across Instagram, Facebook, X and TikTok, seeded political claims and coordinated messaging.

The mechanism lowers the fixed cost of manipulation. An operator can define an objective, audience and story, then let agents perform account creation, content generation and posting. Open models matter because safeguards can be removed and weights modified. Agents matter because they convert generated text into persistent action across platforms.

Crudeness is temporary, but scale alone is not persuasion. Networks still need aged accounts, local language, cultural context, credible engagement and adaptation when platforms remove them. Detection teams traced the Chinese and Iranian campaigns to states and two Israeli campaigns to private firms. The defensive contest now moves from spotting synthetic content to identifying coordinated behavior and infrastructure.

For India, the exposure is direct: many languages, intense electoral messaging, large encrypted groups and cheap political outsourcing. Watermarks will not solve an operation built on modified open models. Platforms need provenance for account creation, velocity limits, graph-based detection and rapid cross-platform exchange of campaign indicators.

Iran and China Create First-of-Their-Kind Autonomous A.I. Influence Campaigns
By Sheera Frenkel, Eli Tan and Dustin Volz

Iran, China and groups in Israel used artificial intelligence in novel ways in recent months to create first-of-their-kind influence campaigns on social media, ratcheting up concerns among U.S. officials and security researchers about the fast-evolving technology.

The countries and groups combined A.I. tactics to ramp up the speed and scale of online campaigns beyond what people could easily achieve and with minimal human interaction, U.S. officials and security researchers with knowledge of the efforts said.

First, the countries and groups turned to Chinese “open-source” A.I. models, which are increasingly powerful systems that are freely available to download and modify. Then they used those A.I. models to create “agents,” which are bots that can operate software and complete tasks on their own.

Once hundreds of those A.I. agents were let loose, they autonomously opened networks of fake accounts on platforms such as Instagram, Facebook, X and TikTok, the officials and security researchers said. The agents then rapidly seeded the fake accounts with false posts about politics and current events to sway opinions and inflame issues.

It was one of the first times that A.I. agents backed by Chinese models were used to handle every stage of influence campaigns, from the creation of the fake accounts to coordinating their messaging online, said the U.S. officials, who were not authorized to speak publicly on sensitive issues. In most of the cases, the safeguards that would normally stop an A.I. model from creating a fake account or manipulating discussions online were turned off by those operating the systems, they added.

The “agentic” campaigns were crude, but caught the attention of U.S. intelligence, major tech companies and security experts, the people with knowledge of the campaigns said. These groups then worked to detect who was behind the efforts and their methods, finding that the Chinese and the Iranian campaigns were state-backed, while two Israeli campaigns were traced to private companies.
