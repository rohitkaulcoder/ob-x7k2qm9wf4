The Permit Is the Scarce Chip

Forty-five US data-centre projects worth $68bn were blocked or delayed in one quarter. The AI build has discovered a bottleneck that cannot be ordered from Nvidia: local consent.

Data Center Watch counted 843 opposition groups across 49 states. Thirty statehouses introduced or adopted rules on siting, power and water. More than half of the large projects tracked between April and June faced disruption. This is not an isolated environmental campaign; it is a distributed permitting constraint.

The mechanism starts with concentration. A hyperscale facility demands power, transmission, substations, water and tax concessions in one place. National benefits - cloud capacity and AI leadership - are diffuse. Costs arrive on a particular grid, aquifer and neighborhood. Local residents therefore have a strong incentive to organize while the eventual users remain abstract.

Developers can buy generators, chips and cooling equipment. They cannot manufacture a social licence on the same schedule. Moratoriums imposed before applications shift the risk forward: land and interconnection plans may lose value before construction begins. A $68bn pipeline disruption is also a financing event because delay extends interest and pushes revenue beyond debt assumptions.

The backlash is going global. Campaigns in Europe, Australia and South Africa target the same resources. For India, the lesson follows last week’s financing stories: patient capital and subsidy are insufficient without state-level power planning, transparent water accounting and benefits residents can see. The marginal gigawatt will be won in public hearings.

New Data Centers Worth $68 Billion Disrupted in US, Data Show
By Newley Purnell

Opposition to data centers continues to mount in the US, with some 45 data center projects worth $68 billion blocked or delayed by local pushback between April and June this year.

Communities across the country are pushing through moratoriums on new construction, and there are now 843 opposition groups across 49 states, with Hawaii the exception.

Data Center Watch reported on protests against data centers outside the US for the first time, given increased international opposition, with local campaigns targeting specific projects and resource usage in European nations, Australia, South Africa, and beyond.

Opposition to data centers continues to mount in the US.

That’s according to research group Data Center Watch, which said some 45 data center projects worth $68 billion were blocked or delayed by local pushback between April and June this year.

Communities across the country are now pushing through moratoriums on new construction, often before developers can apply for permissions. Some 30 statehouses introduced or adopted rules pertaining to issues such as data center siting, electricity and water, the group said in a report Monday provided to Bloomberg News.

The research quantifies growing concern about the rapid pace of expansion of artificial intelligence, both in the construction of new computing clusters and its proliferation into daily life. There are now 843 opposition groups across 49 states, with Hawaii the exception.

“New groups continue to emerge, opposition is appearing across a wide range of states and jurisdictions, and online petition signatures continue to grow,” lead analyst Miquel Vila said. Data Center Watch is a project from AI intelligence company 10a Labs.

Home to the biggest investors in AI infrastructure, the US is experiencing a bipartisan backlash against facilities that critics say harm the environment and siphon away resources in order to fuel AI systems that many fear will imperil jobs and livelihoods.

Tech giants like Amazon.com Inc., Meta Platforms Inc., Microsoft Corp. and Alphabet Inc. — the so-called hyperscalers — argue they need the facilities to win a race against China to develop potentially world-changing AI. President Donald Trump has dismissed opposition to data centers and concern about advances in AI as a “sick conspiracy,” saying “the only one that is happy about it is China.”

The number and value of disrupted projects fluctuates by quarter, with the first three months of this year seeing 75 cases worth roughly $130 billion facing opposition in the US. The 45 projects affected during the second quarter represented more than half of all new large-scale developments Data Center Watch began tracking during the period.

Opposition by public figures and the use of social media are amplifying concerns. One petition against a project in Tennessee garnered over 500,000 signatures on online activism platform Change.org, accounting for more than one third of those on the service during the quarter, Data Center Watch said.

For the first time since it began its research two years ago, Data Center Watch also reported on protests against data centers outside the US, given increased international opposition. Much as in the US, local campaigns are targeting specific projects, governments’ planning authorizations and worries about resource usage, sometimes coordinating nationally. The group highlighted opposition across European nations, Australia, South Africa and beyond.

“As with many other trends, the US has generally been at the forefront, but the phenomenon is increasingly becoming global,” said Vila. “We anticipate that this will just become more prominent as data center development expands across the globe.”

Editorial · Capacity Is Political

Today’s covers show three ways capacity becomes political. Data centres need communities to accept power and water use. Apple needs affluent customers to accept a new price tier. China needs households to feel that strategic investment reaches ordinary life.

Engineering can increase supply. It cannot decide who bears the cost or receives the benefit. Those allocations are made through permits, instalment plans, pensions and public trust.

The AI race is described as a contest for chips and models. It is increasingly a contest for consent.

Apple’s $27 Billion Hinge

IDC expects ten million iPhone Duo units and $27bn of first-year revenue. Apple is using a $1,999 foldable not to conquer the mass market, but to restart the upgrade clock at the very top.

Global foldable revenue rose 16 per cent last year, more than twice the rate for conventional phones. Yet foldables were only 2 per cent of North American smartphone sales. Apple’s opportunity is not invention. It is category validation for customers who ignored Samsung and Huawei.

At ten million units, average revenue would reach roughly $2,700 once higher-storage models are included. That would equal about 6 per cent of Apple’s expected fiscal-2026 revenue. The Duo can matter financially while remaining a niche by unit volume because it raises mix and gives high-end buyers a reason to upgrade sooner.

Financing does the segmentation. Trade-ins can cut almost $900 from the sticker price; instalments turn a shocking capital cost into a monthly comparison. Apple preserves a premium headline while carriers and residual values soften the purchase. The hinge is therefore also a pricing architecture.

The risk is substitution rather than expansion. A buyer who chooses a Duo may skip a Pro Max and perhaps an iPad. First-generation battery, camera and durability compromises can also make novelty a one-cycle spike. Apple must prove the inner screen creates a repeated habit, not merely a record average selling price.

Why Apple Is Betting on Foldable iPhones
By Tiffany Li

When new Apple CEO John Ternus showed off the first version of a foldable iPhone at an event earlier this month, there was a good chance many Americans watching clips of the video stream had never thought hard about buying such a device.

But Apple is betting that the same demand that’s driven double-digit growth of such phones outside North America for Huawei, Samsung and others will boost sales of its flagship iPhones. Demand for such smartphones, whose hinged sides open to one large glass screen, should also make an expected drop in global smartphone shipments this year less severe than it otherwise would have been.

The Takeaway

IDC projects 10 million iPhone Duo units sold in first year.

Foldable iPhone Duo could generate $27 billion in first year revenue.

Apple bets on foldables to drive sales.

Powered by Deep Research

Market researcher IDC expects Apple to sell 10 million units of the new iPhone Duo, which starts at $1,999 with 256 gigabytes of storage, in the first 12 months after its launch.

“The foldable can bring that novelty and can drive those high high-end customers that are willing to pay whatever Apple charges for the latest and greatest they can bring, especially when it’s a new product,” said Francisco Jeronimo, vice president for data and analytics at IDC in Europe, Middle East, and Africa.

Apple has in recent years tried to spice up each new iPhone release with better cameras and chips, but revenue from the devices had remained largely flat from 2022 to 2025, rising just 4% in the fiscal year ending in September 2025, to nearly $210 billion. (iPhone sales jumped 22% in the June quarter due to the popularity of the iPhone 17 line, however, due in part to demand from buyers who last upgraded their iPhones four years ago.)

The iPhone Duo could further lift sales by incentivizing people to switch phones more frequently, as they used to, to get the newest must-have model, Jeronimo said.

IDC projects that Apple’s first 10 million units will generate $27 billion in revenue, including from more expensive models of Duo with greater amounts of storage. That would amount to about 6% of total expected revenue for its fiscal 2026 year, according to brokerage forecasts tracked by Yahoo.

Apple’s first foldable phone is far more expensive than any iPhone Apple has previously released. But high prices haven’t discouraged people from buying similar foldable phones from Apple’s rivals in other parts of the world.

Samsung, which released the first mainstream foldable smartphone in 2019, has been touting the pre-order demand for foldable phones, such as the $1900 Galaxy Z Fold8.

Globally, revenue from foldable smartphones last year rose 16% to $27 billion, while nonfoldable sales rose 7% to $550 billion, says IDC.

Much of the growth has occurred in Asian markets like China and South Korea, with foldable phones making up just 2% of all North American smartphone sales last year, according to Smart Analytics Global.

Linda Sui, founder of that market research firm, believes there is room for growth in the U.S., particularly because Apple customers have shown they are willing to pay for higher-priced phones. Apple’s existing customer base for already expensive Pro or Pro Max iPhone models could easily transition to the iPhone Duo, Sui said.

In addition, customers can lower the upfront cost of the phones by trading in existing iPhones. For customers trading in some recent models, that could lower the cost of the Duo almost $900 from its sticker price.

Sui also notes that installment plans, which allow shoppers to spread the cost of a new iPhone out across multiple months, are becoming increasingly popular and could help consumers justify the cost of the Duo. She projects $6 million Duo units sold in 2026 alone.

Apple’s star power might increase U.S. consumption of foldable phones overall, including of more affordable alternatives from Samsung or others.

“Apple is going to educate the market and drive up the market adoption rate,” Sui said.

The Frontier Cannot Reflate an Economy

China is closing the AI gap while youth unemployment reaches 18.9 per cent, car sales fall 20 per cent and housing drops another 14 per cent. Technological success and economic weakness are not opposites. They share an allocation choice.

Beijing can concentrate credit, talent, power and procurement on strategic technologies. That helps models and hardware advance quickly. The same system can underweight household income and consumption. Former central-bank advisers now say the economy is running too cold and high-tech growth cannot lift the larger base.

AI is capital- and skill-intensive. It can improve national capability without absorbing enough labor to repair an 18.9 per cent youth unemployment rate. A new model may raise productivity while adding fewer jobs than services, health care or consumer businesses constrained by weak demand.

Deflation makes the imbalance self-reinforcing. Falling property and car sales reduce household confidence; weak spending discourages broad hiring; the state responds with more investment in sectors it can direct. Exportable technology then looks stronger beside a domestic economy that feels poorer.

One proposal would raise rural basic pensions from $30 to $150 a month. That is not anti-technology. It is complementary infrastructure for demand. The strategic lesson for India is to resist a false choice: fund chips and AI, but also build purchasing power, education and services that distribute their gains. A frontier model is not a macroeconomic policy.

China’s A.I. Makes a Great Leap Forward. But Its Economy Is Falling Behind.
By Li Yuan

“The U.S. Lead Over China in AI Is All But Gone.”

“China Is AI-Maxxing.”

“China Just Erased America’s AI Lead.”

Those assessments of the artificial intelligence rivalry appeared in U.S. publications recently, and Xi Jinping will be pleased to have them in the air as he arrives in Washington this week for a state visit. Under Mr. Xi’s leadership, China has made huge strides in developing the most consequential technology of our times. What will be less discussed is that he is also the leader of an economy in its worst shape in decades.

The two facts are related.

Economists in China — even those closest to the state — have openly warned that the government is pouring too many resources into a technology that creates relatively few jobs, while doing too little to save the broader economy.

And it’s an economy in crisis: The country’s youth unemployment rate, excluding students, reached 18.9 percent in August. Consumers are not spending. During the first half of the year, domestic car sales fell markedly 20 percent from a year earlier, and housing sales fell another 14 percent, adding to years of decline. The country is in a deflationary spiral.

This summer, the economists’ warnings became a chorus. Li Daokui, a former adviser to the country’s central bank and a professor at Tsinghua University, said in July that China’s economy was “running too cold.” Its booming high-tech sectors, he said, could not lift the larger base.

Liu Shijin, another former adviser to the central bank, proposed at a forum in June to raise basic pension payouts, from $30 to $150 per month, for rural residents to shore up consumer demand.
