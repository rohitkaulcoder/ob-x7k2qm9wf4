Pricing an Hour of Nvidia

CME wants to trade GPU rent. The regulator wants to know who sets the number first. The answer decides how cheaply the AI buildout can borrow.

CME wanted to list two futures contracts on October 5: one on the hourly rental price of Nvidia’s H100, one on the B200, both settled against a benchmark from Silicon Data. The CFTC has pushed its review 45 days, to November 9, citing “novel or complex” issues. The delay is small. The design question is not.

Start with what is being priced. GPU rent today is set in private contracts that vary by term, volume, prepayment, networking and region. Nobody publishes it. The index has to turn that into one number. The future is then a bet on where that number sits on a later date.

Hedging runs both ways. A lab that needs compute next spring buys futures: if rent spikes, the contract gains and offsets the bill. A neocloud that bought chips with debt sells futures: if rent falls, the contract pays and covers the gap in revenue. The Information’s key line is the third user, lenders who hold GPUs as collateral. A chip’s collateral value is roughly the rent it can still earn. Hedge the rent and you can lend against it more cheaply.

Two problems sit underneath. The first is basis risk. A loan against H100s in one data centre, with one network setup, will not move exactly with a national H100 index. Roughly right still helps; jet fuel is hedged with crude. But in a stress case the specific can split from the average.

The second is manipulation, and it is the CFTC’s stated worry. An index built from a thin set of private deals can be pushed by whoever writes the deals. A large cloud that also trades the future could post a few cheap leases, dip the index and collect on a short. This is the LIBOR question again: who reports, can it be checked, how many trades, what happens to outliers.

The part worth watching is the curve. Oil in a barrel is the same oil next year. An H100 next year is further from the frontier. The forward curve for H100 rent should slope down almost by construction, which makes this a depreciating-asset market, closer to aircraft leases than wheat. Separate contracts per chip generation mean the H100–B200 spread becomes a live read on how fast older silicon ages. That is the number every GPU depreciation schedule is currently guessing.

This is actually about who sees the price. Today the big clouds do and everyone else negotiates blind. CME, ICE and several upstart exchanges all want to list. Once a public price exists, private leases start referencing it. The review delays that by weeks. It does not change the direction.

India angleNew Delhi buys subsidised GPU hours for startups and researchers through IndiaAI Mission tenders, with providers bidding hourly rates. A public rental benchmark would give those tenders a market reference, and show whether Indian GPU hours trade at a premium or a discount to global rates. Today that is mostly guesswork.

Wall Street’s GPU Futures Push Stalls at the CFTC
The Information

The launch of futures for rental prices on Nvidia GPUs, which are seen as important to make AI compute an investable asset class, is hitting a snag.

CME Group had hoped to launch contracts as soon as early October, but it won’t have regulatory approval by then. The Commodity Futures Trading Commission, which regulates the futures market, is extending its review as it digs into how such contracts work and seeks comments from the broader industry, according to a letter to the exchange seen by The Information.

In theory, such futures coupled with a liquid market to trade them could let AI companies and compute providers lock in prices for computing power ahead of time, helping them hedge against swings in GPU rental costs. Futures could also provide lenders behind the AI infrastructure financing boom with a way to hedge against the risk of GPUs used as collateral losing value.

CME had announced last month it was aiming to launch compute futures on Oct. 5, pending regulatory approval. The firm would use a benchmark from startup data provider Silicon Data. It planned to start with two new futures products, one based on rental pricing for Nvidia H100s and another for Nvidia’s B200 chips.

But later in August, the CFTC announced a broad request for comment on compute derivatives contracts, seeking to understand potential market manipulation among other risks. The CFTC noted that while a financial market for compute could support U.S. AI development, the underlying market is fragmented and opaque.

For instance, GPU rental prices are typically set in bespoke private contracts as opposed to a big, open market. That raises the prospect that dominant players could influence market prices to their advantage. At the time, the CFTC opened a 60-day comment period to give market participants an opportunity to weigh in on the products.

While that action raised doubts about the CME Group’s ability to meet its October timeline, the CFTC’s Sept. 21 letter confirms that the launch won’t go ahead next month. The CFTC noted that it needed more time to assess the CME launch “in light of novel or complex” issues, and would extend the review period on the products 45 days, until Nov. 9.

In addition to CME, rival exchange firm Intercontinental Exchange has announced plans to launch GPU futures, but hasn’t set a specific date. Upstart exchanges too are vying to offer similar contracts.

“We view additional regulatory review as a natural part of building a new derivatives market,” Carmen Li, CEO of Silicon Data, said in an emailed statement. “So whether the first contracts launch in October or somewhat later doesn’t change the underlying thesis. As more capital flows into AI infrastructure, the need for reliable compute pricing and tools to manage compute price risk is only becoming more important.”

CME Group declined to comment.

Editorial · Who Sets the Price

Three prices were argued over today, and none of them was the price of a model.

The CFTC is asking whether a compute price can be trusted before anyone lends against it. Bond buyers put a price on SoftBank that its credit rating doesn’t match. Amazon refused to let an agent strip the advertising out of the price of shopping.

The model layer gets the attention. The markets for chips, credit and customers decide who gets paid. Watch the layer that sets the price.

Rated BB+, Priced Like B-

SoftBank is selling one of the largest junk bond deals ever, at record yields, to keep funding OpenAI. The market is marking its credit below its rating.

SoftBank is marketing more than $11bn of junk bonds, $10bn in dollars and €1bn in euros, to keep funding commitments nearing $65bn to OpenAI. Price talk: 8.75–8.875% on 3.5-year paper, 9.375–9.5% on 5.5-year, 9.75–9.875% on 7.5-year. Bloomberg says those would be record yields for SoftBank dollar bonds at those tenors.

The number to look at is the gap. S&P rates SoftBank BB+, the top of junk. The short tranche is pricing near the 8.5% average for B- notes, several notches lower. Average BB dollar paper yields about 6.55%; B about 7.5%. The rating is an opinion. The yield is what people pay. They disagree.

The reason is concentration and timing. Bondholders get no upside if OpenAI becomes enormous. They get their coupon back or they don’t. So they price the downside of one large private bet whose value depends on revenue growing fast enough to fund the next training run.

Record yields are not a closed market. Bloomberg’s related coverage puts early interest in a SoftBank junk deal at over $20bn. Investors are lending; they are naming the price. Goldman counts more than $575bn of AI-related debt issued globally in 2026. Masayoshi Son’s case is that AI will be 20% of world output by 2040. If he is right, 9.5% is cheap. If he is a decade early, 9.5% compounds.

Read it next to the lead. Compute futures exist because lenders want to hedge chip collateral. This bond is lending against the same buildout one step removed. Falling rents hit neoclouds first, then chip-backed lenders, then lab valuations, then paper like this.

SoftBank Offers Record Yields on Jumbo Junk Bonds in AI Push
Bloomberg News

Billionaire Masayoshi Son’s SoftBank Group Corp. kicked off what’s set to be one of the largest corporate junk bond sales ever as the Japanese conglomerate offers record yields to entice investors into helping fund its unprecedented investments in artificial intelligence.

One of the world’s biggest investors in AI, SoftBank started marketing the multi-part deal, with price talk underway for the dollar portion, according to a person familiar with the matter who asked not to be identified as the discussions are private. The group aims to raise the equivalent of more than $11 billion in the offering that includes a $10 billion dollar part and a €1 billion ($1.1 billion) euro portion.

The offering is the latest in a flurry of activity by SoftBank in debt markets this year to help fund committments nearing $65 billion to ChatGPT creator OpenAI, as well as more M&A in the sector. The moves have put the Japanese investment firm at the epicenter of debt-fueled bets on artificial intelligence, at a time when the promise of the technology has riveted global markets even as safety concerns about the industry have flared.

SoftBank is offering record yields on the dollar portion at this stage, though those figures could change by the time the deal actually prices. The conglomerate is marketing $1 billion of 3.5-year bonds to yield 8.75% to 8.875%, $4.5 billion of 5.5-year securities at 9.375% to 9.5% and the same amount of 7.5-year securities at 9.75% to 9.875%, the person said. Those would be the highest-ever levels for SoftBank dollar bonds of those maturities, according to data compiled by Bloomberg.

Price talk for the euro portion is still due to come later, people familiar with the matter said.

The group isn’t alone in storming markets for funding, with global AI-related debt issuance already topping $575 billion in 2026, according to a recent report by Goldman Sachs Group Inc. credit strategists.

Son has downplayed concerns about investments in AI infrastructure, and said earlier this year he expects AI-related industries to account for 20% of global output by 2040, equivalent to $46 trillion.

The flood of AI funding in global financial markets, however, has made some bond investors uneasy. Their concern is that the ever-growing pile of the debt threatens to cause a hangover for markets if the technology fails to pay off for the firms that are making the biggest investments.

With price talk for the shortest SoftBank tranche of 3.5 years starting at 8.75% to 8.875%, if the company ended up paying within that range it would be closer to the average market yield of 8.5% on B- rated notes in the US market, several levels below the Japanese company’s own rating of BB+, the highest speculative-grade score from S&P, according to data compiled by Bloomberg.

Broadly, the average yield on dollar bonds rated in the B zone is 7.5% and 6.55% for those rated in the BB area, Bloomberg indexes show.

Aggregator v Aggregator

Amazon has locked Meta’s Muse out of its store. The fight is over $68bn of ads, and Amazon’s real moat is the one agents can’t copy: the vans.

Amazon has blocked Meta’s Muse agent from shopping on Amazon.com. Its stated reasons: Meta never asked, the agent doesn’t identify itself, and it appears to capture customer credentials. Ben Thompson calls it predictable. Amazon has blocked agents for over a year, is suing Perplexity, skipped OpenAI’s Instant Checkout, and last week agreed to serve its own ads inside ChatGPT instead.

His frame is aggregator versus aggregator. The 2010s truce gave Apple devices, Google search, Meta social, Microsoft enterprise and Amazon commerce. Muse is Meta trying to become the interface for the whole internet, shopping included. That walks straight onto Amazon’s demand.

This is actually about ads. Amazon sold $68bn of advertising last year, about double the $34bn it made from e-commerce. Without ads, Thompson says, retail runs at a loss. An agent that buys for you sees no sponsored listing. Each Muse purchase is a sale with no impression.

Four reasons Amazon holds: it is not moved by customer-experience arguments; it must protect ads; agentic carts so far are smaller and convert worse (Walmart left Instant Checkout at one-third of its own conversion rate); and Amazon owns delivery. Everything digital is contestable. Warehouses and vans are not.

The way out is odd but logical. Meta has promised Muse data stays out of its ad systems, and Muse needs to work. Extend the 2023 account-linking deal: Amazon keeps and grows its ad surface, Meta monetizes Muse at once, on Amazon’s data with user permission. The objection is Shopify, which has no ads to protect and owns the long tail. Thompson likes Shopify. He doesn’t think it replaces next-day everything.

Stratechery · Update · Sep 22, 2026
Amazon Blocks Muse, Amazon’s Moat, Aggregator v Aggregator
By Ben Thompson

Good morning,

On Thursday’s episode of Sharp Tech, we discussed Andrew’s article on Sharp Text about the AI doom debate, why China risks falling into a familiar trap with distillation, and Walmart’s Apple Pay capitulation.

One bit of clarity on yesterday’s Article Frontier Overhangs: I heard from a trusted source that if you do incorporate past training (i.e. the training that created models that are being served for revenue) into Anthropic’s financials via depreciation, they are definitely still profitable (setting aside stock-based compensation) and healthily so. That is clarifying, although it doesn’t change the overall takeaway about the need for continued revenue growth to pay for future training.

On to the Update:

Amazon Blocks Muse

From GeekWire:

Amazon says it has cut off Meta’s new Muse personal AI agent from shopping on Amazon.com on behalf of customers, after attempting unsuccessfully to get the Facebook parent company to voluntarily exclude the e-commerce site from the experience. The problem, Amazon says, is that it never agreed to any of it. Meta didn’t tell Amazon that Muse would access its store, the agent doesn’t identify itself when it browses, and it appears to capture and store customer credentials, which the company says could create privacy and security risks.

As of Sunday night, people trying to use Muse to shop on Amazon were seeing the popup, “Continued access by an unauthorized AI agent violates Amazon’s Conditions of Use, to which our customers have agreed.” “We think it’s fairly straightforward that third-party applications that offer to make purchases on behalf of customers from other businesses should operate openly and respect service provider decisions about whether or not to participate,” an Amazon spokesperson said in a statement.

This was a very predictable outcome; Amazon started blocking agents over a year ago, and is suing Perplexity to stop the service from circumventing its robots.txt file. The company also never signed up for OpenAI’s Instant Checkout initiative, which was eventually canceled; instead the companies just last week agreed to a deal in which OpenAI will serve ads from Amazon’s demand-side platform.

Amazon’s Moat

One of the reasons why tech became fairly boring by the time the 2020s rolled around is not only were we firmly in the age of Aggregators, but the biggest Aggregators had by-and-large come to a detente around who owned what. Apple shared devices and App Stores with Google, Google had search (which it shared with Apple), Meta had social, Microsoft had enterprise (including the cloud), and Amazon had e-commerce. The latter two competed for the cloud, but that is a platform competition; Aggregation is about owning demand, i.e. the consumer touchpoints in their respective domains.

It’s interesting in retrospect to see what each company was doing with their advantage in terms of preparing for the future; Apple invested in its own chips, Google in its own cloud service, Meta in the Metaverse, Microsoft in its cloud (and OpenAI), and Amazon in logistics. This tells its own story about the companies’ positions in AI: Apple is focused on its advantage in edge inference, Google is leaning into the cloud, and Microsoft is doing the same while repositioning itself as a neutral harness provider after its relationship with OpenAI fell apart. Meta, meanwhile, had to pivot and spent a lot of money to do so, but the early success of Muse suggests it just might work.

And yet, of all of these companies, you could make the case that Amazon made the most important investments for an AI world, counterintuitive though it may seem. The reality is that everything that is digital is up for grabs; Muse is impressive and getting early traction, but it’s easy enough to envision a world where the entire personal-agent-in-an-app concept feels quaint.

What will endure far longer is anything touching the physical world, and that is exactly where Amazon has spent a tremendous amount of time and money. It’s hard to believe, but it’s only been 10 years since I wrote The Amazon Tax; that is of course a long time in terms of blog years, but it’s a very short amount of time in terms of one of the key predictions in that Article:

It seems increasingly clear that Amazon intends to repeat the model when it comes to logistics: after experimenting with six planes last year the company recently leased 20 more to flesh out its private logistics network; this is on top of registering its China subsidiary as an ocean freight forwarder. No surprise that, as the Wall Street Journal noted:

In a securities filing, Amazon for the first time identified “companies that provide fulfillment and logistics services for themselves or for third parties, whether online or offline” as competition. And it referred to itself as a “transportation service provider.” In both cases, it marked the first time Amazon included such language in its annual report, known as a 10-K.

So how might this play out? Well, start with the fact that Amazon itself would be this logistics network’s first-and-best customer, just as was the case with AWS. This justifies the massive expenditure necessary to build out a logistics network that competes with UPS, Fedex, et al, and most outlets are framing these moves as a way for Amazon to rein in shipping costs and improve reliability, especially around the holidays.

However, I think it is a mistake to think that Amazon will stop there: just as they have with AWS and e-commerce distribution I expect the company to offer its logistics network to third parties, which will increase the returns to scale, and, by extension, deepen Amazon’s eventual moat.

In a securities filing, Amazon for the first time identified “companies that provide fulfillment and logistics services for themselves or for third parties, whether online or offline” as competition. And it referred to itself as a “transportation service provider.” In both cases, it marked the first time Amazon included such language in its annual report, known as a 10-K.

That moat has been built, and this is its biggest test yet; one way to think about Muse is that it is Meta’s play for a new Aggregation service. In this case, however, the company isn’t looking to own a user’s social network, or entertainment more broadly; Muse is seeking to be the user’s interaction layer for the entire Internet, including e-commerce.

Amazon, needless to say, has no desire to give up its position as the primary user interface for e-commerce. That privileged position has all of the advantages that accrue to an Aggregator:

• Amazon gets a huge amount of data about users and what they want.

• Amazon gets the opportunity to recommend things to users, increasing cart size.

• Amazon gains power over suppliers who have no choice but to accede to Amazon’s terms of business in order to reach demand, i.e. Amazon’s users.

And, most critically, Amazon has the surface to advertise to end users. Advertising is the most natural Aggregator business model, and is the key to being a Super-Aggregator; when I defined that term in 2017 the only two companies I mentioned were Google and Facebook, but now there is very clearly a triumvirate. After all, it’s not just that Amazon’s ad business is large; it’s also critical to Amazon’s profitability. Last year Amazon sold $68 billion worth of ads, almost exactly double the $34 billion in profit that Amazon made from its e-commerce business; without ads Amazon’s retail business would run at a loss.

Aggregator v Aggregator

This is all essential context for understanding this dispute, and gives hints as to how it will play out.

First, Amazon is not moved by appeals to the customer experience. The reality of Amazon’s ads is that they are a skim; indeed, while I put Google in this category — most monetized searches have what you are looking for at the top of the search results — Amazon is worse, given the fact that not only search results but also product pages are overwhelmingly filled with ads. In other words, the entire ad experience on Amazon is detrimental to the customer experience; the motivation here is making money, not making customers happy.

Second, Amazon has a business imperative to protect its ad business. Sure, agents are cool and all, but Amazon isn’t in a position to cater to them without carefully considering the impact on its bottom line. That means the company is not going to easily fold on this question.

Third, it’s worth pointing out that all available evidence is that for large retailers, agentic shopping doesn’t just mean no ads to show, but also smaller cart sizes that are less likely to convert. Walmart, Amazon’s most direct competitor in terms of both catalog size and delivery speed (the hour delivery times if you live relatively close to a Walmart store are amazing), jumped in feet first to OpenAI’s Instant Checkout program, only to bail a few months later when Instant Checkout converted at 1/3 the rate of customers on Walmart’s site or app, and with materially smaller cart sizes as well. Now it’s possible that is an implementation issue, but I wouldn’t necessarily expect Walmart to pick up the slack here.

Fourth, Amazon still has a trump card: at the end of the day your purchase needs to make it to your house, and ideally make it to your house the same day or the next day, or at worst in two days. Amazon can credibly make the promise that they will do just that thanks to the last decade of investment; that’s why I find myself regularly ignoring ChatGPT or Claude’s e-commerce links, and instead simply searching for the item in question on Amazon. Yeah, having to be a copy-and-paste bot is annoying, but what is really annoying is not getting my package in a prompt manner.

Now it is true that Shopify is leaning in. However, read this tweet from CEO Tobi Lütke carefully:

Shopify does not have an ad business to protect; what they do care about is owning the transaction, which is why this integration is about preserving Shop Pay’s position. And, while Shopify does own the long tail — I’ve been consistently optimistic about Shopify’s prospects in the AI era — it’s not an Amazon or Walmart replacement.

All of that noted, Meta does care very much about the user experience, and if a user can’t shop for anything and everything, the experience is worse, so there is a high level of motivation to figure this out. Moreover, Meta might be willing to give more than you might think at this specific moment in time. First, Muse is the company’s highest priority, particularly since it already has the makings of a hit. Second, I didn’t highlight it at launch, but Meta is taking a pretty restrained approach to the data you put into Muse. From the announcement post:

Muse doesn’t share a person’s conversations or the data in their VM with Meta’s ad systems.

That Meta made this promise speaks to how important Muse usage is to the company: Muse data is obviously going to be a goldmine, and because of that Meta potentially using it is a reason for end users to be wary — thus the promise not to. What is interesting is that very few people seem to have picked up on this promise — I’m not sure how much slower Muse’s takeoff would have been without it — but the fact of the matter is that it exists.

To that end, is there room to extend the deal that Meta already has with Amazon? Back in 2023 Meta and Amazon agreed to allow users to link their Meta and Amazon accounts and make purchases directly from Amazon ads in Facebook and Instagram; I actually don’t really remember hearing much about this deal since then — Meta moved away from in-app checkout last year, which may or may not be related — but it’s interesting to consider how the general outline might apply here.

Specifically, is it possible that the first way that Meta monetizes Muse is not through its own data, but through Amazon’s? You can see how that solves a bunch of problems. From Amazon’s perspective, they get to expand their ad surface, instead of losing it; from Meta’s perspective, they not only get to deliver on the agentic shopping experience, but they can also monetize Muse immediately with explicit user permission (because they linked their Amazon account). Meta making money via Amazon ads would be a weird outcome, but it makes sense — and it’s a reminder that Amazon’s investments in the real world are the ultimate advantage in AI.
