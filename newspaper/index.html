The Capacity Round

Instinct is discussing a $1 billion raise at a $10 billion valuation, less than a month after a $2.25 billion round. More than 100,000 users have turned compute from an engineering input into the company’s binding constraint.

The most important sentence in The Information’s report is not the valuation. It is the error message. Instinct has sometimes told users that it is running at full capacity and responses may be slower. The invitation-only personal agent now has more than 100,000 users, according to a person familiar with the company, and is discussing a $1 billion financing at about $10 billion. Sequoia and Benchmark have been in talks to lead.

The step-up is abrupt. Less than a month ago, the company, legally Spear Street Technology, raised $250 million at a $2.25 billion pre-money valuation. It has raised $350 million in total. A new round at $10 billion would not merely price user growth; it would capitalize a queue. The product accepts open-ended tasks by text, then spends inference, browser time, tool calls and human-quality verification to finish them. Every successful user can create more work than a chat window suggests.

That makes capacity different from ordinary software scale. A social app pays once to serve another feed view. An agent may plan, search, wait for external systems, retry and maintain context across hours. Cost is shaped by task length, model mix, tool latency and the number of concurrent runs, not only daily active users. When demand spikes, the failure mode is not a slower page load but delayed promises. Reliability therefore becomes the product and the capital requirement.

The funding question is what converts cash into service. More raw tokens help only if scheduling, model routing, caching, browser concurrency and task admission improve with them. The durable moat is not subsidized inference. It is the operating system that assigns expensive reasoning only where it changes the outcome, keeps cheap work cheap, and tells the truth when a task cannot land. A $10 billion mark assumes that system is learnable faster than Meta can give away Muse.

AI Agent Startup Instinct in Talks for $10 Billion Valuation
By Valida Pau

The startup behind personal AI assistant Instinct is in talks to raise $1 billion at a valuation of about $10 billion, a person with knowledge of the deal said. Sequoia Capital and Benchmark have been in talks to lead the financing, the person added.

The Information reported earlier this month that the startup is looking to raise new funding as the invitation-only agent faces constraints in compute capacity. As Instinct’s service has become more widely available, the number of users has grown to more than 100,000, according to the person.

Editorial

Editorial · The Cost of the Interface

Each cover tonight is a negotiation over who owns the interface. Instinct owns the conversation but pays for the compute behind it. OpenAI owns the answer while Amazon insists on owning the commercial link. Hong Kong owns the land while banks decide whether it becomes infrastructure. Crypto wrote much of its rulebook but could not separate the rulebook from the president’s balance sheet.

Interfaces look light. They are where capital concentrates. A personal agent’s text box hides a variable-length production process. An ad below an answer hides a contest over ranking and checkout. A data-center parcel hides power, cables, collateral and tenant risk. A statutory definition hides who may profit while enforcing it.

The mistake is to value the visible layer and treat the dependencies as plumbing. Tonight’s numbers say the opposite. Instinct’s proposed $10 billion value is constrained by compute admission. OpenAI’s ad value depends on keeping trust between answer and placement. Range’s strategic site is worth little until lenders believe the repayment waterfall. Crypto’s lobbying machine is stranded without a legitimacy firewall.

The best businesses do not merely own an interface. They absorb enough of its dependency risk that users can stop thinking about the machinery. That is expensive. It is also what the valuation is for.

The Answer Is an Ad Below the Answer

OpenAI has built a $1 billion annualized ad business in roughly 200 days. Amazon’s response is to sell its advertisers the right to become the commerce link beneath ChatGPT, while keeping the retailer’s data and checkout on its own side.

OpenAI’s advertising run rate has reached $1 billion after roughly 200 days. ChatGPT ads now run in more than 40 countries across the free tier and Go, reaching into a base of one billion weekly active users. The easy interpretation is that scale finally found a business model. Ben Thompson’s better point is that conversational intent changes the unit being sold.

Search advertising infers intent from a short query and a history. A chatbot receives the problem in full sentences, asks follow-ups and may know constraints. That makes placement potentially more relevant and more dangerous. The answer and the commercial unit sit in the same conversational frame. If ranking bends toward payment, trust collapses; if paid placement is clearly separated below the answer, high-intent demand can be monetized without turning the model’s words into copy.

Amazon’s deal is unusually thin. Amazon manages campaigns and targeting for selected brands; OpenAI controls delivery; text or image ads appear beneath responses. Accounts are not linked. Amazon, which blocks most chatbots from crawling its store and rejected affiliate-style access, has flipped the negotiation. It sells advertisers the right to be the Amazon link inside ChatGPT, preserving the ad auction and the checkout while conceding that the conversation is a new discovery surface.

Thompson contrasts that leverage with Walmart’s surrender to Apple Pay. Walmart spent a decade trying to redirect existing card demand into Walmart Pay. Customers resisted, because the card habit already worked. ChatGPT offers Amazon incremental demand, but consumers still expect Amazon fulfillment. Existing habit gave Amazon bargaining power; existing habit defeated Walmart. Distribution wins when it follows demand and loses when it asks demand to move for the platform’s convenience.

The India Angle

India is in the first wave of ChatGPT ad expansion. The open question is whether local commerce links are sold through global platforms or connected to India’s own rails. UPI solves payment acceptance, not product discovery, ranking or fulfillment. Those layers now become the bargaining table.

OpenAI Ads, Amazon Ads in ChatGPT, Walmart to Accept Apple Pay
By Ben Thompson

Good morning,

Jon at Asianometry has a new video up recounting his most recent trip to Silicon Valley and the current vibes around AI.

On to the Update:

OpenAI Ads

From CNBC in late August:

OpenAI on Monday announced that its advertising business has hit $1 billion in annualized revenue run rate, a milestone that the company is touting as proof of its “diversified business model.” The artificial intelligence company is gearing up for what is expected to be a massive initial public offering, and is under pressure to justify its $852 billion valuation to investors. OpenAI said Monday that its ad business, which is roughly 200 days old, builds on its existing revenue engines like its enterprise offerings, consumer subscriptions and usage-based application programming interfaces.

OpenAI began testing ads within its ChatGPT chatbot in the U.S. in February, a decision that was both highly anticipated and controversial. Digital advertising has long been the cash cow for other big tech companies like Google and Meta, but OpenAI’s move was ridiculed by its chief rival, Anthropic, which made OpenAI’s ad push the focus of its first Super Bowl campaign.

ChatGPT Ads are now available in more than 40 countries, and OpenAI said it’s rolling out self-service access across India, Europe, the Middle East and North Africa on Monday. Ads within the chatbot appear for OpenAI’s Go subscribers and its free tier, which makes up the vast majority of its 1 billion weekly active users.

I have to admit, back when ChatGPT finally launched ads earlier this year, I was pretty nervous, particularly given the company’s well-publicized pivot to the enterprise. Of course I was extremely vocal about how the company should have launched ads years ago, and I was worried that the launch was too late, potentially making me look bad!

It turns out, however, that a billion users is a pretty good basis for an ad business, and I was pleased to see this news. Ads work, and are good for everyone: consumers get increasingly better products for free, platforms can increase revenue without raising prices and fighting price elasticity, and advertisers get a chance to have their product placed by a platform better suited than anything ever to understand user intent.

Amazon Ads in ChatGPT

Again from CNBC last week:

Amazon on Thursday announced it’s partnering with OpenAI to let its advertisers run ads in ChatGPT, starting with select brands in the U.S. It marks a vote of confidence from the world’s largest online retailer in OpenAI’s burgeoning ad business, which recently reached a $1 billion annualized revenue run rate…

Amazon has heavily restricted access to its sprawling webstore from artificial intelligence platforms like ChatGPT, Anthropic’s Claude and Google’s Gemini, while investing in its own AI shopping tools. But in recent months, Amazon began quietly buying ads on ChatGPT, and it became the top retail company advertising on the platform between April and August, according to data from market intelligence firm Sensor Tower. By letting brands that advertise on Amazon run ads in ChatGPT, the Andy Jassy-led giant is acknowledging that the platform has become an important marketing channel. OpenAI said in February that ChatGPT reached 900 million weekly active users…

Ad campaigns are set up and managed by Amazon, while OpenAI’s ads system controls all ad delivery decisions, an Amazon spokesperson said. Ad units will appear as text or images beneath ChatGPT responses, they added.

This is an announcement that was probably inevitable after Amazon’s investment in OpenAI, and which CEO Andy Jassy telegraphed on the Q1 earnings call. This is a very smart approach by Amazon that solves a lot of the problems they had with chatbots, including the fact that chatbot-mediated shopping cut out Amazon’s ad opportunity; the answer is to make the Amazon links only available inside of chatbot ads!

What is funny is that I as a paying user would actually like to see these ads. I find it endlessly annoying that when I am searching for product recommendations on ChatGPT there is almost never a link to Amazon (which blocks most chatbots via robots.txt); if I ask for an Amazon link I’m given a link with a search parameter, so I usually have to do the search myself (and wade through ads). If the link I need happens to be an ad on the bottom of the results that’s fine with me!

It is interesting that this partnership is different than the one Amazon has with Meta, where you can link your Meta account to your Amazon account to get ads specifically targeted to you. This is a much thinner connection: Amazon manages the campaigns and targeting, while ChatGPT figures out where to actually place the ads. It works, though, because ChatGPT knows with very high specificity exactly what you are searching for.

In other words, Amazon rejected OpenAI’s attempt at affiliate marketing, and flipped things on its head: Amazon is basically selling to its advertisers the right to be the Amazon link in ChatGPT results. It’s very smart, and absolutely enabled by Amazon’s logistics advantage in the real world: the reason why I want to buy on Amazon is because I have high confidence it will be delivered fast.

Walmart to Accept Apple Pay

From CNET:

Walmart and its subsidiary Sam’s Club are finally launching tap-to-pay options, allowing customers to make convenient, contactless payments at checkout. For years, shoppers at Walmart and Sam’s Club had to insert or swipe a physical credit card or pay in cash, rather than simply tap their card or mobile device. The retailers’ announcement on Friday said that the launch of Tap to Pay for customers and members “is part of a broader effort to make managing and using their money easier.”

Tap to Pay will roll out on Monday, Aug. 24, in select Walmart and Sam’s Club stores and will eventually be available at all US-based locations by the end of the year, according to the retail giant. Gas stations at some large Walmart Supercenters and most Sam’s Club warehouse locations will offer the payment option by mid-2027. You’ll also be able to add eligible Sam’s Club, OnePay and Walmart cards to your digital wallet, Walmart said in Friday’s announcement.

CNET forgot to mention CurrentC, which, if you will recall, was the payment mechanism that Walmart was trying to drive adoption of; CurrentC was the product of a consortium which closed down in 2016, but Walmart soldiered on with Walmart Pay, driven by the same desire to drive a payment method that defaulted to lower-fee bank and debit card transactions.

It’s honestly impressive that Walmart held out this long in their quixotic attempt to circumvent credit cards and the solidification of their place in the ecosystem that Apple Pay represented. Ultimately, however, even the biggest bricks-and-mortar retailer in the world must bow to demand: customers didn’t want to use CurrentC/Walmart Pay, and they did want to use Apple Pay, Walmart finally threw in the towel.

The primary reason I wanted to bring up this August news item is that it is the ultimate resolution to my decade-ago hand-wringing about Apple Pay adoption; Apple was right to not incentivize merchants in any way to adopt Apple Pay and let the market come to them, and collect that 0.15% fee on credit card transactions forever.

The other reason, however, is to draw a contrast to Amazon dictating terms to ChatGPT. The big challenge Walmart had is that they already accepted credit cards; blocking Apple Pay was an ill-fated attempt to leverage the smartphone transition to get off of credit cards, which customers like to use. They fought demand, and lost. ChatGPT, on the other hand, represented potential new demand, but new demand is easier to resist than existing demand; moreover, that demand’s existing habit was simply to search on Amazon.com anyways, and it’s easier to preserve the status quo than to force changes in consumer behavior like Walmart was trying to do.

One Bid, Twenty Billion Dollars of Debt

Hong Kong offered a strategic data-center site and received one bid. Range Intelligent pledged HK$24 billion, then asked banks for HK$20 billion without offering collateral on the first tranche. The city’s AI hub is now a credit-underwriting problem.

Hong Kong wants live data-center capacity to roughly double to more than 1,100 megawatts by 2030. Structure Research estimates at least $7 billion of investment. The government’s Sandy Ridge auction was meant to anchor that expansion. Local developers stayed away. International operators stayed away. Range Intelligent, a mainland company little known offshore, submitted the only bid with a HK$24 billion investment pledge.

Now the capital stack has to exist. Range has asked banks for around HK$20 billion, or $2.6 billion, one of the city’s largest loans this year. The proposed structure is HK$2 billion for phase one and HK$18 billion later. Bankers say the larger tranche may be easier because Range is willing to pledge collateral there; it resisted securing the smaller bridge because it wants to preserve collateral for the main loan.

That inversion exposes the project’s circularity. The first money must make the site credible enough to unlock the large facility, but lenders want security before demand, power delivery and construction are proven. Range says the location is next to substations and cable landing stations, giving it power and network access. Those are necessary assets, not repayment. Banks still need contracted tenants, completion protection and a believable schedule.

Range operates about 750 megawatts in mainland China, more than all current Hong Kong data centers combined, and its founder’s fortune passed $10 billion. Yet onshore scale does not automatically transfer to offshore credit. The project tests whether Chinese bank relationships, strategic policy and collateral can bridge a development whose economics depend on utilization years out. AI infrastructure begins as a power story and ends as project finance.

Hong Kong’s Data Center Ambitions Face $2.6 Billion Funding Test
By Shawna Kwan, Pearl Liu, Kari Soo Lindberg and Aileen Chuang

Hong Kong’s ambitious plan to become a data center hub got off to a slow start.

When the government put a site in the remote Sandy Ridge up for auction late last year, after earmarking it as a high-tech data center zone, the initial response was silence. Local developers had no interest. Big international data center operators stayed away. Eventually, the auction received only a single bid: a HK$24 billion investment pledge from Range Intelligent Computing Technology Group Co., a mainland Chinese firm little known outside of its home market.

Bankers in Hong Kong are now weighing up a financing plan for a company few of them had heard of last year, in a sector that has become a byword for the spending frenzy around artificial intelligence.

Range Intelligent told bankers earlier this year it wanted to raise around HK$20 billion ($2.6 billion) from the loan, according to people familiar with the matter. That would mark one of the biggest loan financings in the city this year, according to Bloomberg-compiled data. But some bankers said they had doubts over how the company will repay the money, or how it will ultimately develop the site.

The financing is a test case for Hong Kong’s ambitions to become a hotspot for data centers, the sprawling physical infrastructure underpinning the AI services being offered by hyperscalers such as Meta Platforms Inc. and Alibaba Group Holding Ltd. Hyperscalers are technology firms that operate at huge size in cloud computing, storage and network services, among other things.

Zhou Chaonan, Range Intelligent’s founderPhotographer: Kobe Li/Nexpher Images/Sipa USA/AP Photo

Range Intelligent is a key player in its domestic market, reaping the benefits of swelling demand by TikTok’s parent ByteDance Ltd. and giving its founder Zhou Chaonan a fortune of more than $10 billion earlier this year, according to Bloomberg calculations. But the firm is only just starting to dip its toes into overseas markets.

Hong Kong’s live data center capacity is expected to roughly double to more than 1,100 megawatts by 2030, requiring at least $7 billion of investment in data centers and related infrastructure, according to estimates from Structure Research.

But North American investors are pulling back, preferring to back data centers housed by big US hyperscalers like Alphabet Inc. and Microsoft Corp., said Brian Groen, principal at BGDC Consulting Ltd. The market increasingly relies on demand from mainland China, particularly from the country’s own hyperscalers: Alibaba, Tencent Holdings Ltd. and ByteDance.

“Hong Kong is positioning itself to be a super hub for Chinese demand,” said Jason Zhou, a senior analyst at Structure Research. The proximity to Shenzhen for the upcoming projects including Sandy Ridge means the new builds can target Chinese hyperscalers, said Zhou.

The shifting demand has put the spotlight on data center builders that are both willing and able to serve this wall of Chinese demand.

Range Intelligent didn’t respond to requests for comment. A representative for Hong Kong’s Innovation, Technology and Industry Bureau said it was closely watching the progress of the Sandy Ridge project, but declined to comment on the financing arrangement or development plan.

Loan Negotiations

In July, a group of executives from Range Intelligent landed in Hong Kong for meetings with bankers, said a person familiar with the matter. Leading the group was vice general manager Zhang Xian, daughter-in-law of the company’s founder, Zhou Chaonan, the person added.

It was months after the firm had first reached out to banks to get commitments for the loan, and the two sides remained locked in negotiations. Range Intelligent largely focused on the offshore arms of mainland Chinese banks, leveraging the relationships it had built onshore.

The groundbreaking ceremony of the Sandy Ridge data center facility in Hong Kong in March 2026.Photographer: Lui Siu Wai/Xinhua/Getty Images

Range Intelligent had talks with some bankers about first raising HK$2 billion from a “phase one” loan before borrowing the remaining HK$18 billion later this year, said a person familiar with the matter.

The bigger portion ironically looked like an easier sell to banks, since Range Intelligent made clear it would be willing to provide collateral to boost the appeal of that loan. But in its attempt to save the collateral for the bigger deal, Range Intelligent executives rebuffed attempts to convince them to offer security for the HK$2 billion clip, the person said.

Some bankers said they were concerned about Range Intelligent’s ability to pay back the loan, as well as its wider plan for the project.

Still, several banks have pushed ahead and sent loan proposals to the borrower, in part because Range Intelligent already has a relationship with their banks inside China, said people familiar with the matter.

The company previously told some banks it wanted to sew up the smaller tranche by the end of September, allowing it to launch the bigger HK$18 billion loan before the end of the year, one of the bankers said. Bankers are now waiting for the loan to move to the next stage, he added.

The Northern Metropolis includes projects such as the Hong Kong-Shenzhen Innovation and Technology Park.Photographer: Lam Yik/Bloomberg

Range Intelligent, which has a market value of almost $15 billion after a wild stock market rally, is responsible for around 750 megawatts of computing capacity in mainland China — bigger than the current capacity of all Hong Kong data centers combined.

But the firm has only recently started to expand overseas, with a project in Indonesia’s Batam Island being developed alongside the Hong Kong site.

The company’s established position in China has given it access to cheap funding. Its three-year cost of funding is as low as 1.7%, it recently told investors. In a bond offering document, the company revealed lending relationships with more than a dozen commercial banks, including branches of Bank of China Ltd., China Construction Bank Corp. and Shanghai Pudong Development Bank Co.

Government Plans

The Sandy Ridge data center site is part of the Hong Kong’s plan for the Northern Metropolis, a 300 square-kilometer project. The government hopes the data center project will generate economic output of about HK$4.6 billion in the first three years.

The University of Hong Kong is building a new campus in the area, while railway operator MTR Corp. is planning to start digging through a new underground network to reach the sparsely populated area. The Hong Kong government has also made a clear show of support: The city has dipped into its currency defense fund for the first time in decades, triggering concerns over a dangerous precedent, to help fund development in the area.

These signs of support may help push Range Intelligent’s deal over the finish line.

Sandy Ridge is a key project for the government, which has been pushing artificial intelligence in the city, said Samuel Lai, head of industrial and logistics at CBRE Group Inc. “Whether it is for training AI models or for AI computing power, it actually holds a very important strategic significance,” he said.

Crypto’s Hundred-Million-Dollar Failure

The industry spent more than $100 million on politics and helped shape the Clarity Act. The Senate blocked it 50 to 49 because no Democrat would separate market structure from the president’s $1.4 billion crypto business.

The Clarity Act was supposed to convert political spending into market structure. Crypto companies spent more than $100 million, shaped the bill with Congress and the White House, and passed it through the House. On Tuesday the Senate voted 50 to 49 to block consideration. Sixty votes were required. No Democrat supported it; Susan Collins and Josh Hawley also voted no.

The bill’s substantive goal was business-friendly clarity over which digital assets and activities belong under which regulator. The political blocker was conflict of interest. President Trump generated $1.4 billion from a network of crypto businesses last year, according to the Times. Democrats demanded language preventing presidents and other officials from using crypto to make money. Republicans would not accept it.

This is not simply another delay. The midterms close the legislative window, and a 50-vote coalition is far from sixty. Industry lobbying solved the familiar coordination problem between firms and lawmakers but could not solve the legitimacy problem created by the executive branch participating in the asset class it regulates. Money bought agenda access. It could not buy a clean principal.

The lesson for crypto is that regulatory clarity and ethics rules have become one package. The industry can keep treating conflict restrictions as hostile amendments and lose the whole bill, or accept that durable market structure needs a firewall strong enough to survive a change in party. A rule written around one administration is not clarity. It is counterparty risk.

Senate Votes to Block Crypto Bill in Major Blow to the Industry
By David Yaffe-Bellany

A sweeping bill that would create business-friendly rules for cryptocurrencies stalled in the Senate on Tuesday, a major blow to the crypto industry’s plans in Washington.

The Senate voted 50 to 49 to block consideration of the Clarity Act, a high-profile piece of legislation that was shaped by crypto executives over months of negotiations with Congress and the White House. The bill passed in the House last year, but it has faced numerous delays in the Senate.

The vote on Tuesday was a stinging defeat for the industry that makes it unlikely the legislation will pass anytime soon — if ever. With the midterm elections coming up in November, the window to advance it is closing rapidly, despite more than $100 million in political spending by crypto companies.

While many Republicans embraced the bill, Democrats largely opposed it. Many of their concerns centered on President Trump, who generated $1.4 billion from a network of crypto businesses last year. Democrats wanted stronger language to prevent the president and other public officials from using crypto to make money.

Ultimately, no Democrat voted for the bill, leaving Republicans well short of the 60-vote threshold required to proceed. Several Republicans, including Senator Susan Collins of Maine and Senator Josh Hawley of Missouri, also cast “no” votes.

“All President Trump wants is for the Senate to give him time to crime, and I won’t support any piece of legislation that enables him,” Senator Ruben Gallego, Democrat of Arizona, said in a statement after the vote. “This legislation failed squarely because Republicans refuse to say no to the president.”
