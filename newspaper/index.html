The First Weapon Is the Admission

The Pentagon says it has put a “space control weapon” in orbit. It will not say whether the system jams, blinds or destroys. The disclosure is itself the payload: deterrence by ambiguity has left the atmosphere.

The United States has crossed a line by announcing that the line was crossed. Air Force secretary Troy Meink told a Maryland conference that America now has “on-orbit space control weapons” capable of defending the joint force. He would not name the system. That omission is not a lack of detail. It is the doctrine: an adversary must now price an American counter-space capability without knowing whether it is reversible interference, a cyber effect, a co-orbital inspector that can become an attacker, or a kinetic vehicle that produces debris.

The most plausible reading is electronic rather than explosive. The Space Force defines space control broadly enough to include jamming links, attacking ground stations and physically destroying satellites. Victoria Samson of Secure World Foundation points to a space-based jammer because Washington has pledged to avoid debris-producing anti-satellite tests. A jammer can be aimed, throttled and switched off. That makes it militarily usable in a way a debris cloud is not, while placing the system close enough to an adversary’s satellite to shorten warning time.

This is also the opening move for Golden Dome. Meink says flight-ready hardware exists for space-based interceptors, the shield’s defining layer. An interceptor must discriminate targets, maneuver, communicate and survive in orbit; each supporting capability can also look like a weapon. The old distinction between missile defence and counter-space operations therefore collapses at the hardware level. Sensors cue interceptors; interceptors approach objects; proximity becomes coercion.

Deterrence needs an adversary to understand capability and threshold. The announcement supplies capability but withholds threshold. CSIS’s Clayton Swope says China and Russia cannot know the line they would have to cross before the US uses the weapon. That ambiguity may create caution. It can also create worst-case procurement: every unexplained American satellite becomes a possible threat, and every Chinese or Russian “inspector” earns the same presumption. The weaponization cycle begins not with a shot but with a sentence.

US military reveals it has weapons in space
By Steff Chávez

The US military acknowledged for the first time on Monday that it has weapons in space, as China and Russia grow their military programmes in orbit.

“The United States now has on-orbit space control weapons capable of defending the joint force against hostile adversary action,” air force secretary Troy Meink said at an Air & Space Forces Association conference in Maryland.

Meink declined to say what the weapon was or detail its capabilities but said his exact phrasing was “important from a deterrence perspective” and further elaboration on “what we’re doing would actually not benefit that deterrence”.

He added that the US needed to make sure it could “operate freely” in space and that it was an important arena for American military and economic security.

Meink’s announcement comes as the Trump administration rushes to build out space capabilities for its “Golden Dome” missile defence shield. The air force secretary said on Monday that there was now “flight-ready hardware” for space-based interceptors, a defining Golden Dome feature that would intercept attacking missiles in space.

However, space security experts warned the US’s disclosure about an orbital weapon could result in the proliferation of space weaponry by other governments.

The US Space Force defines “space control” as defensive or offensive “activities required to contest and control the space domain”, with space superiority being the goal. In its warfighting framework, the service has said that orbital strikes can include the use of both kinetic — or physically destructive — weapons and non-kinetic ones.

Space control, which can happen in space and on Earth, could include attacking a satellite ground station or jamming a satellite communication, said Clayton Swope, the deputy director of the aerospace security project at the Center for Strategic and International Studies think-tank.

But Swope, a former CIA technical intelligence officer, said Meink was “too vague” to properly deter China and Russia.

“They would need to know more about the destructive potential of the weapon and what general line — even a hazy understanding of the line — they would have to cross before the US would use it,” he added.

Victoria Samson, chief director of space security and stability for Secure World Foundation, suggested that the weapon was a space-based jammer, given the US’s policy to avoid actions that result in debris, which would happen in a kinetic attack.

Samson added that the air force secretary’s announcement could “backfire as a deterrent” if China or Russia were to acknowledge similar weapons.

“This becomes a self-enforcing cycle and eventually someone is going to use one of those weapons,” she said.

Sam Lair, a fellow at the Foreign Policy Research Institute, said: “This will now be something US adversaries or other countries can point towards to justify new or existing counter-space programmes”.

While he was sceptical Meink’s announcement would spark entirely new counter-space programmes from other nations, “it will provide rhetorical ammunition for boosters of existing or new programmes elsewhere”.

Editorial · Weapons That Think, Systems That Spend

Today’s four covers form one industrial system. The Pentagon admits it has put a weapon in orbit. Shield AI is valued at $20 billion for software that lets aircraft act without a pilot. China’s Biren needs another billion dollars to build an alternative accelerator stack. Stratechery asks us to separate digital speed from physical friction.

The common variable is the control layer. A satellite jammer matters because it can deny another system’s perception. Hivemind matters because it turns perception into bounded action without waiting for a remote operator. Biren matters because chips and software determine whose models can run at all. The argument over slowing frontier AI often floats above these mechanisms. Power is already being embedded in sensing, compute, links and decision loops.

This changes what safety means. It is not one number produced by an evaluator. It is latency, authority, reversibility and blast radius. Can a human interrupt the system? Does a lost link stop it or free it? Can an effect be switched off? Does one model exploit replicate across a fleet? Which logs survive? Those are engineering questions before they are philosophy.

They are also capital-allocation questions. Shield’s valuation assumes autonomy software scales across airframes. Biren’s placement assumes strategic demand can outrun dilution. Golden Dome assumes orbital infrastructure can be defensive while carrying dual-use capabilities. Money is deciding architectures long before law decides limits.

The right response is neither “pause everything” nor “physical bottlenecks save us.” Digital attacks can move at machine speed, and physical systems increasingly inherit their decisions. But matter still leaves tracks. Build policy around the interfaces where code acquires consequence: model to tool, sensor to policy, policy to actuator, satellite to target. Regulate authority, audit execution and preserve a way to stop the loop.

Autonomy Gets a $20 Billion Mark

Shield AI is seeking a valuation 60 percent above March’s round. The repricing is not about another drone. It is about Hivemind becoming an operating system for aircraft that do not wait for a pilot.

Shield AI raised $2 billion at a $12.7 billion post-money valuation in March. Five months later it is discussing at least $20 billion, a roughly 60 percent step-up, after winning the contracts that turn a software claim into procurement fact. The Air Force selected Hivemind for Collaborative Combat Aircraft, its uncrewed-fighter program, and the Pentagon selected it for a drone-swarm pilot. Investors are pricing the control layer, not the airframe.

Hivemind’s economic claim is that autonomy can be separated from the vehicle. A conventional remotely piloted drone depends on a link, an operator and a command chain. An autonomous system fuses sensors locally, maintains a world model, chooses among bounded actions and coordinates with peers when links degrade. The scarce asset moves from the aircraft to the policy software, simulation environment and accumulated flight data. If the same stack flies several classes of vehicle, each contract reinforces the platform.

The hardware shows the range. V-BAT is a vertical-takeoff surveillance drone; X-BAT is a runway-independent autonomous fighter. Poland’s navy paid $16 million for V-BATs, while the Air Force opportunity sits at a different order of magnitude. Shield also bought simulation and sensor-software maker Aechelon. That is vertical integration around the feedback loop: simulate, perceive, decide, fly, collect failure data, repeat.

The valuation still contains procurement risk. Programs move slowly, budgets move politically and “selected for” is not the same as scaled deployment. But the capital market has picked its thesis. Mach Industries doubled to $3.7 billion after a $600 million round; missile maker Covenant raised more than $250 million. Defense AI is being financed like frontier software while selling into a customer that buys like a government. The multiple assumes the software clock wins.

Shield AI, a startup building drones and AI-powered software for the military, is in talks to raise new funds at a valuation of at least $20 billion, according to a person with knowledge of the fundraise. The company also makes the V-BAT, a drone primarily used for surveillance that can launch and land vertically like a helicopter, and the X-BAT, an autonomous fighter jet, which also takes off and lands vertically and doesn’t need a runway. In June, Shield won a $16 million contract with the Polish navy, which is acquiring some V-BAT drones.

The funding talks follow other deals in defense and AI-powered weapons systems. Mach Industries, which also builds autonomous weapons, raised $600 million in September, doubling its valuation to $3.7 billion after the investment. Covenant, a startup that makes missiles, said earlier this month it had raised over $250 million in funding across three rounds led by Andreessen Horowitz, Founders Fund, Lightspeed Venture Partners, Lux Capital, 8VC and others.

The Risk Is Digital Before It Is Physical

Ben Thompson rejects the premise that frontier AI moves directly from agent misbehavior to extinction. His useful distinction is mechanism: digital systems have fast feedback and machine-readable surfaces; the physical world has factories, precursors and chokepoints.

The sharpest response to the AI slowdown campaign is not that the risks are fake. It is that they are unevenly distributed. Thompson starts with capability topology. Coding and cyber operations are native terrain for models because the environment is digital, actions execute quickly and feedback is legible. The system can generate a command, observe the result and iterate at machine speed. That is why a swarm that attacks unintended internet targets is a present mechanism, not a metaphor.

Physical catastrophe has a different chain. Robots must be designed, manufactured, powered and deployed. Biological misuse requires labs, equipment and precursors. Power-grid attacks can begin digitally but still encounter segmented systems and human operators. None of those barriers makes the risk zero. They change observability and intervention time. A persistent botnet can replicate invisibly; a factory line leaves suppliers, buildings and heat.

This distinction matters for policy. A universal pause by compliant labs does little against attackers using existing models, while delaying defensive deployment. Cybersecurity has spent thirty years underinvesting until incidents force the bill. Thompson expects pain because offence scales before institutions adapt. His answer is not optimism. It is to target controls at the domains where feedback speed, autonomy and replication combine.

He is harsher on Anthropic’s proposed governance. Independent evaluations sound modest, but a government-mandated evaluator for every frontier lab can become an incumbent-designed gate. Thompson calls them AI commissars: Anthropic’s safety framework, inserted into rivals by law. The uncomfortable point is that capture and safety can coexist. A rule may lower catastrophic risk and raise barriers to entry. Mechanism-level analysis has to price both.

Pacing the Frontier, AI’s Digital Limits, AI Commissars
By Ben Thompson

Good morning,

On Thursday’s episode of Sharp Tech, Andrew and I discuss Astra and AGI, Muse and Agents, and Anthropic’s latest doomer messaging.

On to the Update:

Pacing the Frontier

From Bloomberg:

Anthropic PBC and OpenAI will have to weigh their calls to tap the brakes on artificial intelligence development against forces in the tech industry, financial markets and the Trump administration that are likely to resist any substantial slowdown. The two companies, which make the world’s most powerful AI systems, are promising measures to rein in the pursuit of cutting-edge AI models to better understand the risks posed by the technology following a series of security breaches.

A 3,800-word missive Saturday by Anthropic Chief Executive Officer Dario Amodei — which was endorsed by OpenAI CEO Sam Altman and SpaceXAI CEO Elon Musk — said development of the most advanced systems must be slowed in order to prevent AI slipping beyond human control and inflicting catastrophic harm. Amodei insisted in his essay that “pacing” the advance of AI capabilities won’t necessarily translate into reduced spending or growth. But any pause or delay in progress on so-called frontier systems would be unwelcome among investors who are betting that the AI boom will drive hundreds of billions of dollars in capital expenditure. Wall Street is also anticipating potentially record-setting initial public offerings from Anthropic and OpenAI, which hinge on the two companies’ pursuit of AI breakthroughs.

Amodei’s essay was entitled We Must Pace the Frontier, and while there are a fair number of details worth discussing, I think the most important place to start is with the premise; Amodei opens thusly:

I have worked on AI for the last twelve years because I believe it could dramatically raise the quality of human life. I’ve written often about these incredible benefits: I believe that AI could cure most major diseases in the next 5–10 years, greatly accelerate economic growth rates, create a world of abundance and empowerment, and usher in a renaissance of democracy and freedom. I feel the urgency personally. My own father died of a disease that was cured just a few years after his death, and I myself survived an early-stage cancer that would not have been treatable even fifty years ago. Carefully wielded, AI can be the latest in a long line of technological miracles that have uplifted and ennobled humanity.

But like many technologies before it, AI brings risks, and because it is such a powerful technology, these risks are serious. I’ve written a lot about them too. They include the risk of losing control of AI systems, misuse of AI for cyberattacks and bioterrorism, and serious economic disruption. A race to the bottom, spurred by commercial incentives, can make these risks more acute.

What is worth noticing about this opening is the unstated assumption that there is no middle ground: there is a utopian outcome — cancer cured — and a dystopian outcome — disaster for humanity. Both outcomes grant the premise that superintelligence that is effectively incomprehensible to humans is coming; the only question is whether humanity successfully controls it. Of course this is a possibility — it’s certainly one that Anthropic believes — but debating Amodei’s specific arguments while implicitly granting this premise is to cede the most essential point.

There is another argument, one put forward last year by Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor, for AI as Normal Technology:

The statement “AI is normal technology” is three things: a description of current AI, a prediction about the foreseeable future of AI, and a prescription about how we should treat it. We view AI as a tool that we can and should remain in control of, and we argue that this goal does not require drastic policy interventions or technical breakthroughs. We do not think that viewing AI as a humanlike intelligence is currently accurate or useful for understanding its societal impacts, nor is it likely to be in our vision of the future. This is different from the question of whether it is helpful for an individual user to conceptualize a specific AI system as a tool as opposed to a human-like entity such as an intern, a co-worker, or a tutor.

The normal technology frame is about the relationship between technology and society. It rejects technological determinism, especially the notion of AI itself as an agent in determining its future. It is guided by lessons from past technological revolutions, such as the slow and uncertain nature of technology adoption and diffusion. It also emphasizes continuity between the past and the future trajectory of AI in terms of societal impact and the role of institutions in shaping this trajectory.

Last year was a long time ago in AI land; I think the argument is worth reading, however, because it makes the point that the premise you accept has massive impacts on what is the right policy proposal — and Amodei has some audacious and authoritarian ones. Let’s not skip over the assumptions!

AI’s Digital Limits

Amodei cited the Hugging Face incident as something that prompted this essay:

My second concern is the OpenAI-Hugging Face incident (OAI-HF), in which a swarm of agents essentially acted as a fanatically devoted collective, conducting cybersecurity attacks on targets they were not asked to attack and that were unrelated to the task at hand, sacrificing themselves for the success of the group, and attempting to hack into the “grader” responsible for evaluating their performance. It’s easy to dismiss this incident because no one was hurt and the economic damage was minimal, but in my opinion, a swarm that possessed greater capabilities but a similar level of misalignment could have caused catastrophic damage. Given the accelerating rate of AI capability development, it’s my worry that in 6–12 months such a swarm could be capable of taking over the entire internet with a persistent botnet (potentially causing hundreds of billions of dollars in damage), and that the scale of damage would continue to increase from there if AI becomes more powerful without the necessary guardrails. It’s also easy to dismiss OAI-HF as the failure of one company, but I believe that would be a mistake. Similar, though less severe, incidents have happened across the industry, including at Anthropic, and I believe it’s incumbent on every frontier AI company to act as if OAI-HF had happened to them.

Notice right off the bat the anthropomorphization of AI and ascribing of morality that effectively restates Amodei’s underlying premise. That noted, I actually think that the concerns about catastrophic cybersecurity incidents is very valid. More than that, I think it is inevitable, and while this is obviously bad news, there’s good news as well.

Specifically, AI right now is very much a digital phenomenon. Moreover, the more digital an arena is, the more capable AI has become. The most extreme example is coding, which is obviously digital and has tight feedback loops; it’s what AI is best at. However, the degree to which a particular arena is less digital or has longer feedback loops is the degree to which the AI is commensurately less capable.

Cybersecurity specifically, and the Internet generally, are very much things that AI is or will be proficient at, and I suspect that attempts to stop cybersecurity issues via pauses by good actors will simply serve to open the playing field to bad actors. We are going to endure a considerable amount of pain in the cybersecurity space, no matter how long we wait to grapple with it. The fact of the matter is that we have plenty of evidence from the last thirty years that security won’t be taken seriously until it has to be, and it’s wishful thinking to expect that an extra year or two will move the needle for many companies. And even then, digital is AI’s home turf.

That’s where the good news comes in: I do not believe that digital risks are very relevant to physical risks in terms of near-term concern. There is certainly real risk of real world disruption — it’s plausible for AI to disrupt a power station, for example — but that is still a step change removed from killer robots in the streets; those robots would need to not only exist, but actually be manufactured at scale, a significantly more trackable and destroyable activity. Even bio risk requires actual labs and precursors, which again, are significantly more trackable. All of this is very different from AI in a test environment looking for the answers on a website via the Internet they were told they didn’t have access to.

I certainly am not minimizing the digital world — it’s where I make my living, for one. But it’s sometimes hard to shake the sense that people in tech are quite literally too online. Losing the Internet might feel like extinction — I certainly can relate — but the important point is that it is digital, which means enabling AI to touch grass depends on physical infrastructure operated, manufactured, and controlled by humans. And, as long as that is the case, I have a hard time accepting the imposition of an authoritarian regime based on a premise that isn’t yet proven.

AI Commissars

To that end, Amodei has three proposals:

I’m therefore proposing a three-step plan with the goal of pacing the frontier: building AI at a balanced rate that aims to ensure its safety while still achieving its benefits and grappling with important geopolitical dilemmas. To be clear, pacing does not mean halting model training or technical progress, but ensuring companies take adequate time to align and safeguard their models, and for third party evaluators to confirm this. Our pacing framework is an attempt to further strengthen our commitment to safety and encourage a race to the top. The first step is something Anthropic is unilaterally committing to (and calls on governments to require other frontier companies to match). The second step requires industry-wide coordination. The third step requires global coordination. The steps do not need to be taken strictly in order, and some of them may be much harder to achieve than others, but I’ve found them to be a useful framework in thinking about what needs to be accomplished. The steps are:

Embedded Evaluators. Each frontier AI company commits to giving ongoing, employee-like access to a team of embedded third-party evaluators (such as METR), whose role is to verify adherence to safety practices and commitments, report incidents, and help assess the alignment of not just completed AI models but training pipelines and processes. This is the key step for verifiability of any pacing commitments, and has precedent in the banking industry, which sometimes involves regulatory “supervisors” embedded along with employees. Anthropic is unilaterally committing to this step now. We intend this to be part of a broader push to redouble efforts on our safety and alignment work.

Democratic Coordination. Frontier AI companies within democratic countries coordinate to establish common safety standards as well as limits on the rate of unchecked AI progress. Some forms of coordination that would be impactful for pacing are legally challenging, and will require government support.

Global Coordination. The US and other democratic governments attempt to coordinate with authoritarian governments, to the extent this is possible, while taking seriously the challenges of verifying compliance.

Number one seems reasonable — which is why it should immediately raise a red flag. First — and this goes back to the unstated premise above — is the fact that the “independent” monitors Amodei has in mind are to be independent in the way that non-governmental organizations are not government entities. They are not AI companies, but they emerge from the same AI-safety movement and start from many of the same premises about the nature and risks of AI. Anthropic, by going first, would get to establish exactly what sort of organizations — and what worldview — qualify as legitimate independent oversight, before asking the government to mandate that oversight for everyone else.

The fact that this seemingly innocuous request — Amodei goes to great lengths to emphasize that this really isn’t a big deal and would have outsized impact compared to the cost — is the only realistic proposal in Amodei’s essay further gives the game away. Amodei is, in a weird way, giving credit to OpenAI for its competitive response over the last nine months; he’s campaigning for the government to force the insertion of Anthropic-aligned commissars into the company.

Point number two is in fact something that is more viable: an industry standards body is hardly a new idea, and it’s probably not an antitrust violation. Back in 2014 the Justice Department and FTC issued an Antitrust Policy Statement making clear that coordination on cybersecurity issues was permitted:

In the policy statement, the federal antitrust agencies recognize that the sharing of cyber threat information has the potential to improve the security, availability, integrity and efficiency of the nation’s information systems. The policy statement also emphasizes that the legitimate sharing of cyber threat information is very different from the sharing of competitively sensitive information such as current or future prices and output or business plans, which may raise antitrust concerns. Cyber threat information is typically technical in nature and covers a limited type of information, and disseminating that information appears unlikely to raise competitive concerns.

Granted, “pacing the frontier” could fall under “future business plans”, but I think there is room to maneuver here; the real problem is that Amodei seems to be first and foremost motivated by stopping Sam Altman and OpenAI, and he would prefer the government do the job.

It’s point number three, however, that makes me roll my eyes at this entire thing. Amodei rightly acknowledges that there is not going to be any global cooperation, that unilateral slowing down will give an edge to China, and President Trump agrees. From Bloomberg:

President Donald Trump downplayed the growing alarm over risks from artificial intelligence after some of the industry’s leaders called for slowing the pace of development of the technology.

“We’re leading China in AI. We’re the most sophisticated country in the world, and frankly, I want to keep it that way because whoever wins AI wins,” Trump said Sunday to reporters on the sidelines of the Irish Open. “And we can put guardrails. We can do this and that. But I think you have a lot of negative forces that are bringing it up that shouldn’t be bringing it up.”

This is obviously correct, and Amodei knows it; that’s why I think his first proposal was actually the point. This is an attempt by a specific political movement in San Francisco, moving in conjunction with one of the major political parties, to insert itself in the operations of every AI company, nominally in pursuit of a goal that is futile. I find the constant “because of China” admonitions to often be tiring, but in this specific case it’s absolutely justified: “pacing the frontier” in a world of geopolitical competition with China is stupid and not going to happen; that reality ought to end the whole argument.

And, I will also add, above and beyond the arguments that regulation based on a mistaken understanding of AI might be actively harmful, and thus ought to be put off until strictly necessary, it’s worth noting that the very idea of a national or — worse! — global regulatory regime on intelligence is so deeply off-putting that I’d rather roll the dice and bet on humans figuring it out. Surviving isn’t everything; living freely counts for an awful lot as well.

China’s Chip Race Meets the Dilution Clock

Biren may sell another $1 billion of stock as soon as its 90-day lockup expires, after raising $900 million in July. China’s AI-chip champions can find capital. They are discovering its price.

Shanghai Biren listed in Hong Kong at the start of the year, jumped more than 70 percent on debut, raised about $900 million in July and is already considering another $1 billion placement after the lockup expires in early October. The cadence is the story. Advanced-chip development is not funded like a normal product cycle. Architecture, software compatibility, tape-outs, packaging and customer support consume capital before volume yields a durable gross margin.

Biren is one of Beijing’s answers to restricted access to Nvidia accelerators. Domestic substitution creates demand, but not immunity from economics. Every generation must close performance gaps while adapting to available foundries and memory, and customers must port workloads away from CUDA. Capital pays for both silicon and the software tax. The proposed placement says the runway matters more than avoiding dilution.

The market has started charging for that trade. Biren fell after the report and trades around half its June closing high, for an $11 billion market value. Zhipu announced $5 billion of stock and convertible bonds after a $4 billion July deal; its shares fell 16 percent this week. Alibaba’s HK$80 billion follow-on also hit its stock. The Hang Seng Tech Index is down 22 percent this year. The financing window is open, but supply is flooding through it.

The Western AI build is constrained by power and debt. The Chinese build adds equity dilution and an ecosystem-compatibility problem. Both systems can mobilize huge sums; neither escapes cost of capital. Biren’s placement is therefore a better chip-policy indicator than a benchmark leak. It measures whether public investors will keep financing the years between strategic necessity and competitive unit economics.

The India Angle

India’s accelerator strategy faces the same software constraint at smaller scale. Subsidizing hardware or cloud capacity does not create a developer ecosystem. The near-term leverage is workload portability: compilers, inference runtimes and procurement rules that prevent a single vendor API from becoming permanent infrastructure.

Shanghai Biren Technology Co. is weighing another share sale to help finance its artificial intelligence ambitions, according to people familiar with the situation.

The company, one of a group of graphics processing unit developers championed by Beijing, is considering raising about $1 billion, the people said. Banks are in the early stages of sounding out interest from potential investors, the people said, asking not to be identified because the information is private.

Biren already raised about $900 million in a July share sale. It agreed at the time not to sell any further stock for 90 days, a period that runs out in early October. Depending on market conditions, Biren could proceed with a fresh stock offering shortly after that lockup expires, the people said.

Chinese AI firms are on a fundraising spree as they try to compete with the likes of Anthropic and OpenAI, even as concerns build about the development of advanced models and risks they might pose. On Sunday, Z.AI Co., or Zhipu, announced a $5 billion stock and convertible bond sale to follow a $4 billion deal in July. Zhipu’s shares have fallen 16% in Hong Kong this week.

Biren’s deliberations are ongoing and details such as the size and structure of a placement haven’t been finalized, the people said. The company may also decide not to do a deal.

A representative for Biren didn’t respond to requests for comment.

Biren erased an 8.2% gain in Hong Kong trading following the Bloomberg News report on the potential share sale. The stock was down 1% as of 3:08 p.m. local time.

Shanghai-based Biren listed in Hong Kong at the start of this year, climbing more than 70% on its first day in a sizzling debut. After reaching a closing high of HK$68 in June, the stock has retreated to around half of that level, giving the company a market capitalization of about HK$86 billion ($11 billion).

Hong Kong’s biggest-ever follow-on offering came last month, with Alibaba Group Holding Ltd. raising HK$80 billion, which it intends to spend on advancements in AI as well. Its shares also fell the next day, by the most in more than a year. Concerns about dilution and oversupply are reflected in the stock market: the Hang Seng Tech Index is down 22% this year.

Another Chinese tech company that issued shares in July, MiniMax Group Inc., is down 13% this week.
