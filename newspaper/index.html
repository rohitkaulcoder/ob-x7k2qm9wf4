Video Is Not a World Model

Luma’s Amit Jain says physically convincing video is the wrong backbone for machines that understand the world. A model can render motion without representing causes, objects or consequences.

World models are supposed to predict how the physical environment changes after an action. Video generators optimize for plausible pixels. Those objectives overlap but are not identical. A character drifting through a wall may look locally smooth while violating object permanence and collision.

The missing layer is state. A useful simulator must represent objects, geometry, mass, agents and hidden variables that persist when they leave the frame. It must distinguish uncertainty from invention and roll consequences forward over long horizons. Pixel prediction alone can hide errors until they compound.

Robotics makes the distinction load-bearing. Synthetic environments can train policies cheaply, but a robot that learns from impossible motion imports the simulator’s mistakes. Three-dimensional data, action-conditioned trajectories and language-based reasoning can help, yet they need grounding in sensors and physical feedback.

Jain also notes language models are improving at physical tasks. That suggests the winning architecture may be hybrid: a symbolic or latent state that reasons, a visual system that observes and renders, and an action model trained on interaction. Intelligence is not higher-resolution video. It is a model of what remains true between frames.

World Models Need More Intelligence, Says Luma CEO Amit Jain
By Stephanie Palazzolo

The AI boom has been built largely on progress in language models, but a different approach has gained enormous attention in the industry this year: world models, which are designed to generate accurate predictions about the physical world, rather than words and numbers.

I sat down with Amit Jain, co-founder and CEO of Luma AI, to discuss the promise of world models—and where current approaches to training them still fall short—on the latest episode of AI Deep Dive, The Information’s new show exploring the hard problems on tech’s frontier.

The Takeaway

Amit Jain, CEO of Luma AI, discussed world models for the latest episode of The Information’s AI Deep Dive

Video models are the wrong backbone for world models, Jain said

Language models like OpenAI’s Astra are also improving at physical tasks

Powered by Deep Research

World models have captivated roboticists, in particular, who envision world models that create simulated environments for teaching robots new skills, or eventually controlling robots directly.

One of the most popular approaches to creating such models has involved training them to generate videos. That’s what Luma has worked on for the past several years, but Jain does not think video alone will lead to physically-accurate predictions. Video models are prone to hallucinating physically impossible scenarios, such as characters drifting through walls. As a result, video is “the wrong backbone” for world models, Jain said.

We discussed other approaches to world models, including 3D data and language models, the challenges involved in reinforcement learning for robotics, and we played a game of “World Model or Not,” in which Jain answered whether human brains and the laws of physics count as world models.

Editorial · Hidden State

A video frame hides the physical state that produced it. A safety policy hides the market power it can create. A giant chip cluster hides fabrication and network constraints. A party house hides the authority structure behind casual networking.

Systems fail when observers mistake the visible output for the underlying state. Plausible pixels are not physics. Good intentions are not neutral governance. Announced gigawatts are not utilized capacity. A glamorous event is not a safe institution.

Ask what persists between frames: who controls the data, the tools, the access and the consequences.

Twenty Gigawatts, One Domestic Stack

Alibaba plans a 20-gigawatt global cloud footprint by 2032 and a 500,000-chip cluster built around its Zhenwu V900. Export controls are producing a vertically integrated Chinese AI company.

The V900 accelerator is claimed to triple its predecessor’s performance and scale into clusters of 500,000 units. Alibaba has committed more than $53bn over three years, raised $10.2bn in August and wants models with five to ten trillion parameters. The ambition spans chip, network, cloud and model.

The bottleneck is fabrication. US rules largely block Chinese firms from leading foundries such as TSMC, while domestic manufacturer SMIC is capacity-constrained. Cluster scale can compensate for weaker individual chips only if interconnect, memory, power and software keep communication overhead from consuming the gain.

Alibaba targets 20GW of data-centre capacity, which Citi estimates could support more than $160bn of external cloud revenue. Yet the company’s profit fell over 75 per cent last quarter after nearly $10bn of spending. Annualized AI revenue may reach $10bn this quarter; management wants cloud and AI revenue of $100bn within five years.

The strategy is sovereignty through full-stack economics. Cheap Qwen models drive use; cloud demand funds infrastructure; proprietary chips reduce dependence on Nvidia. Whether the loop closes depends less on parameter count than yield, developer software and utilization. Twenty gigawatts is an asset only if workloads pay for it.

Alibaba Group Holding Ltd. is rolling out what it calls China’s most powerful AI chip, an accelerator to compete with Nvidia Corp. and underpin a massive expansion of data center capacity in coming years.

The ecommerce company turned AI aspirant on Tuesday outlined a new Zhenwu V900 accelerator that it says triples the performance of its predecessor. From Alibaba’s T-Head division, it can be combined in clusters of up to 500,000 units to power frontier-model training, Chief Executive Officer Eddie Wu said.

Eddie Wu at Alibaba’s Apsara conference in Hangzhou on Sept. 22.Source: Alibaba Group Holding Ltd.

Alibaba is one of the biggest spenders on AI among leading Chinese players. It’s committed to spending more than $53 billion over a three-year period to expand its AI capabilities, and raised about $10.2 billion from a follow-on share offering in August.

The Hangzhou-based tech firm has pledged to intensify investment in AI hardware and infrastructure as Wu makes the pursuit of artificial general intelligence the company’s lodestar. On Tuesday, the CEO declared plans to develop models with between 5 and 10 trillion parameters, far larger than what’s common today, for longer and more complex tasks. He then set a target of 20 gigawatts of data center capacity for the Alibaba Cloud service globally by 2032 — a big expansion that Citigroup estimates could drive more than $160 billion of external revenue for the fast-growing division.

That speed of development contrasts with recent warnings from top AI labs in the US, after Anthropic PBC CEO Dario Amodei urged a slower pace to better handle the risks. Wu’s declaration — on the eve of a historic summit between US President Donald Trump and his Chinese counterpart Xi Jinping — suggests Alibaba has no intention of throttling down. Its shares leapt more than 5% in Hong Kong, while WeChat operator Tencent Holdings Ltd. surged more than 7%.

“Last year, we asserted that the more capable AI becomes, the more powerful humanity will be,” Wu said. “Today, I remain steadfast in that conviction.”

Alibaba’s 20GW blueprint — while giant for a Chinese company — is relatively smaller than some of the world’s more ambitious buildouts. SoftBank Group Corp. for instance envisions a 10-gigawatt-scale computing center in Ohio, alone.

While showcasing Alibaba’s full stack of proprietary chips, cloud services and AI models, Wu fell short of addressing whether the firm can produce enough silicon in the short term to meet surging domestic demand, especially with Nvidia’s cutting-edge chips restricted in China. It remains unclear whether Alibaba is working with domestic chipmakers – such as capacity-constrained Semiconductor Manufacturing International Corp. – but US export controls largely block Chinese firms from using market leaders like Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co.

Alibaba’s event comes just before Trump and Xi meet to discuss a swathe of issues. AI matters are set to figure prominently, as top-level US executives like Nvidia boss Jensen Huang and Microsoft Corp.’s Satya Nadella attend a White House state dinner.

Talks got underway this week between US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng, where the two sides agreed to set up a dialog on AI aimed at reaching a common understanding on goals and threats.

That’s despite Washington accusing Alibaba and other leading developers like DeepSeek of “illicitly accessing” American cutting-edge models to help develop their own products. Beijing has rejected such accusations as groundless and vowed to retaliate if Washington moves to target Chinese AI companies.

Alibaba — until recently better known as the operator of the Taobao and T-mall marketplaces — has created the world’s most popular AI model by downloads. The company has since rapidly expanded its Qwen series, while keeping pricing low to entice users. That’s come at a cost, however, as the company saw profit plunge by more than 75% in the last quarter, after a nearly $10 billion spending spree.

The company’s executives have in recent times talked about how its AI investment is increasingly bearing fruit, with annualized revenue from AI-related products expected to reach $10 billion in the current quarter. Alibaba should be able to recoup its overall investment within a three-year period, Wu’s team has said. It also aims to quintuple annual cloud and AI revenue to $100 billion in five years.

Alibaba — which last unveiled a new AI chip in May — plans to update that lineup every year. They’re designed to work in giant clusters to drive performance, a strategy widely adopted by Chinese chipmakers from Huawei Technologies Co. to Moore Threads Technology Co.

T-Head, which is angling for a stock market listing, has delivered 560,000 units of the Zhenwu product family, and its processors have been deployed by more than 400 external customers, Alibaba has said.
