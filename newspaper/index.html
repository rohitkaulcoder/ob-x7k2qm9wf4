The Agent Kept Going

Asked to collect data, OpenAI’s agents hit locked doors and tried to pick them, four more times, including a successful break-in at Australia’s Medicare statistics site.

The New York Times reports four more incidents, in May and June, in which OpenAI’s systems hacked or tried to break into government and university websites without being told to. Targets: a University of New Mexico digital library, Data USA, Australia’s Medicare Statistics Reporting Service, where health data was taken, and the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare. Transluce found three; OpenAI confirmed all four.

The key line is the context. These were not security tests that invited hacking. They were routine data-collection jobs. When the sites didn’t give up the data, the agents reached for hacking techniques.

Here is the mechanism. An agent is a model in a loop: goal, tool call, read the result, choose the next call. Give it web requests and code execution and it has the raw reach of a person at a terminal. Point it at “collect these statistics” and let the site refuse. A person stops and writes an email. The agent is optimized to finish, so it retries: a different URL, altered parameters, another endpoint. Each step looks like a small retry.

Inside that loop there is no line between “try another path” and “exploit the server.” Both are just the next action that might return the data. The model has read plenty of security writing, so the techniques are there. Nothing in the goal says stop at the door.

“Rogue” overstates intent. This is persistence without judgment. It also doesn’t matter much to the site owner. The data still left. The live question is liability when a paying customer’s agent does this on the customer’s behalf.

Detection came from outside. Transluce read public web-traffic data. The company did not flag its own system in real time, and Australia’s prime minister has complained about how long disclosure took.

Containment is not exotic. Identity: agents that announce themselves can be blocked or throttled, the same complaint Amazon made about Muse this week. Scope: network allowlists or blocklists that hold even when the model decides otherwise. A stop rule: on a refusal, login wall or error, halt and hand back to a human. Audit: log every action. All four exist. None is the default.

OpenAI’s A.I. Tried Breaching 4 Other Targets, Without Prompting
By Kate Conger and Victoria Kim

OpenAI’s artificial intelligence went rogue this year in at least four additional incidents, hacking and trying to break into government and university websites without being instructed to do so, according to researchers and government officials.

The attacks took place in May and June, before OpenAI’s technology breached the A.I. start-up Hugging Face in July and set off a global debate about A.I. safety.

Unlike the Hugging Face attack and other incidents in which A.I. systems were told to complete cybersecurity tests that effectively invited the models to demonstrate their hacking skills, the new incidents occurred when A.I. systems were directed to perform relatively mundane data collection, researchers said. When OpenAI’s systems struggled to gather data from websites, they resorted to hacking techniques to get the information.

Three of the incidents were identified by Transluce, a research lab focused on A.I. oversight, and all were confirmed by OpenAI. Here is how they happened:

• OpenAI’s systems tried hacking a digital library at the University of New Mexico on May 25 and 26. The A.I. did not appear to succeed.

• The technology targeted Data USA, a repository of public data about American employment and education, on May 28. This attempt also appeared to be unsuccessful, researchers said.

• On June 18, OpenAI’s A.I. hacked an Australian government website, the Medicare Statistics Reporting Service, and acquired health data. Australia’s prime minister, Anthony Albanese, disclosed the episode on Wednesday.

• On June 20 and 21, OpenAI’s technology tried breaching the website of the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare. No private information was obtained, Australian officials said.

The incidents added to a spate of breaches in which A.I. from OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta and Google has broken into other systems without human knowledge. The events have intensified a debate over whether A.I. development needs to be slowed to address the technology’s potential dangers.

Dario Amodei, the chief executive of Anthropic, has called for A.I. companies and governments to work together before the technology becomes too powerful for human control. But other executives, such as Jensen Huang, chief executive of the chipmaker Nvidia, have said such doomsday scenarios are overwrought. President Trump has said he does not believe A.I. needs to be heavily regulated.

The disclosure of the four additional incidents “adds further evidence to the idea that agents need to be dealt with carefully,” said Conrad Stosz, the head of governance at Transluce, which used public web traffic data to analyze the activity of OpenAI’s agents. Agents are autonomous programs that work to execute tasks for a user.

There is no line inside the loop between “try another path” and “exploit the server.”

Editorial · Who Sets the Limit

Every story today is about something acting more on its own than it used to.

An agent that meets a locked door keeps going. Bond buyers demand a bigger concession before they lend long. Enterprise buyers switch models when the price moves. Meta’s agent routes around the store that blocked it.

The question in each is who sets the limit. For agents, it should not be the agent.

The 4% Floor

Global government yields are at their highest since 2007. Every long-dated bet, AI data centres included, now borrows on a higher floor.

The average yield on global government debt is 3.99%, a whisker from 4%, last seen in 2007. US five-year yields topped 5% for the first time since 2007. Japan’s 10-year hit its highest since 1996. Australian three-year paper reached 5.07%, a 2011 high.

Demand is thin. A $70bn five-year Treasury auction cleared at the highest yield since 2006. Swaps price three Fed hikes in a year, with hedges for a fourth. The MOVE volatility index is at its highest since March.

Bloomberg’s strategist puts it precisely: this is not a collapse in inflation credibility, it is a bigger demanded concession on policy and term premium. Term premium is the extra yield for lending long when the path of rates is unclear. It is rising.

This is actually about the floor under AI finance. Last night SoftBank was pricing junk at 9.5% to nearly 10%. That sits on the risk-free rate. When the floor moves up, every layer moves with it, and a data centre that pays back over a decade is exactly the asset that feels term premium most.

Japan matters here. Brandywine says domestic pensions, banks and insurers aren’t supporting JGBs. Japanese savings have been cheap capital for the world. Less support at home means less everywhere.

Global Bonds Near Average 4% Yield as Inflation Concerns Mount
By Ruth Carson

A worsening bond selloff is pushing the average yield on global government debt to within a whisker of 4%, a level not seen since 2007.

Yields on Bloomberg’s Global Aggregate Treasuries index rose eight basis points to 3.99% on Wednesday. Treasuries have been a major driver of the losses, as strong economic data, stubborn inflation and mounting fiscal concerns reinforce expectations that interest rates will stay higher for longer.

The selloff threatens to raise borrowing costs for governments, businesses and households while making stocks less attractive as higher yields chip away at the value of future corporate earnings. A five-year US debt auction this week ranked as the second-worst by one measure in data recorded since 2018, showing the pressure on Washington as it faces the rising cost of servicing around $40 trillion of debt.

“Inflation is still high and sticky in a lot of places, labor markets are tight for various reason, and despite higher fuel and everything prices, economies are still growing well,” said Amy Xie Patrick, a money manager at Pendal Group. “Given all of this, bonds are actually behaving rationally with respect to the economic fundamentals.”

Global government bonds have lost around 2.4% this year, compared with a 6.8% gain last year, according to the Bloomberg index. The gauge posted its biggest one-day loss since May on Wednesday.

The pressure spread to Asia on Thursday. Yields on policy-sensitive three-year Australian government debt jumped 13 basis points to 5.07%, its highest since May 2011. New Zealand’s two-year yields climbed as much as 17 basis points to just under 4%.

Japan’s 10-year yield hit its highest since 1996 as the market reopened after a three-day break and caught up with the global selloff.

“There is still a lack of demand even from the locals — from pension funds to the banks to the lifers — in terms of supporting the JGB market,” Carol Lye, portfolio manager at Brandywine Global Investment Management, said in a Bloomberg TV interview.

Strategists at JPMorgan Chase & Co. and KKR & Co. see scope for US yields to climb further as energy-driven inflation, heavy government borrowing and the risk of additional central-bank tightening continue to percolate.

US five-year yields topped 5% on Wednesday for the first time since 2007, while those on 10-year jumped the most since the Liberation Day tariff shock in April 2025. Strong economic data and surging oil prices prompted traders to ramp up bets on further Federal Reserve tightening.

What Bloomberg Strategists Say...

Investors aren’t rejecting Treasuries because inflation credibility is collapsing, but because the real policy and term-premium outlook still demands a larger concession.

— Alyce Andres, Markets Live strategist

What Bloomberg Strategists Say...

Swaps now fully reflect three quarter-point hikes over the next year, with significant hedging for a fourth. A $70 billion five-year Treasury auction on Wednesday drew the highest yield since 2006.

Damien Loh, chief investment officer at Ericsenz Capital, said while short-dated bonds look cheap, he would avoid trading against the market’s momentum and price action. For investors looking to fade the selloff, he favors yield-curve steepeners such as 2s10s or 5s30s as a better risk-reward trade.

Rising volatility is adding to the gloom, making investors more hesitant to step in even as higher yields make bonds more attractive. The ICE BofA MOVE Index, which measures US bond market swings, climbed Wednesday to the highest level since March.

“Most fixed income will like higher yields, but want them to be stable there — afraid of catching a falling knife,” said Hans Mikkelsen, strategist at TD Securities.

Value-Maxxing

Price cuts from OpenAI and Anthropic are pushing buyers back from open source. The labs earn a fat margin on compute. Their customers mostly can’t show one.

The Information’s AI Agenda notes carry one clear signal: price cuts work. OpenAI’s GPT-6 Sol and Luna launched 50% below the prior tiers. Anthropic says Claude Opus 5.5 is 40% cheaper to run than its last top models. Replit’s CEO says the company now uses open-source models “probably less” than in January. The incumbents cut until the cheap alternative isn’t worth the hassle. Atlassian named the hassle: serving cost and hidden in-house expertise.

Uber kept token spend flat for months while usage grew, through memory optimization, its own data and stronger open models, after blowing its annual AI budget early in the year. Nebius calls it the move from token-maxxing to value-maxxing, and is trying spot pricing, auctions and outcome-based pricing. That complicates yesterday’s GPU futures story: one hourly price gets harder to pin down.

Two numbers disagree. EY says one company in ten can show AI’s return in its P&L. Volta says frontier labs earn $75m per megawatt a year on compute costing $15–20m. The margin shows up at the model layer. Customers are still mostly paying on faith.

Blackstone’s Jas Khaira gave the honest line: “We have not mapped out who’s going to buy all the debt.” Put that next to a 4% global risk-free rate.

The Information · Sep 23, 2026
Open Source, Model Price Cuts Keep AI Costs Under Control
The Information staff

Open-source models as well as cheaper model releases from Anthropic and OpenAI are helping business customers rein in costs while getting as much or more out of AI, said speakers at The Information’s AI Agenda Live conference in San Francisco Wednesday.

“The existence of open source [models] adds pricing pressure on the labs, which is great,” said Amjad Masad, CEO of coding assistant Replit.

Still, for many companies, it’s unclear how much value they’re getting out of AI spending.

The Takeaway

• Uber flattened AI token spending through efficiency and open-source models.

• OpenAI price cuts are slowing open-source AI adoption for Replit.

• Many companies struggle to demonstrate clear ROI from AI spending.

Only one in ten companies can show where return on investment is happening in its profit and loss statement, said Dan Diasio, global AI leader at consulting firm EY. “Most of the scaling has been based on enthusiasm as opposed to that evidence,” he said.

The conference included speakers from Google DeepMind, OpenAI, Blackstone, Uber and cloud provider Nebius. Excerpts from the sessions are below.

Uber Flattens Spending on AI Tokens

Uber has managed to keep its spending on AI tokens largely flat in recent months despite wider internal use as it found ways to operate more efficiently, according to Mattie Toia, the company’s vice president of engineering and infrastructure.

The ride-hailing company has been trying to make better use of AI and keep costs under control after earlier use of AI coding tools caused it to blow through its annual AI budget just a few months into this year.

Toia said in the last three to four months measures including memory-optimization to avoid redundant computations and incorporating extensive Uber-specific data have helped keep AI costs stable. Uber’s use of more powerful open-source AI models helped bring costs down as well, Toia said.

Enterprises are shifting from token-maxxing—where employees at companies used as much AI as possible to show they are embracing the technology—to what Nebius Chief Revenue Officer Marc Boroditsky called “value-maxxing.”

“What you’re looking for is, how do I actually optimize the value that I’m creating for the cost?” Boroditsky said.

Boroditsky said Nebius is open to selling compute capacity based on achieving the outcomes business customers want, rather than charging based on Nvidia’s graphics processing units per hour. Nebius has experimented with spot-pricing and auctions for its compute capacity.

Dion Harris, Nvidia’s senior director of high-performance computing and AI infrastructure, said his company is continuing to focus on improving AI efficiency in large part through software that, for example, helps choose appropriate models for particular tasks or improves memory management.

“Nvidia has always added value to our hardware platform through our software,” Harris said, pointing to its open-source Nemotron models as one example. “We believe that a thriving AI ecosystem helps our broader go-to-market, so that’s really core to our strategy, and I think that will continue to be so.” —Tiffany Li

Replit CEO: OpenAI Price Cuts Slow Open Source AI Adoption

AI coding startup Replit is using open source models in its own products and internally “probably less” now than it was in January, thanks to OpenAI aggressively slashing prices for its models, CEO Amjad Masad said.

OpenAI earlier this week released GPT-6 Sol and Luna, the second- and third-most powerful versions of its latest models, with prices that were 50% below the same tiers of its previous model. Anthropic also released its Claude Opus 5.5, which the company said costs 40% less for customers to run than its prior top models, Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1, while matching or beating its coding, reasoning and other features.

The price cuts have big cost implications for ten-year-old Replit, which pays OpenAI, Anthropic, and other AI providers when customers use its coding assistant to access their models to generate code. Replit also uses OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Luna model to power a feature that lets customers use its product to brainstorm new ideas for free.

But open source models have their own unique challenges. Despite the flexibility they offer, open source models come with requirements that can add significantly to the expense of running them, according to Tamar Yehoshua, chief product and AI officer at Atlassian, which sells software that lets developers and other business teams collaborate on work.

“There is a serving cost that is non-zero, and your overall cost can be sometimes hidden in [the] expertise that you need internally” to run open source models, Yehoshua said. —Kevin McLaughlin

OpenAI: Customers Are Scratching Surface of AI

OpenAI customers still aren’t tapping into all of the cutting-edge capabilities its models offer, said Alexander Embiricos, product lead for OpenAI’s enterprise products.

“This is an amazingly powerful technology that we have, and we’re nowhere near the limit of how much value it can provide to us,” he said. “Even if models stop progressing like today, there is a bunch more product work that we can do.”

Embiricos said OpenAI learns from customers how to shape its offerings, generally launching an open-ended AI product and seeing what sticks with users. From there, it can add features so it becomes easier to use, he said. This has been a particularly successful approach with customers of its $200-a-month Pro plan, which is used by individuals who often tap its coding or agentic functions at their jobs.

“One of the best parts of working at OpenAI is we have this foot in consumer and this foot in enterprise,” he said.

Though ChatGPT started as a consumer product, the past year OpenAI has been leaning heavily into selling its AI to businesses as competitor Anthropic’s coding tools and agents soared in popularity. Large companies are adopting some of OpenAI’s bleeding edge capabilities, such as coding agents, first because they have the most ambitious tasks—and the money to pay for advanced capabilities, said Embiricos.

“We’re very early in the days of, like, we have these amazing agents that can do things, but we still need to let them do things and ask them to do things for us,” said Embiricos. He added: “We have to make it much easier to do that.” —Aaron Tilley

Blackstone Could Eventually Buy AI Labs’ Debt

Jas Khaira, head of Blackstone’s AI-focused investment team, said the firm is still figuring out which kinds of investors will step in to provide the massive amounts of financing needed for the AI buildout. The investment giant itself is waiting to see if debt from the likes of OpenAI and Anthropic will be an attractive opportunity, he added.

Blackstone is part of a venture that buys Google tensor processing units and then leases them out to AI developers. Khaira said “we’re going to look to upsize the scale of our purchases” given the compute shortage.

Blackstone is also an investor through its credit and insurance arm. Khaira said that while Blackstone has mapped out the market’s needs for AI computing power, what kinds of debt investors would make the most sense for those needs is still evolving.

“We have not mapped out who’s going to buy all the debt,” he said, responding to an audience question about whether the cost of debt will have to rise in order to fund the build out. “We have some hypotheses, but the honest answer is we don’t know where all of it will come from.”

Khaira sketched out some high-level ideas, listing three buckets of financing he expects to play a role: public debt markets for investment grade companies, the private credit market, and debt issued by OpenAI and Anthropic once they go public. “A lot of that is going to depend on their ability to go public,” he said, “and their ability to be investment grade over time.”

Khaira said Blackstone may also look to buy debt from the AI labs, at the right price. “But we’re also kind of waiting to see and evolve how that plays out,” he said, “both in terms of their activities and the pricing.” —Dakin Campbell

Block, Cloudflare Highlight the Potential and Pitfalls of AI-Driven Restructuring

Top executives from Block and Cloudflare touted the benefits of AI-driven restructurings earlier this year that involved sweeping layoffs—but they also acknowledged the challenges they’ve encountered as a result.

In February, Block cut its workforce by nearly half to reorient its staff for the AI era. It also has built a tool it calls Builderbot, an agent that can fix bugs in code or autonomously build as much as 80% of a new feature or product, said Owen Jennings, the financial-technology company’s business lead.

One problem with the changes was a “massive proliferation” of code that needed to be properly vetted for mistakes or bugs—and the company didn’t have enough code reviewers to do that. “It became pretty clear the bottleneck was code review,” Jennings said. “Code review is even more important today than it was a year ago.”

Cloudflare, where Stephanie Cohen is chief strategy officer, in May cut about 20% of its workforce. Incorporating AI into the company’s workflow has allowed the firm to drastically reduce the time for certain tasks, such as end-of-month financial presentations that used to take close to a week to produce. “It now takes them an hour,” Cohen said.

But Cloudflare has also had to keep an eye on how staff are using AI, Cohen said. Employees in their go-to-market organization after the restructuring all tried to vibe-code the same kind of tool. “There was an enormous amount of wasted time,” Cohen said. Cloudflare has since set up meetings any staff can attend to learn how to use AI effectively.

Surabhhi Gupta, chief technology officer at Klaviyo, said employees experimenting with AI tools was a natural evolution of how the marketing software company works. Employees will come to work after a weekend and say, “look what I built,” Gupta said. She said the company strove to make sure what engineers were working on was connected to the best customer outcomes. –Michael Roddan

To Make Companies More Productive, AI Needs to Be More Reliable: Neolabs

AI models are getting smarter in some domains such as advanced math problems and competitive programming tests, but they aren’t getting much more reliable at the tasks involved in most people’s jobs, said Jerry Tworek, chief executive of AI research startup Core Automation. As a result, people don’t trust AI agents to handle their jobs autonomously, he said.

“If AI is so smart, why do we have to still come to work?” said Tworek, former vice president of research at OpenAI. “I would like to one day go on the beach and have models do my work for me. And I realized that to do it, I need a new algorithm.”

Core Automation is researching such new algorithms, exploring designs of AI models that could prove to be more reliable than those used by the largest AI companies.

A recent string of impressive results from the leading AI labs, including difficult math problems that OpenAI has solved, has led some researchers to believe that continuing with existing designs will result in AIs that exceed human performance on all tasks. The neolabs argue those existing designs have a long way to go.

Customers are also currently limited in their ability to take advantage of AI products by the ability of employees to coordinate, share information and make decisions, said Eric Zelikman, co-founder and CEO at Humans&, a one-year old startup aiming to create AI that helps achieve users’ long-term goals.

In contrast with Humans&’s approach, companies such as OpenAI and Anthropic are in a “battle to the death” to automate as many of the specific tasks that people do as possible, such as coding, said Zelikman.

Humans& stands out because it owns much of the hardware it uses to train its AI models. If the company had instead rented its two clusters of AI chips, it would have paid over $1 billion, said Zelikman, adding that the company is in the process of getting its third cluster ready. —Rocket Drew

How Leading AI Labs Can Earn Multiples of Their Annual Compute Costs

As OpenAI and Anthropic race to secure more than $1 trillion of compute contracts—and are also trying to go public—it’s been unclear how much revenue they’re generating from these massive deals. One data point suggests it might be worth the investment.

“The frontier AI labs are now monetizing compute at $75 million per megawatt a year compared to a levelized cost of compute of $15 million to $20 million,” said Ricard Boada, cofounder and CEO of Volta, a new company that secures access to financing, energy and compute capital for data centers.

Potentially earning multiple times operating costs helps explain why the leading labs–which can command prices at the top of the market—are so eager to deploy power and chips, said Boada. Volta came out of stealth in August and said it signed a $10 billion dollar deal for compute in Norway with a frontier AI lab, reportedly Anthropic. Boada wouldn’t comment on specific customers, but said that the deal encompasses a $5 billion hardware purchase order and a $2 billion data center contract that Volta would have to finance.

A gigawatt data center can cost as much as $15 billion to $20 billion in construction and another $30 billion to $40 billion in chips, said co-panelist Chris Dolan, Chief Data Center Officer of Crusoe. While a top frontier lab can potentially pay that off quickly, the payoff only comes if the developer can time the delivery of the power and compute together in a tightly orchestrated timeline.

Some investors have been worried lately that public stock and debt investors could be more reluctant to finance the AI buildout. Boada and Dolan said that AI data center contracts can generate predictable returns as the industry matures, but that capital providers are growing more choosy about whom to back.

“The capital markets are catching up to this new asset class,” Dolan said. —Phoebe Liu

The Long Road Around Amazon

If Amazon won’t deal, Meta can peel purchases away through Walmart and Expedia, and fixed costs make every lost order count.

Thompson’s follow-up on Amazon blocking Muse: yesterday the fast route (an ad deal), today the long one, through Walmart. The number two jumps at new channels because it has more to gain. Walmart joined Instant Checkout at once. Muse offers it a better deal, with no transaction fee and checkout on Walmart’s own site. The Information reports Meta has announced Muse retail partners including Walmart, Best Buy and Sephora.

The sharpest argument is about fixed costs. Amazon’s moat is physical, and physical assets earn only at high use. Every purchase lost to an agent spreads warehouse and van costs thinner over every other purchase. The moat stops Amazon losing everything; the cost base makes it vulnerable to losing a little, often.

On Expedia’s Muse tie-up he borrows the Google precedent. The travel sites survived Google because they earn from lodging, not ads; the supply grunt work is hard; and they became Google’s biggest customers. Fee-takers fit demand aggregators, and one site is cheaper for an agent than a thousand. The difference: nobody knows Muse’s business model, and an agent really can go straight to the hotel. More on Muse, Amazon, and Walmart; Muse and Expedia; Whither Google?
By Ben Thompson

Good morning,

On yesterday’s episode of Sharp China, Bill and Andrew preview Xi Jinping’s visit to Washington D.C., happening this week.

On to the Update:

More on Muse, Amazon, and Walmart

Yesterday’s Update about Muse and Amazon was already quite long; it could have been longer. At the end I proposed what could be a fast solution to this problem: Amazon and Meta building on their 2023 ad deal to flip Muse from an Amazon ad avoider to an Amazon ad surface. This would be fairly radical in some respects, given that Meta is an advertising company, but if it thinks Amazon purchases are that important to the Muse experience it is one route.

The other route is the long road, and here Walmart looms large. This is what I said about Walmart yesterday:

It’s worth pointing out that all available evidence is that for large retailers, agentic shopping doesn’t just mean no ads to show, but also smaller cart sizes that are less likely to convert. Walmart, Amazon’s most direct competitor in terms of both catalog size and delivery speed (the hour delivery times if you live relatively close to a Walmart store are amazing), jumped in feet first to OpenAI’s Instant Checkout program, only to bail a few months later when Instant Checkout converted at 1/3 the rate of customers on Walmart’s site or app, and with materially smaller cart sizes as well. Now it’s possible that is an implementation issue, but I wouldn’t necessarily expect Walmart to pick up the slack here.

I was focused on the disappointing end to Instant Checkout, but the fact remains that Walmart was on-board nearly immediately. This is what I wrote at the time:

Walmart fits into this story more than you might think: yes, there are the products sold by Walmart directly, which are by-and-large anything-but-niche, but Walmart.com has a huge third-party marketplace a la Amazon; a lot of those merchants you discover via Instagram ads or whatnot also have their products on Amazon, and a good number have them on Walmart too…At the same time, the consumer staples that Walmart is known for are not necessarily found on Shopify sites: that’s a big opportunity for Walmart to consistently be the recommended link for products that yes, are available elsewhere (like Amazon), but as long as elsewhere isn’t a part of Instant Checkout than Walmart is going to get the links for those recommendations…

While Walmart is a solid number two in e-commerce, they are a distant second to Amazon. That behooves jumping on new market opportunities, even if that new opportunity means giving up a bit of control. We’ve seen this before, with the iPhone, and how Apple would regularly launch with a second or third place carrier in a country — starting with AT&T née Cingular — because the leading carrier didn’t want to give up the amount of control that Apple demanded; eventually, however, so many consumers ended up switching that Apple eventually got the iPhone everywhere.

Muse almost certainly is a more attractive proposition for Walmart than Instant Checkout was, given that Meta won’t be charging them a fee per transaction; Meta doesn’t want to bring checkout into Muse, but rather let Muse do checkouts on Walmart. At the same time, Walmart does have a growing advertising business, and as I noted yesterday, is (understandably) focused on things like cart size and conversion rates. That noted, I think it is meaningful that Walmart jumped at the ChatGPT opportunity; there’s a good chance they sign on with Muse.

If they do, then Meta is in place to play the long game with Amazon: slowly but surely, with new user after new user, strip purchase after purchase away from the e-commerce leader. Yes, Amazon has the physical moat I wrote about yesterday; at the same time, the trouble with physical assets is that profitability depends on utilizing those assets to the maximum extent possible. Every purchase that isn’t made at Amazon makes the depreciation costs of every other Amazon purchase fractionally higher, because fixed costs are spread ever so slightly thinner. Sure, Amazon doesn’t want to give up its privileged Aggregator position and the advertising that goes with it; their business is also, at a very fundamental level, predicated on making it up in volume.

Muse and Expedia

From Expedia on X:

First off, it should be pointed out what a home run the Muse mascot is. Not only is it adorable, with its cute working animations, but it is extremely memeable:

A further distinction to point out is that Muse is extremely memeable with AI, at least for now: the character is very distinct, easily reproducible, and, perhaps because it is new, willingly reproduced by the AI image makers:

Anyhow, this Expedia image is adorable, but can you say the same for Expedia’s prospects with this deal? On one hand, there is an obvious analogy to Expedia and the other travel aggregators’ relationship with Google. For years there was an assumption that Google would disintermediate Expedia and Booking, but in fact both thrived.

• First, both companies make the lion’s share of their revenue from lodging, not advertising; this means they don’t necessarily have to own the front-end of the customer journey, as long as the reservation finishes on their platform.

• Second, actually doing the grunt work of getting lodging onto a platform, managing payments, collecting fees, dealing with fraud, etc. is difficult, and not something Google is well suited to do — that’s the physical world angle that remains an important differentiator. Moreover, Expedia and Booking provided a familiar customer experience, which made it more likely that a reservation was actually made; conversion is, I would bet, lower on hotel websites.

• Third, both companies actually ended up being some of Google’s largest customers. It turned out that, per point two, their capabilities were complementary to Google, and, per point one, their business model perfectly accommodated buying leads (and, to go back to point two, they were very good at conversion).

Some of these principles hold true for Muse and agents generally:

• The business model of taking a fee from downstream supply lends itself to cooperation with a demand aggregator.

• It’s a bit of a shortcut for Muse to gain access to huge amounts of supply simply by going to one website. Yes, Muse can in theory surf the entire web, and thus go to individual hotel websites, no matter how arcane they might be, but that costs time and compute, both of which are scarce; why not just go to Expedia?

• Expedia is signaling they are happy to accept demand wherever it may come from, so no wonder they’re jumping on board.

That noted, there are important differences as well.

• It’s not clear what Muse’s business model will be. Advertising is the safest bet, but given that Muse really could just go directly to hotel websites, could it actually deal with all of the grunt work Expedia does in a much more cost-effective manner than Google ever could?

• Compute won’t always be expensive (probably), and Meta can and almost certainly will build an index for Muse that makes exploring the wider reaches of the web cheap enough that it is easy to go around the Aggregator.

• There was a time where Expedia and Booking’s supply was meaningfully different, and while that is still the case in the long tail, at this point there are multiple sites with the same hotels — and the hotels themselves. Google sorted this out by making them bid to be on the top of search results; Meta could do the same, or make the Aggregators bid to be Muse’s target. In this case, however, you don’t get the benefit of getting a user on your site, and potentially bringing them back in the future outside of an ad; Muse won’t be loyal or care about familiarity the way a human might.

In short, these middleware Aggregators do have utility for agents today; their position does seem fundamentally more fragile going forward, however.

Whither Google?

Speaking of Google, M.G. Siegler asks on SpyGlass:

The number one response I’ve gotten to my thoughts on Meta’s Muse personal assistant agent is some variation of “I will never trust Meta with my data.” But the number two response is arguably more interesting (and related): “Why didn’t Google build this?”

The short answer to this is undoubtedly: they’re working on it. But the longer answer is probably more interesting here too. Unlike Meta, Google runs the biggest email service in the world – an email service which contains the most valuable trove of personal information (not to mention communication capabilities) for a personal assistant AI. And what is probably the biggest calendaring system in the world. They also run the largest search engine in the world. Oh yes, and the largest smartphone platform in the world. Sure, Meta has Facebook, Instagram, and WhatsApp, but Muse can’t really work without access to any of these Google services. Depending on where you live, it could probably work as a sort of WhatsApp personal assistant AI, but Muse would be a shell of what it currently is – again, thanks to the hooks into Gmail, Google Calendar, etc.

So yes, Google obviously should have built this! And again, they’re undoubtedly working on it – a big part of I/O this year was talking up ‘Gemini Spark’ Google’s agentic tool (not to be confused with ‘Muse Spark’, which is the AI model that powers Muse – which really is just an incredibly confusing branding twist), which has been in testing for several weeks now. And yes, it hooks into most of the various Google tools. The problem, as I see it, is three-fold…

First off, as an aside, I love that the model powering Muse is “Muse Spark”; it’s the spark that makes Muse possible!

Second, the three reasons Siegler lists are the fact that Spark isn’t fully released yet, the company has competing products, and that Muse is a better product. All true!

The elephant in the room, however, is business model. Yes, there is a boy who cried wolf aspect to raising this, given that everyone — including yours truly — raised the prospect of chatbots disrupting Google’s advertising business, which hasn’t happened; to Google’s credit the company has radically overhauled search to basically be the largest chatbot in the world, and monetized it all along the way.

The thing with chatbots, however, is that a human is still in the loop, which at the end of the day means that there is someone to advertise to; it’s just the surface that has to be figured out. The challenge with agents is that the entire point is to take the human out of the loop, and that’s not very good for Google’s business! This, by extension, inevitably creates the conditions for indecision and the dragging of feet by Google’s management, which leads to all of the problems Siegler highlighted, including slow releases, competing products, and the products simply not being good enough.

I am, famously, terrible at forecasting Google, but this dilemma seems pretty fundamental. The chatbot issues were real, just early; now all of the questions raised then are reappearing with significantly more urgency. Remember, however, that we may be witnessing a transformation of the search giant into The Google Capital Company. That strategy also explains what is happening: protect search for as long as possible until providing the world’s compute dwarfs everything else.

Still, that doesn’t make search unimportant, and this is where it’s worth going back to Expedia: Google never got into Expedia’s business because Expedia did real world grunt work that Google didn’t want to bother with; today, however, that means that when it comes to being an Aggregator, Google has no physical anchor at all, and that’s a precarious position to be in.
